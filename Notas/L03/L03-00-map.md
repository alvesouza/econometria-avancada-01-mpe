# Classes 3+4 — Introduction to Bayesian learning · Map of the notes

> **One claim.** **Bayesian learning is one rule applied over and over: posterior ∝ likelihood ×
> prior, normalised by the predictive.** It turns Diego's 70% prior into 85% after one positive test
> and 99% after two, and the same arithmetic, with conjugate priors, adds data to pseudo-data (Beta,
> Gamma, Pareto, normal). **A point estimate is a decision**: quadratic loss reports the posterior
> mean, absolute loss the median, 0–1 loss the mode. **A model is judged by its marginal likelihood**,
> which charges for every parameter the data did not need (Greenberg's Table 3.2). **When integrals
> have no closed form, simulate**: Monte Carlo with independent draws, and Gibbs or
> Metropolis–Hastings chains whose invariant distribution is the posterior, at the price of
> autocorrelation. **Priors are also a modelling tool**: hierarchies let units borrow strength, and
> sparsity priors shrink noise while leaving signals alone.

| Layer | What it means here |
|---|---|
| **U** — intuition | forward vs inverse probability; the prior as earlier data; the physicists' joint density; "accept or stay"; borrowing strength; a horseshoe of shrinkage |
| **C** — course (examinable) | Bayes' rule and the predictive; Beta–Bernoulli, Gamma–Poisson/Exponential, Pareto–Uniform, normal–normal; sequential updating; Bayes estimators under four losses, Bayes risk, credible/HPD intervals; marginal likelihood, posterior odds, Bayes factors, Jeffreys' scale, the large-sample penalty, improper priors; Monte Carlo, inverse cdf, composition, accept–reject, importance sampling, SIR; Markov chains, Gibbs (with the switch point), Metropolis–Hastings, mixing and n.s.e.; hierarchical Beta–Binomial and Gaussian models; ridge/lasso as MAP, scale mixtures, normal–gamma, horseshoe |

## The one result to hold

$$\boxed{\;p(\theta\mid y)=\frac{f(y\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{\int f(y\mid\theta)\pi(\theta)\,d\theta}\;}\qquad\text{numerator: learning;}\quad\text{denominator: the predictive, and the evidence for the model.}$$

> **Everything in the unit is this formula.** Its numerator gives the posterior and, through a loss,
> the estimate (L03-01 – L03-04). Its denominator is the predictive of the data and the marginal
> likelihood that ranks models (L03-05). When neither can be computed, simulation evaluates them
> (L03-06 – L03-08). And the prior $\pi$ itself can be a model of how units or coefficients relate
> (L03-09 – L03-10).
