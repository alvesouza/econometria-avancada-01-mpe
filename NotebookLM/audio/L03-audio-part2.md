Explain and discuss in depth (part 2/2) Classes 3+4, Introduction to Bayesian learning: Monte Carlo, MCMC and sparsity. Use the uploaded slides `Aula_class-03-04_aprendizagembayesiana-bayesianingredients.pdf` (the Monte Carlo slides at the end) and the shrinkage pages of `Aula_class-01_multiplelinearregression.pdf` (slides 46–109). Pair them with `Livro_Edward Greenberg - Introduction to Bayesian Econometrics (2007, Cambridge University Press) - libgen.li.pdf` (Ch. 5–7, pp. 63–107) and `Livro_James, Witten, Hastie, Tibshirani, Taylor - An Introduction to Statistical Learning with Applications in Python (2023).pdf` (§6.2, pp. 240–253). This is the computational half; part 1 built priors, posteriors, predictives and Bayes factors. The thread: when the posterior has no closed form, as for physicist C's Student-t prior, we simulate from it. It covers:

Monte Carlo: Use the deck's toy example. Draw M points in the unit square and estimate π by four times the share inside the quarter circle: M = 1000 gives 3.196. Explain the law of large numbers behind it, why the error shrinks like 1/√M whatever the dimension, and how the same idea approximates physicist C's predictive and posterior.

Classical simulation: From Greenberg Ch. 5, explain the inverse-CDF method, the method of composition, and the accept–reject algorithm with the Beta(3, 3) target: why the envelope constant sets the acceptance rate. Add importance sampling and its weights, and when heavy-tailed proposals are needed.

Markov chains: From Greenberg Ch. 6, explain invariant distributions, irreducibility and aperiodicity in plain terms, and why an MCMC algorithm builds a chain whose invariant distribution is the posterior.

Gibbs sampling: Derive Greenberg's Algorithm 7.3 for the normal mean and precision, alternating draws of μ given h and of h given μ from a Gamma. Then present the Poisson switch-point model (eq. 7.3–7.4): Gamma(10, 10) and Gamma(20, 20) priors on the two rates and a uniform prior on the switch point k. Have the hosts connect it to the coal-mining profile likelihood of Class 1: Gibbs integrates over the cut year instead of maximising over it. Explain burn-in, and why highly correlated blocks mix badly (the bivariate normal example).

Metropolis–Hastings: Derive the acceptance probability α(x, y) = min{f(y)q(y, x)/[f(x)q(x, y)], 1} from detailed balance (eq. 7.5–7.6). Contrast random-walk and independence proposals using the Beta(3, 4) target with a U(0, 1) proposal. Cover numerical standard errors, autocorrelation and convergence checks (§7.3), and Chib's marginal likelihood from Gibbs output (§7.1.2), which links back to the Bayes factors of part 1. Mention Hamiltonian Monte Carlo as the engine behind Stan and PyMC.

Hierarchical models: Show how a Beta–Binomial or Gaussian hierarchy (Greenberg §4.6) is fitted by Gibbs sampling, one block per level. Each unit's posterior mean is a precision-weighted compromise between its own data and the group mean: partial pooling, the same shrinkage idea as below.

Sparsity and shrinkage: From the regression deck, derive ridge, (X'X + λI)⁻¹X'y. In the one-observation case ridge gives y/(1+λ), while the lasso gives the soft-threshold S_λ(y) = sign(y)(|y| − λ)₊, which sets small coefficients exactly to zero. Explain cyclic coordinate descent and the elastic net. On the Hitters data, test MSE is 125,154 for OLS, 87,150 for ridge and 77,426 for the lasso. Then give the Bayesian reading. With a scale-mixture-of-normals prior, the MAP estimate is penalised least squares. A Gaussian prior gives ridge and a Laplace prior gives the lasso, which ISLP §6.2.2 also shows. Add the normal–gamma prior and the horseshoe, with its half-Cauchy local scale: a tall spike at zero and heavy tails. In the deck's simulation (n = 100, p = 20, β = (2, 3, 4, 0, …, 0)), the coefficient RMSE is 3.325 for OLS, 2.820 for ridge, 2.307 for Laplace and 2.044 for the horseshoe. Discuss why the horseshoe shrinks noise hard but leaves large signals almost untouched.

Python walkthrough: In words, describe a NumPy Gibbs sampler for the switch-point model, a random-walk Metropolis loop with a tuned step size, and the same models in PyMC with NUTS, checking R-hat, effective sample size and trace plots in ArviZ.

Close with misconceptions: a long chain is not a converged chain, a high acceptance rate is not efficiency, and a lasso zero is a point estimate, not posterior certainty that the coefficient is zero. Point ahead: these samplers return in stochastic volatility (Class 8), hierarchical and probit models (Class 7) and Bayesian VARs (Class 9).
