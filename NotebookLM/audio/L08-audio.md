AR(2) and business cycles: Derive the AR(2) ACF recursion ρℓ = φ1ρℓ−1 + φ2ρℓ−2 with ρ1 = φ1/(1−φ2), and the stationarity triangle. Roots are complex when φ1² + 4φ2 < 0. Compare the deck's cases (1.75, −0.8) and (0.9, 0.05): damped oscillation against smooth decay, and how sample ACFs at n = 200 to 10,000 approach them. For US real GNP growth, Tsay's AR(3) is r_t = 0.0047 + 0.348r_(t−1) + 0.179r_(t−2) − 0.142r_(t−3). Its polynomial factors as (1 + 0.521B)(1 − 0.869B + 0.274B²), and the complex pair gives an average cycle of 2π/cos⁻¹[φ1/(2√−φ2)] = 10.62 quarters. Relate this to expansions of about 12 quarters and contractions of about 3.

Identification and forecasting: The PACF cuts off after lag p for an AR(p) and the ACF after lag q for an MA(q). Compare AIC with BIC, whose log T penalty picks smaller models. Derive the AR forecast recursion, the error variances σ² and (1 + φ1²)σ², mean reversion, and the half-life log 0.5/log|φ1|. Show that an MA(1) forecast reverts to the mean after one step, and contrast conditional with exact likelihood for MA models.

Unit roots and long memory: For a random walk, every forecast equals p_h and the error variance is ℓσ², so shocks are permanent. Contrast a random walk with drift, where the mean is p0 + μt and the variance σ²t, with a trend-stationary series of constant variance. Derive the ADF regression Δy_t = c_t + βy_(t−1) + Σ φᵢΔy_(t−i) + e_t, why β = 0 means a unit root, and why the t-ratio has a nonstandard Dickey–Fuller law. For US log GDP the deck's text reports ADF −1.61 (p = 0.45), but its R output gives −0.37 (p = 0.987): have the hosts explain how the deterministic terms and lag choice change the test. The S&P 500 log level gives −1.998 (p = 0.602), but returns give −37.5. For long memory, (1 − B)^d xt = at with |d| < 0.5 gives ρ1 = d/(1−d) and ρk ∝ k^(2d−1), a polynomial rather than exponential decay.

Seasonal models: Write the airline model (1 − B)(1 − B^s)xt = (1 − θB)(1 − ΘB^s)at and show its ACF is nonzero only at lags 1, s−1, s and s+1. For Johnson & Johnson log earnings, θ = 0.678 and Θ = 0.314, with Ljung–Box Q(12) = 10.0 (p = 0.44). For log AirPassengers, ARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)12 gives MA coefficients of −0.4018 and −0.5569 (R's sign convention, 1 + θB) with AIC −483.4, beating (0,1,1)(1,1,1)12 at −481.91. Explain why the h-step rolling RMSE comparison is the honest test.

Volatility: Start from the stylised facts: returns are nearly uncorrelated, but their squares are not. That is why the Ljung–Box test on squared residuals, and Engle's LM test, detect ARCH effects (Tsay §3.3). Define ARCH(m) and GARCH(1,1), σt² = α0 + α1a²_(t−1) + β1σ²_(t−1), with unconditional variance α0/(1 − α1 − β1) and fourth-moment conditions. Use Tsay Example 3.1 (ARCH(3) for Intel) and Example 3.3 (S&P 500 GARCH(1,1) with α1 = 0.1213 and β1 = 0.8523, persistence 0.97). From the Bayesian GARCH deck on Petrobras, use 2001–2006 (1,506 days) to build priors and 2007–2013 (1,760 days) for inference. The random-walk check gives b = 1.0003. The Student-t GARCH(1,1) gives Pr(α1 + β > 1 | data) = 0.0034. Close with stochastic volatility, where log σt² follows its own AR(1) with a separate shock (Tsay §3.12, §12.7), and why that shock means MCMC rather than a recursion.
