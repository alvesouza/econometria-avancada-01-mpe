Explain and discuss in depth (part 1/2) Classes 3+4, Introduction to Bayesian learning: the ingredients. Use the uploaded slides `Aula_class-03-04_aprendizagembayesiana-bayesianingredients.pdf`, `Aula_class-03-04_question0.pdf` and `Aula_class-03-04_notas-bayesianas-slides.pdf`. Pair them with `Livro_Edward Greenberg - Introduction to Bayesian Econometrics (2007, Cambridge University Press) - libgen.li.pdf` (Ch. 2–4, pp. 7–59, especially §2.2, §3.1–3.2 and §4.3) and `Livro_Bolfarine, Sandoval - Introdução à Inferência Estatística (2010).pdf` (§4.3–4.4, pp. 61–70). The idea: posterior ∝ prior × likelihood, the predictive is the normalising constant, and the same arithmetic drives updating, estimation and model choice. This part is conceptual; part 2 covers the computation. It covers:

Is Diego ill?: Walk through the deck's first example. The prior is P(θ = 1) = 0.70; the test has a false positive rate of 0.40 and a false negative rate of 0.05. The likelihood-ratio argument (0.95/0.40 = 2.375) gives θ̂ = 1. Bayes needs the predictive P(X = 1) = 0.785 and gives P(θ = 1 | X = 1) = 0.847. Stress the common wrong answer, 0.95, which confuses P(X | θ) with P(θ | X). Then add the better test Y (false negative 0.01, false positive 0.04): the posterior becomes 0.9928. Y alone would give 0.983, so test X is worth little unless it is free. Show that updating in sequence equals updating on both results at once.

Three coins: Coins with head probabilities 0.5, 0.9 and 1 are equally likely, and three heads are observed. Compute P(three heads) = 0.618 by total probability and posteriors 0.0674, 0.3932 and 0.5394. Answer the deck's closing question: a fourth head gives about 0.036, 0.382 and 0.582. Explain why the posterior after three heads is the prior for the fourth.

Gaussian measurement error: x | θ ~ N(θ, 40²). Physicist A's prior is N(900, 20²) and B's is N(800, 80²). Derive the predictive x ~ N(θ0, σ² + τ0²) by completing the square. Then derive the posterior mean as a precision-weighted average, with weight on the prior equal to σ²/(σ² + τ0²) and posterior variance equal to that weight times τ0². With x = 850, A moves to N(890, 17.9²) and B to N(840, 35.7²). Note that slide 40 labels B's prior N(800, 40²) although its numbers need 80². Then bring in physicist C, with prior t5(900, 240): the same variance as A, but fat tails. The predictive and the posterior have no closed form, which motivates Monte Carlo.

Conjugacy and Bayes estimators: Derive Beta–Bernoulli updating (Greenberg §2.2). Show that the predictive P(y_(n+1) = 1 | y) equals the posterior mean (α0 + Σy)/(α0 + β0 + n) (Greenberg p. 33). From the Bayes-estimator notes, derive the Exponential–Gamma update Gamma(α0 + n, β0 + Σx). Prove that squared loss gives the posterior mean, absolute loss the median, and 0–1 loss the mode (MAP). Then explain why the Bayes estimator is biased in general yet converges to the MLE n/Σx as the prior becomes vague or n grows (B&S §4.4). Mention the Pareto–uniform pair as the conjugate case where the support depends on θ, linking to the U(0, θ) estimators of Class 1.

Choosing priors: From Greenberg Ch. 4, contrast proper and improper priors (§4.2) and conjugate priors for the normal regression model (§4.3). Explain why conditionally conjugate priors (§4.9) lose the closed form but keep simple full conditionals, which leads straight to Gibbs sampling. Show how exchangeability (§4.5) justifies a hierarchical prior (§4.6) whose hyperparameters pool information across units. Discuss sensitivity and robustness (§4.8).

Model comparison: Define the marginal likelihood, the Bayes factor, and posterior odds = prior odds × Bayes factor (Greenberg §3.2.4). Read Jeffreys' scale in Table 3.1. Use Greenberg's Michaela-and-Lila coins, Table 3.2. With m = 10 tosses each, proportions 0.1 and 0.9 give log10 BF = −2.506 against a common θ, while 0.5 and 0.5 give 0.297. With m = 100 these become −30.775 and 0.756. Derive the large-n approximation in which the log Bayes factor penalises each extra parameter by (log n)/2, which is BIC. Explain model averaging, and why Bayes factors are sensitive to vague priors.

Python walkthrough: In words, describe the closed-form conjugate updates with scipy.stats beta, gamma and norm, and a discrete grid posterior for the coins, saying what each prints.

Close by contrasting this with Class 1, where p = 0.036 sat beside P(H1 | x) = 0.31. Address two misconceptions: that a flat prior is non-informative under every reparameterisation, and that the posterior mode is always the best summary.
