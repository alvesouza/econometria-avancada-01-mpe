Explain and discuss in depth (part 1/2) Class 1 of Econometria Avançada: potential outcomes, the Neyman–Rubin model and selection bias. Use the uploaded sources `Livro_Joshua D. Angrist, Jorn-Steffen Pischke-Mostly Harmless Econometrics_ An Empiricist's Companion (2008).pdf` (MHE, Ch. 1 and Ch. 2, §2.1–2.2, printed pp. 3–12) and `Livro_Wooldridge J.M-Introductory econometrics. A modern approach-Cengage Learning (2016).pdf` (Wooldridge, §1-4 and §7-6). The listeners are master's students who are comfortable with conditional expectations. They want the formal argument for why a randomized trial is the benchmark, with each derivation spoken step by step. It covers:

The four research FAQs: Walk through MHE Ch. 1: the causal relationship of interest, the ideal experiment, the identification strategy, the mode of inference. Use the start-age example to explain why the identity "start age = current age − time in school" makes the question FUQ'd: unidentified in principle, not merely hard. Connect this to the ceteris paribus thought experiment in Wooldridge §1-4.

The hospital puzzle: Present the MHE NHIS table (p. 10). Hospitalized respondents report 2.79 and the rest 2.07 on a 1–5 scale where higher means worse health; the gap is 0.71 with t = 58.9. Have the hosts argue about what that t-statistic measures. It measures precision, not identification: a larger sample makes a biased gap more precise, never less biased.

Potential outcomes and the switching equation: Define y0i and y1i. Derive y_i = y0i + (y1i − y0i)d_i (MHE eq. 2.1.1) by checking both cases. Explain Holland's fundamental problem of causal inference (MHE fn. 2). Then discuss what this notation already assumes, SUTVA: no interference and one version of treatment. Give failures, such as contagion between patients in a ward or the general-equilibrium wage effects of a large training programme, and explain why y1i is then not even well defined.

Estimands: Define ATE, ATT and ATU, and derive ATE = π·ATT + (1 − π)·ATU from iterated expectations.

The decomposition: Derive E[y|d=1] − E[y|d=0] = ATT + selection bias (MHE p. 11) by adding and subtracting E[y0|d=1], reading each line aloud. Stress that E[y0|d=1], the untreated health of the treated, is the one unobservable piece. Then derive the second form, naive gap = ATE + selection bias + (1 − π)(ATT − ATU). Say that MHE does not write this out, and discuss when the heterogeneity term is positive: people sorting on their own gains, Roy-style. Check both forms on a worked eight-person population with ATE = 1.125, ATT = 2, ATU = 0.25, selection bias = −5 and naive gap = −3: −3 = 2 − 5, and −3 = 1.125 − 5 + 0.875.

Signing the bias: Use MHE pp. 12–16. Hospitals and subsidized training programmes give negative bias (the sick and the low-earning select in). Hormone replacement therapy gives positive bias (healthier users). Class size gives negative bias, because weaker students are put in smaller classes. Insist that the sign is an economic argument about who selects into treatment, not a statistical property.

Random assignment: From independence of (y0i, y1i) and d_i, derive that selection bias is zero and ATT = ATE (MHE p. 12), and explain why the heterogeneity term vanishes too. Then sharpen the assumption. Zero selection bias needs only mean independence of y0, E[y0|d] = E[y0]. ATT = ATE needs, in addition, E[y1 − y0|d] = E[y1 − y0]. Construct a case where the first holds and the second fails, so that the naive gap recovers the ATT but not the ATE. Separate unbiasedness over the randomization distribution from being right in one draw. On the eight-person table, all 70 four-of-eight assignments average exactly 1.125, yet single draws range from −3 to 5.25, and 17 of the 70 get the sign wrong. State what independence does not deliver: protection against SUTVA failures, attrition or noncompliance. Preview that conditioning on covariates is Class 2 and noncompliance leads to instrumental variables and LATE in Classes 3–4.

Misconceptions to confront by name: defining selection bias with observed y or with y1 instead of y0; reading a large t as evidence of causality; assuming ATT = ATE outside an experiment; believing that one randomized estimate equals the ATE.

Keep it a real conversation: one host derives and the other pushes back with "why does that line hold?" No list-style summaries.
