Explain and discuss in depth (part 1/2) Monte Carlo methods across Class 1 and Classes 3+4: computing integrals and judging estimators with independent draws. Use the uploaded slides `Aula_class-03-04_aprendizagembayesiana-bayesianingredients.pdf` (slides 53–56), `Aula_class-01_poisson-with-breakpoint.pdf` (slides 8–12) and `Aula_class-01_comparing-estimators-MSE-slides.pdf` (slides 9–12), with `Livro_Edward Greenberg - Introduction to Bayesian Econometrics (2007, Cambridge University Press) - libgen.li.pdf` (Ch. 5, pp. 63–75) and `Livro_Morettin, Singer - Estatística e Ciência de Dados (versão preliminar, abril 2021).pdf` (App. B, §B.2, §B.4, §B.6, pp. 456–471). Part 2 covers MCMC. The thread: every posterior summary and every sampling property is an integral, and a Monte Carlo answer means nothing without its own standard error. It covers:

Monte Carlo integration and its error: Write E[g(X)] as an average of g over M independent draws. It is unbiased, converges by the law of large numbers and has variance V(g(X))/M, so Greenberg's numerical standard error is √(V̂/M) in any dimension. For the deck's hit-or-miss π, the indicator is Bernoulli with p = π/4, so se(π̂) = 1.6422/√M = 0.052 at M = 1000. The deck's 3.196 is 1.05 standard errors high, an ordinary draw. Four times the draws halve the error.

Monte Carlo studies of estimators: Treat a simulation study as an experiment with its own error. In the Poisson deck (λ = 2, N = 1000) the MC variance of ȳ at n = 10 is 0.193 against 0.200, and that of s² is 1.245 against 1.089; explain why a variance of s², driven by fourth moments, is estimated more noisily. For the four U(0, θ) estimators at θ = 3, n = 10, the MC MSEs 2.336, 0.149, 0.308 and 0.084 sit above the exact 2.325, 0.136, 0.300 and 0.075. Give the yardstick: a sample SD from N draws has relative error about 1/√(2(N−1)) = 2.2%, so 0.290 against 0.274 for (n+1)x_(n)/n is 2.6 such units, inflated by the skewed Beta(n, 1) law of the maximum. Note the catch: the study needs θ, which real data never give. The bootstrap answers that.

Inverse cdf and composition: Prove that F⁻¹(U) has cdf F. Work Greenberg's f(y) = 3y²/8 on [0, 2], so Y = 2U^(1/3), and the truncation formula F⁻¹(F(c1) + U[F(c2) − F(c1)]), which later draws the truncated normals of the Tobit and probit samplers of Class 7. Then show by composition that a normal with a Gamma(ν/2, ν/2) precision is a Student-t: integrate the precision out of the joint kernel. This is why t errors and the horseshoe fit inside Gibbs samplers.

Accept–reject: Prove with Bayes' rule that accepted draws have density f and that the acceptance rate is 1/c. For Beta(3, 3) from uniforms, c = 1.875, so 53.3% are accepted, and a proposal at 0.15 survives with probability 0.260. For the half-normal from an exponential proposal, c = √(2e/π) = 1.3155 and the acceptance rate is 0.760. Reconcile this with Greenberg's printed 0.6577, the constant for the unnormalised kernel.

Importance sampling and SIR: Derive the weights f/h, the self-normalised estimator of Morettin and Singer (B.17), and the variance condition that ∫g²f²/h be finite. Then expose a flaw in Greenberg's Algorithm 5.5. The Beta(2, 3) proposal vanishes at zero where the truncated exponential target does not, so the second moment diverges like ∫dx/x. The exact value is 0.8302, yet a seeded run gives 0.8003 with a reported n.s.e. of 0.0089, more than three standard errors away: the n.s.e. itself is meaningless. A uniform proposal fixes it. For SIR, resample the proposals with normalised weights. In the lecturer's physicist C example (10⁶ uniform proposals on 600 to 1000, likelihood N(850, 40²), a Student-t prior centred at 900), the SIR mean 891.13 matches quadrature, but the effective sample size (Σw)²/Σw² is 144,275, one draw in seven.

Python walkthrough: In words, describe numpy.random.default_rng for the π funnel with its ±2 standard-error band, a vectorised four-estimator study reporting each MSE with its own Monte Carlo error, the Beta(3, 3) accept–reject loop checked against variance 1/28, and an importance sampler that prints its effective sample size.

Close with misconceptions: a small reported n.s.e. does not prove a finite variance, a higher acceptance rate comes from a tighter envelope rather than a cleverer test, and none of these methods scales to many dimensions, which motivates the Markov chains of part 2.
