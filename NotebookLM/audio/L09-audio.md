Explain and discuss in depth Class 9, Multivariate time series: VARs, structural identification, Bayesian shrinkage, factors and time-varying volatility. Use the uploaded slides `Aula_class-09_BVAR-part11.pdf` and `Aula_class-09_BVAR-part2.pdf`. Pair them with `Livro_Ruey S. Tsay - Analysis of Financial Time Series, Third Edition (Wiley Series in Probability and Statistics)-John Wiley & Sons (2010).pdf` (§8.1–8.2, pp. 390–417; Ch. 9, §9.1–9.2; Ch. 10, §10.1–10.5; §11.1–11.3) and `Livro_Edward Greenberg - Introduction to Bayesian Econometrics (2007, Cambridge University Press) - libgen.li.pdf` (§9.1 SUR, §10.3 time-varying parameters). Class 1 introduced the VAR(1), and Class 8 the univariate volatility models; this class scales both to many series, where the main enemy is the number of parameters. It covers:

VAR mechanics: Write the VAR(p) as a multivariate regression Y = XB + U with U matrix-normal, and in vec form y = (I_q ⊗ X)β + u. Stack it in companion form y_t = A y_(t−1) + u_t, so the h-step forecast is A^h y_t. That forecast, like the impulse responses, is a highly nonlinear function of B, which is why posterior draws beat plug-ins. Stationarity requires every eigenvalue of A inside the unit circle (Tsay Example 8.3 from Class 1). Derive the VMA recursion Ψs = B1Ψs−1 + … + BpΨs−p with Ψ0 = I. The (i, j) element of Ψs is the impulse response of variable i at horizon s to a shock in j. Then orthogonalise Σ = ADA' by Cholesky to get the forecast-error variance decomposition, and explain why the variable ordering matters. Use the deck's simulated trivariate VAR(2) and the real-data example (stock returns, real interest rates, production growth and inflation, 1947–1987).

Minnesota and other priors: Explain Litterman's prior, which centres each equation on a random walk. Own first lags shrink toward one and everything else toward zero, with variance λ²/k² for own lags and λ²θ²σᵢ²/(k²σⱼ²) for other variables' lags, so longer lags are shrunk harder. Compare the Normal–Wishart, Jeffreys, Normal–Diffuse and extended natural conjugate priors of Kadiyala and Karlsson. Their seven-variable, six-lag model already has 301 coefficients. Count the parameters of a VAR(1), q² + q(q+1)/2: 15 at q = 3, 610 at q = 20 and 25,807 at q = 131.

Structural VARs: Write A0y_t = A1y_(t−1) + … + u_t with u_t ~ N(0, I), so that Bi = A0⁻¹Ai and Σ = (A0'A0)⁻¹. Explain why the reduced form cannot pin down A0 without restrictions, and the exact-identification definition of Rubio-Ramírez, Waggoner and Zha. Use the monetary-policy, production-sector and commodity-market equations in the deck, and explain what a monetary policy shock means once A0 is fixed.

Large BVARs, variable selection and factors: Present SSVS, a spike-and-slab prior with c0 = 0.1 and c1 = 10 times the OLS variance, and the Bernoulli full conditional of each inclusion indicator. Then the factor model y_t = βf_t + ε_t with Ω = ββ' + Σ. Its identification uses a lower-triangular β with a positive diagonal, and the parameter count bounds k: m = 6 allows at most 3 factors. Use the exchange-rate example (Lopes and West). Bańbura, Giannone and Reichlin show that shrinkage tightened with the number of variables lets a 131-variable BVAR forecast and produce credible impulse responses. Explain the FAVAR, which adds factors extracted from 132 Stock–Watson series to a small VAR (Bernanke, Boivin and Eliasz; Korobilis), and why the out-of-sample comparison uses RMSFE.

Time-varying parameters and covariances: Contrast VAR-GARCH, Uhlig's BVAR with stochastic volatility, and Primiceri's TVP-VAR-SV. In the last, Σt = At⁻¹Dt(At⁻¹)', and the coefficients, Cholesky elements and log volatilities each follow random walks; link this to the state-space and Kalman filter machinery of Tsay Ch. 11 and Greenberg §10.3. For covariances, compare factor stochastic volatility (common factors with their own log-volatility AR(1)) with DCC, which fits univariate GARCH models and then a correlation recursion (Tsay Ch. 10). Explain which one scales and which one has interpretable factors.

Python walkthrough: In words, describe statsmodels VAR with select_order, irf and fevd, a Minnesota-prior posterior computed equation by equation in numpy, and a factor SV or BVAR in PyMC, saying what each output should show.

Close with misconceptions: that Cholesky impulse responses are structural whatever the ordering, that more variables always hurt forecasts, and that a time-varying model is needed whenever residuals look heteroskedastic.
