This is the computational half: estimators and models are judged by simulation and by what they predict. Part 1 covered the likelihood, MSE and p-values. It covers:

Monte Carlo and the bootstrap: Present the Monte Carlo MSE as an average of squared errors over replications, and the bootstrap standard error as the spread of resampled estimates. Then prove that the nonparametric bootstrap fails for the maximum of U(0, θ). A resample reproduces x_(n) with probability 1 − (1 − 1/n)^n, which tends to 0.632, a point mass the true sampling distribution of x_(n) does not have. Explain why resampling from U(0, θ̂) instead, the parametric bootstrap, reproduces the right spread: at n = 10 with θ̂ = 2.568, its standard errors for the four estimators match the plug-in formulas to about two decimals. Discuss why every bootstrap answer is conditional on the one observed sample.

One rate or two: Use the breakpoint deck on the British coal-mining disasters, 1851–1962. Derive the dispersion test D = (n−1)s²/ȳ, approximately χ² with n−1 degrees of freedom: the index 1.592 rejects equidispersion with p < 0.001. Then fit two Poisson rates split at a year τ and derive the profile likelihood of τ. It peaks at 1891, with rates 3.125 and 0.917 against a single rate of 1.705, and an LR statistic of 70.0. Argue carefully why 70.0 is not referred to χ² with 1 degree of freedom. The regularity behind Wilks' theorem in B&S §6.5 fails because τ was searched over, is discrete, and is unidentified under the null. The year itself is uncertain: weight 0.251 on 1891 and 0.696 on 1889–1892. Contrast profiling τ with integrating it out, the route Greenberg's Gibbs switch-point model takes in Classes 3+4.

Gaussian regression in matrix form: Derive β̂ = (X'X)⁻¹X'y from the normal equations as a projection. The hat matrix P is symmetric and idempotent with trace q. Show that OLS is the Gaussian MLE, and that the unbiased σ² divides RSS by n − q. Explain why R² never falls as regressors are added, so it cannot choose a model. Compare adjusted R², Cp, AIC and BIC (Wooldridge eq. 6.21–6.22). Use the overfitting example: an in-sample R² of 1.0000 against 0.052 out of sample, with test RMSE 47.13 against 4.48. Derive the optimism 2pσ²/n. Then have the hosts read the municipal abstention table (5,565 municipalities, HC1 errors). The IDHM coefficient is +1.013 without region dummies and −0.356 with them, an omitted-variable sign flip. The size dummies have small individual t-values yet are jointly significant (F = 13.22).

AR(1), the unit root and VAR(1): With the AR slides and Tsay §2.4, write the AR(1) as a regression on its lag, so OLS is the conditional MLE. Derive the mean φ0/(1−φ1), the variance σ²/(1−φ1²) and the ACF φ1^ℓ. The equilibrium law is N(μ, σ²/(1−φ1²)): at φ1 = 0.9 the variance is 5.263σ² and the half-life is 6.58 periods. At φ1 = 1 the variance grows like tσ² and no equilibrium exists. Write the AR(p) as a regression, with stationarity when every characteristic root lies outside the unit circle. For VAR(1), use Tsay Example 8.3. Eigenvalues 0.8 and 0.5 make it stationary although one diagonal coefficient is 1.1. Derive μ = (I − Φ)⁻¹φ0 = (1, 2)' and the vec formula for the covariance, vec(Γ0) = (I − Φ⊗Φ)⁻¹vec(Σ), which here gives Γ0 = (1/9)[25, 20; 20, 40]. Mention the Ljung–Box statistic Q(m) = T(T+2)Σ ρ̂ℓ²/(T−ℓ) as the check that residuals are white noise.
