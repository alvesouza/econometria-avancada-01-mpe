Part 1 showed why naive regression fails: confounding, OVB and attenuation. This part covers three designs that recover a causal effect when randomisation is unavailable, and the assumption each one buys. It covers:

Instrumental variables: Start from the DAG in section 9, Z → X → Y with unmeasured confounders between X and Y. Write the two-equation system y = β0 + β1x + εy and x = α0 + α1z + εx, with correlated errors. Substitute to get the reduced form, whose slope is β1α1, and derive β_IV = cov(z, y)/cov(z, x), the effect of z on y over the effect of z on x. Correlation ρ = 0 means no endogeneity, and α1 = 0 means no instrument. Derive from section 9.2 why simply adding z to the outcome regression does not identify β1. Connect this to MHE eq. 4.1.3, the Wald estimator (0.0891 with quarter-of-birth instruments, Table 4.1.2) and two-stage least squares. Use the notes' cases. For Card's college-proximity instrument, OLS gives 0.075 (s.e. 0.003) and IV 0.132 (s.e. 0.055), with a 95% interval of (0.022, 0.242). For married women with father's education as the instrument, the return is 11% by OLS and 5.9% by 2SLS. The cigarette-price instrument for smoking fails relevance, the one IV condition we can test. Discuss exclusion, weak instruments and the first-stage F (Wooldridge §15.3), testing endogeneity (§15.5), and why IV estimates a local effect for compliers. Mention Greenberg's Bayesian treatment of endogenous covariates (§11.2).

Difference-in-differences: From section 10, explain the two back doors, through time and through group membership, and how differencing closes both. Write y = β0 + β1P + β2T + β3P·T + x'γ + ε and read each coefficient as a cell mean or a difference of means. Use Card and Krueger: New Jersey raised its minimum wage from 4.25 to 5.05 dollars in April 1992, while Pennsylvania did not. Full-time-equivalent employment rose 0.467 in New Jersey and fell 2.283 in Pennsylvania, so the DiD estimate is 2.75 (s.e. 1.731, p = 0.113, 384 restaurants). Note that the notes once label NJ as 0 and PA as 1, but the code and numbers treat NJ as treated. State the parallel-trends assumption, how pre-period trends probe it (MHE §5.2, Table 5.2.1; Wooldridge §13.2), and the Bayesian version in the notes, where draws of β4 come from its posterior.

Regression discontinuity: From section 11, use the minimum legal drinking age. Treatment D = 1 when age is at least 21 is a deterministic, discontinuous function of the running variable: a sharp RD. Fit y = α + ρD + γa + e: the jump in deaths per 100,000 at 21 is ρ̂ = 7.7. Discuss nonlinearity mistaken for a discontinuity, quadratic fits with separate slopes on each side, and the non-parametric version: local linear regression in a window [a0 − b, a0 + b], trading bias near the cutoff against variance. Contrast motor-vehicle deaths, which jump, with internal causes, which do not, as a placebo. Explain why RD fails if units can sort precisely around the cutoff: age cannot be manipulated, but test scores or incomes can, so check the density of the running variable. Explain Gelman and Imbens' warning against high-order polynomials, and the fuzzy RD of MHE Ch. 6 as IV at the cutoff.
