Angrist, Jorn-Steffen Pischke-Mostly Harmless Econometrics_ An Empiricist's Companion (2008).pdf` (MHE §2.2–2.3, printed pp. 12–18) and `Livro_Wooldridge J.M-Introductory econometrics. Part 1 derived naive gap = ATT + selection bias; this episode takes that result to data. It covers:

The Tennessee STAR design: The 1985/86 kindergarten cohort, about 11,600 children, three arms (small classes of 13–17, regular classes of 22–25, regular with a full-time aide), randomized within schools. Explain why within-school randomization is conditional random assignment.

Balance first (MHE Table 2.2.1, p. 14): Free lunch is .47/.48/.50 (joint p = .09) and age 5.44/5.43/5.42. Explain what a balance table can test (observables) and what it cannot (unobservables, which are balanced only in expectation). Class size, 15.1 vs 22.4, shows the treatment "took". Attrition, .49/.52/.53 with p = .02, threatens later-grade comparisons (MHE fn. 3), which is why the kindergarten results are the most reliable.

Results (MHE Table 2.2.2, p. 15): The small-class estimate is 4.82 (SE 2.19) with no controls, 5.37 (1.26) with school fixed effects, 5.36 (1.21) adding student covariates, and 5.37 (1.19) adding teacher covariates. That is roughly 0.2 standard deviations. Explain the two roles of covariates: school fixed effects are required by the design, and the other controls buy precision. Explain formally why the SE falls: covariates uncorrelated with d leave the coefficient alone but cut the residual variance. Connect to Wooldridge §6-3, "Adding Regressors to Reduce the Error Variance", and its point that a low R-squared is no evidence of bias when the regressor is randomly assigned.

Experiments as regressions: With a constant effect, derive MHE eq. 2.3.1, y = α + ρd + η, where α = E(y0) and η = y0 − E(y0). Take conditional expectations to reach ρ + E[η|d=1] − E[η|d=0], and conclude that selection bias is a correlation between the regressor and the error. Show the same idea in Wooldridge eq. 7.34, where self-selection means E(u|partic=1) ≠ E(u|partic=0). For Wooldridge Problem 7.6, talk through the reasoning about less able workers being selected into training, and leave the final sign of the bias as a question for the listener. Note that OLS on a single dummy returns exactly the difference in means. With heterogeneous effects, random assignment still makes OLS estimate the ATE; link this to random slopes in Wooldridge §9-3.

The long regression (MHE eq. 2.3.2): In the population, adding X uncorrelated with d leaves ρ unchanged. In a finite sample, chance imbalance moves the estimate a little. Explain why a large move when controls are added is a red flag: failed randomization, or controls that are themselves outcomes of treatment (bad controls, Class 2).

Precision versus identification, by simulation: Walk verbally through an R simulation, without printing code. Draw y0 from a normal with σ = 2, build a latent selection index with correlation λ = −0.6 to y0, set a homogeneous effect of 1, and treat half the population. Derive the closed form SB = σλφ(c)/(π(1 − π)); at π = 0.5 this is 4σλφ(0) ≈ −1.915, so the naive estimate settles near −0.91. Explain why 95% intervals then cover the true effect almost never, from N = 100 to 10,000, while under a coin flip they cover it about 95% of the time. Explain why coverage at N = 10 sits a bit under 95%: 1.96 is a large-sample critical value.

Experimental versus observational job training: Wooldridge C1.4 uses JTRAIN2, the Lalonde (1986) experiment. C9.10 sets it against JTRAIN3, where men choose whether to participate. Discuss why participation shares differ, and why adding re74, re75, educ, age, black and hisp should barely move the experimental estimate but can move the observational one a lot. Connect to MHE p. 13 on non-experimental training evaluations. Reason about directions only, and do not invent numerical answers.

Close on the limits of trials (STAR's cost of about 12 million dollars and its duration). Introduce natural experiments, such as Angrist–Lavy's class-size cap of 40 (MHE p. 16), as the bridge to the identification strategies of later classes.
