Explain and discuss in depth (part 1/2) Class 1, Review of basic ingredients: likelihoods, estimators and what counts as evidence. Use the uploaded slides `Aula_class-01_mle_sampling_rules.pdf`, `Aula_class-01_class1-poisson.pdf`, `Aula_class-01_class1-poisson-with-exponential-prior.pdf`, `Aula_class-01_comparing-estimators-MSE-slides.pdf`, `Aula_class-01_part1-p-hacking.pdf` and `Aula_class-01_p_hacking_slides-economics.pdf`, with `Livro_Bolfarine, Sandoval - Introdução à Inferência Estatística (2010).pdf` (B&S §1.3, §2.1–2.2, §3.1–3.2, pp. 4–45) and `Livro_Edward Greenberg - Introduction to Bayesian Econometrics (2007, Cambridge University Press) - libgen.li.pdf` (§2.2, pp. 12–18). This is the conceptual half: the likelihood picks the estimator, but the design decides how it behaves. The listeners are master's students; speak each derivation step and argue about edge cases. It covers:

Two experimenters, one likelihood: Binomial data (n fixed) and negative binomial data (s fixed) share the kernel θ^s(1−θ)^(n−s), the MLE s/n, the observed information and the posterior. Then show what differs. With s = 1 and θ = 0.1 the MLE has mean 0.100 under the binomial but 0.256 under the negative binomial. For s = 1 and n = 10, the p-values against θ = 0.5 are 0.0107 and 0.0020. Debate the likelihood principle and why frequentist properties depend on the stopping rule. Mention Haldane's fix, (s−1)/(N−1), which is exactly unbiased under inverse sampling for s ≥ 2. Note that with s = 1 the Wald interval leaves [0, 1], a sign that the asymptotics have not arrived.

The four properties: Derive the Poisson MLE λ̂ = ȳ from the Poisson slides and B&S. Check sufficiency by factorisation, then unbiasedness, consistency, and Cramér–Rao efficiency with Fisher information n/λ. Contrast s². It is also unbiased, but its efficiency is 1/(1+2λ), only 20% at λ = 2. Explain why unbiased does not mean good. On consistency, contrast the deck's Monte Carlo with Chebyshev's bound for P(|λ̂ − 2| < 0.25). At n = 10 the probability is 0.42 but the bound is vacuous; at n = 200 they are 0.98 and 0.84. Chebyshev proves the limit but cannot measure the speed.

A first prior: From the handwritten exponential-prior notes and Greenberg §2.2, derive that an exponential prior with rate a times a Poisson likelihood gives Gamma(s_n+1, a+n). State its mean, mode and variance. On the coal-mining counts for 1851–1891, the posterior mean is 3.084 and the MLE is 3.098. Read the weight a/(n+a) as shrinkage toward the prior mean of 2.

MSE and the four estimators of θ in U(0, θ): Derive MSE = variance + bias². Use B&S §1.1.5, Table 1.1 and the MSE deck to compare x̄, x_(n), 2x̄ and (n+1)x_(n)/n. Their MSEs are (1+3n)θ²/(12n), 2θ²/((n+1)(n+2)), θ²/(3n) and θ²/(n(n+2)), which at θ = 3, n = 10 give 2.325, 0.136, 0.300 and 0.075. Explain why the biased x_(n) beats the unbiased 2x̄. The ranking is strict only from n = 3; at n = 2, x_(n) and 2x̄ tie at θ²/6, and at n = 1 all four MSEs are equal. The handwritten note `Aula_class-01_Class1-comparing-estimators-MSE.pdf` states the ranking for n ≥ 2, so say why that slip matters. Also derive x_(n)/θ ~ Beta(n, 1), which gives the mean nθ/(n+1). Then explain why the MLE x_(n) sits on the boundary of the support, so the score never equals zero and the Cramér–Rao regularity conditions fail. Flag the misprint in B&S Table 1.1: θ²/662 should read θ²/66.

p-values versus evidence: From the two p-hacking decks, compute the chance of at least one false positive across 32 independent specifications: 1 − 0.95^32 = 0.81. Discuss why shared data make this an upper bound, and why Bonferroni uses 0.05/32. Then take X = 1.8. The p-value is 0.036, yet the Bayes factor favours H0 by 2.23 and P(H1 | x) = 0.31. Reaching 0.9 would need prior odds of about 20:1. Connect this to the economics deck: Brodeur and coauthors (2016) find a two-humped distribution of more than 50,000 published z-statistics around 1.96. Brodeur, Cook and Heyes (2020) find the most inflation in IV and difference-in-differences and less in RCTs and RDD. Then discuss the remedies: pre-registration, Romano–Wolf corrections and the proposal to use p < 0.005.

Python walkthrough: Without reading code aloud, describe how to simulate the binomial and negative-binomial sampling distributions with numpy.random.default_rng, and how to check the four MSE formulas by Monte Carlo with scipy.stats.

Close with two misconceptions: that a sufficient statistic must be unbiased, and that a small p-value is the probability that H0 is true. Point ahead: the Gamma update becomes conjugate analysis in Classes 3+4, and MSE becomes prediction error in Class 2.
