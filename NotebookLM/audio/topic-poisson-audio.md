Explain and discuss in depth the Poisson model as one thread across the course, from Class 1 to Classes 3+4. Use the uploaded slides `Aula_class-01_class1-poisson.pdf`, `Aula_class-01_poisson-with-breakpoint.pdf` and `Aula_class-01_class1-poisson-with-exponential-prior.pdf`, with `Livro_Bolfarine, Sandoval - Introdução à Inferência Estatística (2010).pdf` (Ex. 2.1.2, 2.2.3–2.2.4, 3.2.5, §3.4, §4.4, §6.5), `Livro_Edward Greenberg - Introduction to Bayesian Econometrics (2007, Cambridge University Press) - libgen.li.pdf` (§2.2, §3.2.3, §7.1.1, pp. 91–95) and `Livro_James, Witten, Hastie, Tibshirani, Taylor - An Introduction to Statistical Learning with Applications in Python (2023).pdf` (§4.6, pp. 167–173). The listeners already know the MLE ȳ, its Cramér–Rao efficiency, the coal-mining LR of 70.0 and the first Gamma posterior. Go one level deeper and keep linking the classes. It covers:

The fingerprint E = V = λ: Derive it from the factorial moment E[y(y−1)] = λ². Prove sufficiency by the definition, not only by factorisation: given the total t, the counts are multinomial(t; 1/n, …, 1/n), balls dropped uniformly into n boxes, so they say nothing about λ (Bolfarine Ex. 2.2.3). Derive V(s²) = λ/n + 2λ²/(n−1) from μ4 = λ + 3λ², so s² has efficiency 0.184 at n = 10, λ = 2. Note that no MLE exists when every count is zero, which happens with probability e^(−nλ). Use invariance (Ex. 3.2.5): the MLE of P(y = 0) is e^(−ȳ), with asymptotic variance λe^(−2λ)/n, while Rao–Blackwell gives the unbiased ((n−1)/n)^(s_n). Since nȳ is Poisson(2n), the deck's Monte Carlo coverage of ±0.25 can be checked exactly: 0.424, 0.789 and 0.987 at n = 10, 50, 200.

Overdispersion as a mixture: The coal series has 112 years, mean 1.705 and variance 2.714. Apply the law of total variance to two regimes, 40 years at 3.125 and 72 at 0.917: V = 1.705 + π(1−π)(3.125 − 0.917)² = 2.825. The sample split 301.3 = 175.9 within + 125.4 between says the same. Zero years are 29.5% of the data against e^(−1.705) = 18.2% under one rate, and large counts are too frequent too: a mixture's signature. Each segment then passes the dispersion test (p = 0.774 and 0.168).

Profiling, integrating, sampling: Contrast three answers to "where is the break?". Class 1 profiles the rates out and puts weight 0.251 on 1891. Integrating them against Exp(a) priors gives each segment a factor Γ(s_j + 1)/(n_j + a)^(s_j + 1); with a = 1/2 the weights barely move because both segments are long. Explain why height and area part ways when a segment is short. Then link to Greenberg's switch-point model (7.3)–(7.4), sampled by Gibbs in Classes 3+4. Its full conditionals are Gamma(α0 + S_k, β0 + k), Gamma(α0 + S_n − S_k, β0 + n − k) and a discrete law on k. With Exp(1/2) on both rates, the exact P(k = 1891 | y) is 0.240, with E(λ1 | y) = 3.106 and E(λ2 | y) = 0.930. Point out that E(λ1 | k = 1891, y) = 128/41.5 = 3.084 is exactly the Class 1 posterior mean for 1851–1891.

Conjugacy and prediction: With a Gamma(α0, β0) prior the posterior is Gamma(α0 + s_n, β0 + n), adding events to the shape and exposure to the rate (Bolfarine Ex. 4.4.2). So Exp(1/2) is one disaster seen in half a year, and updating year by year gives the same posterior. Derive the posterior predictive, a negative binomial (Greenberg §3.2.3), with mean α1/β1 and variance (α1/β1)(1 + 1/β1). For Gamma(128, 41.5) these are 3.084 and 3.159: parameter uncertainty alone makes the predictive overdispersed. Warn that a prior cannot rescue a wrong model: on all 112 years the posterior mean 1.707 is a confident estimate of a single rate that does not exist.

Poisson regression: With ISLP §4.6, let log λ_i = x_i'β. The score becomes X'(y − λ), the Class 1 score with covariates and the same form as the logit's. In the lecturer's blog-visitor example (28 days, weekday mean 109, Friday-to-Sunday mean 83.33), each standard error comes from group totals: SE(β̂) = √(1/1744 + 1/1000) = 0.0397, the Fisher information n/λ on the log scale. The Pearson dispersion 1.444 moves z from −6.77 to −5.63. Name the zero-inflated Poisson of Classes 3+4 (fishing groups: 24 zeros among 45, MLE λ̂ = 4.223, π̂ = 0.526), whose variance (1−π)λ(1+πλ) is a third route to excess zeros.

Python walkthrough: In words, describe the exact coverage check with scipy.stats.poisson, the integrated weight of each cut year with scipy.special.gammaln, a NumPy Gibbs sampler compared with that exact posterior, and a statsmodels Poisson GLM with the scale set to Pearson chi-squared, saying what each check should reproduce.

Close with misconceptions: overdispersion is a verdict on a model against data, not a property of counts; profile weights are not posterior probabilities; and Poisson standard errors are right only when the variance equals the mean.
