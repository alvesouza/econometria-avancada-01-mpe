A reminder on notation for the volatility half. r sub t is the return; mu sub t is its conditional mean given the information at t minus one; a sub t, equal to r sub t minus mu sub t, is the shock; sigma t squared is the conditional variance, and sigma sub t the volatility; epsilon sub t, equal to a sub t over sigma sub t, is the standardized innovation, iid with mean zero and variance one. Part three, section 6. ARCH and GARCH: a variance that is forecastable, a kurtosis that is not normal.

This section covers the site page garchmodeling, its ARCH and GARCH sections, Petrobras 2000 to 2021, n equal to 5167; the site page garch-sv-petrobras, its ARCH, GARCH and GARCH with Student t sections, Petrobras 2000 to 2025; Tsay sections 3.1 to 3.3, printed pages 110 to 115; section 3.4, printed pages 115 to 131, equations 3.5 to 3.13, Example 3.1 on Intel and Example 3.2; section 3.5, printed pages 131 to 140, equations 3.14 to 3.21, Example 3.3 on the S and P 500, Table 3.1 on printed page 138, and two-pass estimation on printed page 140; section 3.6, printed pages 140 to 142, equation 3.22; and section 3.16, printed pages 165 to 166; and Wooldridge section 12.6, printed pages 391 to 396, equations 12.46 to 12.50 and Example 12.8.

The claim, before the derivations. Returns can be serially uncorrelated and still dependent: their conditional variance moves. ARCH writes that variance as a linear function of past squared shocks: sigma t squared equals alpha zero plus the sum of alpha i times a sub t minus i squared. That one equation produces volatility clusters and, even with Gaussian innovations, an unconditional kurtosis above 3: for the ARCH of order one, three times one minus alpha one squared, over one minus three alpha one squared. GARCH adds the lagged variance and is an ARMA model for a sub t squared: the long-run variance is alpha zero over one minus alpha one minus beta one, and forecasts decay to it at rate alpha one plus beta one. The empirical regularity is alpha one plus beta one close to one, 0.9727 for Tsay's S and P 500 and 0.980 for Petrobras, so a volatility shock has a half-life of months or weeks. At alpha one plus beta one equal to one, IGARCH, the variance has a unit root, the unconditional variance does not exist, and RiskMetrics' exponential smoothing is the special case alpha zero equal to zero.

The notation. r sub t is the log return at t; the garch-sv-petrobras page uses simple returns. F sub t minus one is the information at t minus one. Mu sub t is the conditional mean, from the mean equation. a sub t is the shock, or innovation. Sigma t squared is the conditional variance, from the volatility equation, always positive, and sigma sub t is the volatility. Epsilon sub t is the standardized innovation. Alpha zero, alpha i and beta j are the constant, the ARCH coefficients and the GARCH coefficients, with alpha zero positive and the others non-negative. m and s are the ARCH and GARCH orders. Eta sub t, equal to a sub t squared minus sigma t squared, is the variance surprise, a martingale difference. Sigma infinity squared is the unconditional variance, the expected value of a sub t squared. Sigma h squared of ell is the ell-step variance forecast at origin h. K is the excess kurtosis of epsilon sub t, so the expected value of epsilon to the fourth is K plus 3, with K equal to zero for the normal. And nu is the degrees of freedom of the standardized Student t, above 2.

Mapping to the site pages. The garchmodeling page writes y sub t equals sigma sub t times epsilon sub t, and sigma t squared equals omega plus alpha one epsilon at t minus one squared plus beta one sigma at t minus one squared. Its omega is Tsay's alpha zero, and the epsilon at t minus one inside the recursion must be read as the raw residual, a sub t minus one, which equals y sub t minus one, because fGarch filters the residuals, not the standardized ones. Taken literally, with epsilon standardized, the recursion would make sigma t squared independent of the level of past volatility.

Six point one. Intuition layer: what volatility does. Tsay section 3.1, printed pages 110 to 111.

Volatility is not observed: one return per day carries no variance by itself. Implied volatility, such as the VIX, rests on an option-pricing model. Tsay lists four regularities that any model must reproduce. First, clusters: high-volatility periods and calm periods. Second, continuity: volatility evolves smoothly, and jumps are rare. Third, mean reversion within a range: volatility does not diverge, so it is "often stationary". Fourth, leverage: a big price drop raises volatility more than a big rise of the same size.

ARCH and GARCH capture the first three. The fourth needs the asymmetric models of section 7. The motivating evidence is already in Class 1: IBM monthly returns pass Ljung-Box at lag 30, while their squares give Q of 30 equal to 182.12.

Six point two. The structure of a volatility model. Tsay sections 3.2 and 3.3, printed pages 111 to 115.

The conditional mean and variance of r sub t given F sub t minus one. Equation 1: mu sub t equals the expected value of r sub t given F sub t minus one; sigma t squared equals the variance of r sub t given F sub t minus one, which is the conditional expected value of r sub t minus mu sub t, squared.

The mean equation is a simple stationary ARMA with regressors, Tsay equation 3.3. Equation 2: r sub t equals mu sub t plus a sub t; mu sub t equals the sum over i from one to p of phi i y sub t minus i, minus the sum over i from one to q of theta i a sub t minus i; and y sub t equals r sub t minus phi zero minus the sum over i from one to k of beta i x i t.

Because mu sub t is known at t minus one, substituting equation 2 into equation 1 gives, Tsay equation 3.4, equation 3: sigma t squared equals the conditional variance of mu sub t plus a sub t, which is the conditional variance of a sub t.

A volatility model is an equation for equation 3. GARCH makes sigma t squared an exact function of F sub t minus one. Stochastic volatility adds its own shock, section 8.

Four steps, Tsay printed page 113. First, fit the mean equation and remove linear dependence. Second, test the residuals for ARCH effects. Third, if present, specify a volatility model and estimate the mean and volatility equations jointly. Fourth, check and refine.

Six point two point one. Testing for ARCH effects. Tsay section 3.3.1, printed page 114.

McLeod-Li: apply the Ljung-Box statistic Q of m to the series a sub t squared.

Engle's Lagrange multiplier test: run the auxiliary regression, equation 4: a sub t squared equals alpha zero plus alpha one a sub t minus one squared plus, and so on, plus alpha m a sub t minus m squared, plus e sub t, for t from m plus one to T. Test the null that alpha one through alpha m are all zero with, equation 5: F equals SSR zero minus SSR one, over m, divided by SSR one over T minus two m minus one. Here SSR zero is the sum, from t equal to m plus one to T, of a sub t squared minus omega bar, squared, where omega bar is the average of a sub t squared over the whole sample; and SSR one is the sum of the squared residuals e hat sub t.

Tsay states that equation 5 is asymptotically chi-squared with m degrees of freedom. Strictly, it is m times F, equivalently T times the R squared of equation 4, that has the chi-squared limit; the software prints that statistic against a chi-squared with m degrees of freedom.

Worked: Intel monthly log returns, 1973 to 2008, T equal to 432, Tsay printed pages 114 to 115. On the returns, Q of 12 is 18.26, p-value 0.108: no serial correlation, so a sub t is r sub t minus its mean. On the squares, Q of 12 is 89.85, p-value 5.3 times ten to the minus 14. The Lagrange multiplier statistic is 53.62 on a chi-squared with 12 degrees of freedom, p-value approximately zero. Uncorrelated but not independent.

Six point three. The ARCH model. Tsay section 3.4, printed pages 115 to 123.

An ARCH model of order m, Engle 1982, Tsay equation 3.5. Equation 6: a sub t equals sigma sub t times epsilon sub t; sigma t squared equals alpha zero plus alpha one a sub t minus one squared plus, and so on, plus alpha m a sub t minus m squared; with epsilon sub t iid, mean zero and variance one. Alpha zero is positive and the alpha i are non-negative, which keeps sigma t squared positive. A large a sub t minus one squared makes sigma t squared large, which makes a large absolute a sub t more likely, but not certain: that is the cluster.

Six point three point one. The moments of the ARCH of order one. Tsay printed pages 117 to 118.

Take m equal to one: sigma t squared equals alpha zero plus alpha one a sub t minus one squared.

The mean. By iterated expectations, with sigma sub t known at t minus one and the expected value of epsilon sub t zero. Equation 7: the expected value of a sub t equals the expected value of its conditional expectation, which is the expected value of sigma sub t times the expected value of epsilon sub t, which is zero.

The variance. The conditional expected value of a sub t squared is sigma t squared times the expected value of epsilon sub t squared, which is sigma t squared. So the variance of a sub t equals the expected value of a sub t squared, which equals the expected value of the conditional expectation, which is the expected value of alpha zero plus alpha one a sub t minus one squared, which is alpha zero plus alpha one times the expected value of a sub t minus one squared. Under stationarity the expected values of a sub t squared and a sub t minus one squared both equal the variance of a sub t. Equation 8: the variance of a sub t equals alpha zero plus alpha one times the variance of a sub t, so the variance of a sub t equals alpha zero over one minus alpha one, with alpha one between zero and one, one excluded.

The fourth moment. With Gaussian epsilon, the expected value of epsilon to the fourth is 3. Equation 9: the conditional expected value of a sub t to the fourth is sigma t to the fourth times 3, which is three times alpha zero plus alpha one a sub t minus one squared, squared.

Expand the square and take expectations: the expected value of a sub t to the fourth is three times the expected value of alpha zero squared plus two alpha zero alpha one a sub t minus one squared plus alpha one squared a sub t minus one to the fourth, which is three times the bracket alpha zero squared plus two alpha zero alpha one times the variance of a sub t plus alpha one squared times the expected value of a sub t minus one to the fourth.

Under fourth-order stationarity, m four, the expected value of a sub t to the fourth, equals the expected value of a sub t minus one to the fourth. Substitute equation 8 for the variance. Equation 10: m four equals three times the bracket alpha zero squared, plus two alpha zero alpha one times alpha zero over one minus alpha one, plus alpha one squared m four.

Collect the m four terms: m four times one minus three alpha one squared equals three alpha zero squared times one plus two alpha one over one minus alpha one, which is three alpha zero squared times one plus alpha one, over one minus alpha one. Equation 11: m four equals three alpha zero squared times one plus alpha one, divided by one minus alpha one times one minus three alpha one squared.

Since m four is positive, it exists only if one minus three alpha one squared is positive, that is, alpha one squared below one third, alpha one below 0.577.

The kurtosis, dividing equation 11 by the square of equation 8. Equation 12: m four over the squared variance equals three alpha zero squared times one plus alpha one over one minus alpha one times one minus three alpha one squared, times one minus alpha one squared over alpha zero squared; which is three times one plus alpha one times one minus alpha one, over one minus three alpha one squared; which is three times one minus alpha one squared, over one minus three alpha one squared, and that is greater than 3.

Gaussian conditionally, fat-tailed unconditionally. A mixture of normals with random variances has heavier tails than a normal, so ARCH "produces outliers" more often than Gaussian white noise, Tsay printed page 118. Every alpha one between zero and one over the square root of three gives kurtosis above 3.

Six point three point two. Weaknesses, Tsay printed page 119. Four of them. First, positive and negative shocks enter through a sub t minus one squared, so they have the same effect: no leverage. Second, it is restrictive: for the ARCH of order one with a finite fourth moment, alpha one squared must be below one third, which limits the kurtosis it can generate. Third, it describes; it does not explain. Fourth, it responds slowly to large isolated shocks and tends to over-predict volatility afterwards.

Six point three point three. Order, likelihood, checking and forecasts. Tsay printed pages 119 to 123.

The order. Define eta sub t equal to a sub t squared minus sigma t squared. Substitute sigma t squared equal to a sub t squared minus eta sub t into equation 6. Equation 13: a sub t squared equals alpha zero plus alpha one a sub t minus one squared plus, and so on, plus alpha m a sub t minus m squared, plus eta sub t. That is an autoregression of order m for a sub t squared, with uncorrelated but not iid errors. So the partial autocorrelation of a sub t squared suggests m, Tsay printed page 120, as the partial autocorrelation suggested p in section 1.

The Gaussian conditional likelihood. The joint density factors into one-step conditionals; drop the density of the first m shocks, Tsay printed page 120. Equation 14: the density of a sub m plus one through a sub T, given alpha and the first m shocks, is the product over t from m plus one to T of one over the square root of two pi sigma t squared, times the exponential of minus a sub t squared over two sigma t squared. Equation 15: the log-likelihood, ell of alpha, equals minus one half times the sum over t from m plus one to T of the bracket: the log of two pi, plus the log of sigma t squared, plus a sub t squared over sigma t squared. Here sigma t squared is computed recursively from equation 6. There is no closed form: equation 15 is maximised numerically; the Python note does it with scipy's optimizer.

The standardized Student t, Tsay equation 3.7. If x sub nu is Student t with nu degrees of freedom, its variance is nu over nu minus two, for nu above two. So epsilon sub t equal to x sub nu divided by the square root of nu over nu minus two has variance one. Change variables, x equal to epsilon times the square root of nu over nu minus two, in the Student t density, using x squared over nu equal to epsilon squared over nu minus two, and the Jacobian, the square root of nu over nu minus two. Equation 16: the density of epsilon given nu equals gamma of nu plus one over two, divided by the square root of nu pi times gamma of nu over two, times one plus epsilon squared over nu minus two raised to the minus nu plus one over two, times the square root of nu over nu minus two; which simplifies to gamma of nu plus one over two, divided by gamma of nu over two times the square root of nu minus two times pi, times one plus epsilon squared over nu minus two, raised to the minus nu plus one over two.

The rescaling matters: it keeps sigma t squared the conditional variance, so the volatility equation means the same thing under both distributions. With a sub t equal to sigma sub t epsilon sub t, the conditional density of a sub t is the density of a sub t over sigma sub t, given nu, divided by sigma sub t. For fixed nu the log-likelihood is, up to a constant, Tsay equation 3.8, equation 17: ell of alpha equals minus the sum over t from m plus one to T of the bracket: nu plus one over two, times the log of one plus a sub t squared over nu minus two times sigma t squared, plus one half the log of sigma t squared.

Estimating nu jointly adds T minus m times the bracket: the log of gamma of nu plus one over two, minus the log of gamma of nu over two, minus one half the log of nu minus two times pi. Prespecified values are usually between 4 and 8. The skew t, Tsay equation 3.9 after Fernández and Steel, and the generalized error distribution, Tsay equation 3.10, are the other options; they are read in section 7, subsection 5.

Checking. If the model is right, the standardized residual, a sub t over sigma hat sub t, is iid. Ljung-Box on the standardized residuals checks the mean equation; on their squares, the volatility equation; and skewness, kurtosis and a quantile-quantile plot check the distribution, Tsay printed pages 122 to 123.

Forecasts, Tsay equation 3.11. At origin h, the one-step variance forecast is alpha zero plus alpha one a sub h squared plus, and so on, plus alpha m a sub h plus one minus m squared. In general, equation 18: sigma h squared of ell equals alpha zero plus the sum over i from one to m of alpha i times sigma h squared of ell minus i, where sigma h squared of ell minus i is the observed a sub h plus ell minus i squared if ell minus i is zero or negative. That is the autoregressive forecast recursion of section 2 applied to equation 13.

Six point four. GARCH. Tsay section 3.5, printed pages 131 to 138.

ARCH often needs many lags: an ARCH of order nine for the S and P 500 monthly series, Tsay printed page 131. Bollerslev's 1986 GARCH of orders m and s, Tsay equation 3.14. Equation 20: a sub t equals sigma sub t epsilon sub t; sigma t squared equals alpha zero plus the sum over i from one to m of alpha i a sub t minus i squared, plus the sum over j from one to s of beta j sigma t minus j squared; with the sum over i up to the larger of m and s of alpha i plus beta i below one.

Six point four point one. GARCH is an ARMA for a sub t squared.

Eta sub t, equal to a sub t squared minus sigma t squared, is a martingale difference: its conditional expectation given F sub t minus one is sigma t squared times the expected value of epsilon sub t squared minus one, which is zero. So its expected value is zero and its covariance with eta sub t minus j is zero for j at least one. It is not iid. Substitute sigma t minus i squared equal to a sub t minus i squared minus eta sub t minus i, for i from zero to s, into equation 20: a sub t squared minus eta sub t equals alpha zero plus the sum of alpha i a sub t minus i squared plus the sum of beta j times a sub t minus j squared minus eta sub t minus j.

Rearranged, equation 21: a sub t squared equals alpha zero plus the sum over i up to the larger of m and s of alpha i plus beta i, times a sub t minus i squared, plus eta sub t, minus the sum over j from one to s of beta j eta sub t minus j.

That is Tsay equation 3.15: an ARMA, with autoregressive order the larger of m and s and moving-average order s, for a sub t squared. The autoregressive coefficients are alpha i plus beta i; the moving-average coefficients are beta j. The ARMA mean formula of section 2 gives, equation 22: sigma infinity squared, the expected value of a sub t squared, equals alpha zero over one minus the sum of alpha i plus beta i.

Six point four point two. The GARCH of order one and one: kurtosis. Tsay section 3.16, printed pages 165 to 166.

Equation 23: sigma t squared equals alpha zero plus alpha one a sub t minus one squared plus beta one sigma t minus one squared, with alpha one and beta one between zero and one, and alpha one plus beta one below one.

Let the expected value of epsilon to the fourth be K plus 3; let v, the expected value of sigma t squared, be alpha zero over one minus alpha one minus beta one, by equation 22; and let s four be the expected value of sigma t to the fourth. Then the expected value of a sub t to the fourth is K plus 3 times s four. And, since sigma t minus one is known before epsilon t minus one is drawn, the expected value of sigma t minus one squared times a sub t minus one squared equals the expected value of sigma t minus one to the fourth times epsilon t minus one squared, which is s four.

Square equation 23: sigma t to the fourth equals alpha zero squared, plus alpha one squared a sub t minus one to the fourth, plus beta one squared sigma t minus one to the fourth, plus two alpha zero alpha one a sub t minus one squared, plus two alpha zero beta one sigma t minus one squared, plus two alpha one beta one sigma t minus one squared a sub t minus one squared.

Take expectations, with stationarity giving the expected values of sigma t to the fourth and sigma t minus one to the fourth both equal to s four: s four equals alpha zero squared, plus alpha one squared times K plus 3 times s four, plus beta one squared s four, plus two alpha zero times alpha one plus beta one times v, plus two alpha one beta one s four.

The s four coefficients add to K plus 3 times alpha one squared, plus beta one squared, plus two alpha one beta one, which is alpha one plus beta one squared, plus K plus 2 times alpha one squared. Write pi for alpha one plus beta one. Then s four times the bracket one minus pi squared minus K plus 2 times alpha one squared equals alpha zero squared plus two alpha zero pi times alpha zero over one minus pi, which is alpha zero squared times one plus pi, over one minus pi.

Equation 24: s four equals alpha zero squared times one plus pi, divided by one minus pi times the bracket one minus pi squared minus K plus 2 times alpha one squared.

The kurtosis of a sub t, dividing K plus 3 times s four by v squared, which is alpha zero squared over one minus pi squared: the expected value of a sub t to the fourth over the squared expected value of a sub t squared equals K plus 3 times one plus pi times one minus pi, over one minus pi squared minus K plus 2 times alpha one squared; which is K plus 3 times one minus pi squared, over one minus pi squared minus K plus 2 times alpha one squared.

With Gaussian epsilon, K equal to zero, the excess kurtosis is, equation 25: three times one minus pi squared, over one minus pi squared minus two alpha one squared, minus 3; which is six alpha one squared over one minus two alpha one squared minus alpha one plus beta one, squared.

It is finite if and only if one minus two alpha one squared minus alpha one plus beta one squared is positive, Tsay printed page 132. Alpha one, not beta one, creates the fat tails: with alpha one equal to zero the excess kurtosis is zero. With beta one equal to zero, equation 25 collapses to equation 12: six alpha one squared over one minus three alpha one squared is the excess of three times one minus alpha one squared over one minus three alpha one squared.

Six point four point three. The GARCH of order one and one: forecasts. Tsay printed page 133.

Six point six. ARCH in a regression. Wooldridge section 12.6, printed pages 391 to 396.

Wooldridge's angle is the regression error. Heteroskedasticity in a time-series regression leaves ordinary least squares unbiased and consistent but invalidates the usual standard errors. Heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors work as in cross-sections, provided there is no serial correlation, so test for that first. The Breusch-Pagan regression, Wooldridge equation 12.46, u sub t squared equals delta zero plus delta one x sub t one plus, and so on, plus delta k x sub t k plus v sub t, needs homoskedastic, serially uncorrelated v sub t.

Worked: Wooldridge Example 12.8, NYSE weekly returns, n equal to 689. The efficient-markets regression of the return on its own lag gives a t ratio of 1.55 on the lag: no predictability in the mean. The ARCH of order one for errors, Wooldridge equation 12.49: the expected value of u sub t squared given its past equals a zero plus a one u sub t minus one squared. That is the conditional variance only if the expected value of u sub t given its past is zero. Written as equation 34, Wooldridge equation 12.50: u sub t squared equals a zero plus a one u sub t minus one squared plus v sub t, with the expected value of v sub t given the past equal to zero. It is a first-order autoregression in u sub t squared, stable for a one below one: equation 13 with m equal to one. Under ARCH errors with otherwise Gauss-Markov assumptions, ordinary least squares is still the best linear unbiased estimator and the usual statistics are asymptotically valid. ARCH still matters for two reasons: weighted least squares or maximum likelihood based on equation 34 gives asymptotically more efficient estimators, and volatility itself is the object of interest in finance.

Part three, section 7. The GARCH family: leverage, log-variance, risk premia and fat-tailed innovations.

This section covers the site page garchmodeling: the Taylor-Schwert GARCH, threshold GARCH, GJR GARCH, APARCH and its special cases, EGARCH through the rugarch package, and the GARCH of order one and one with Student t, skewed t, t with nu equal to 7, and Laplace or generalized error distribution innovations, all on Petrobras 2000 to 2021, n equal to 5167. The claim, before the derivations. GARCH treats a positive shock and a negative shock of the same size alike. Asymmetric models give bad news a bigger coefficient. GJR and threshold GARCH do it with an indicator; APARCH with the absolute value of a minus gamma times a, raised to the power delta; EGARCH with a signed term in the log of sigma t squared. What the site's output reports is not always what its formula says: fGarch's leverage option fits the APARCH form, so the printed gamma one of 0.208 for Petrobras means bad-news and good-news coefficients of 0.135 and 0.058, a ratio of 2.33, not "alpha plus gamma against alpha". GARCH in mean puts sigma t squared in the mean equation, as a risk premium. Fat-tailed innovations matter more than any asymmetry: the Student t fit adds 141 log-likelihood points to Gaussian GARCH, against 22 for GJR. Tiao's formula splits the total kurtosis into the part the innovations bring and the part the volatility dynamics bring.

The notation, as in section 6: a sub t equals sigma sub t epsilon sub t, with epsilon sub t iid, mean zero and variance one; and sigma infinity squared the unconditional variance. In addition: omega is the site's constant, Tsay's alpha zero. Gamma is the asymmetry parameter, whose meaning depends on the model. Delta is the APARCH power, positive. N sub t minus one is the indicator that a sub t minus one is negative, Tsay equation 3.34, equal to zero or one. g of epsilon is EGARCH's weighted innovation. Theta is EGARCH's sign coefficient, rugarch's alpha one. Lowercase c is the GARCH-in-mean risk-premium parameter. K is the excess kurtosis of epsilon sub t, equal to 6 over nu minus 4 for the Student t. K sub a is the excess kurtosis of a sub t, and K sub a with a superscript g is its value with Gaussian epsilon. Nu is the Student t degrees of freedom, above 2, and xi is the skew parameter, positive.

Part three, section 8. Stochastic volatility: a second shock for the variance, its moments, and why it needs Markov chain Monte Carlo.

This section covers the site page sp500-gaussian-student-SV-models: the S and P 500 from May 2017 to May 2022, iid Gaussian against iid Student t against the four stochastic volatility models, with the stochvol package's default priors. It also covers the site page GARCH11-SVar1, sections 5 to 5.2: Petrobras standardized returns, the stochastic volatility model with a first-order autoregression and Student t errors against the GARCH of order one and one with Student t; the site page garch-sv-petrobras, section 5, the svsample call on simple returns; Tsay section 3.12, equation 3.40, and the Jacquier, Polson and Rossi moments, printed pages 153 to 154; section 3.13, equation 3.41, printed page 154; and Tsay section 12.7.1, equations 12.20 to 12.27, and Example 12.3, printed pages 636 to 643.

The claim, before the derivations. A stochastic volatility model gives the log variance its own first-order autoregressive shock: the log of sigma t squared equals alpha zero plus alpha one times the log of sigma t minus one squared plus v sub t, instead of GARCH's exact function of past squared returns. With Gaussian shocks every moment is a lognormal moment: the expected value of a sub t squared is e to the mu h plus sigma h squared over two; the kurtosis is three times e to the sigma h squared, above 3; and the correlation of a sub t squared with a sub t minus i squared is e to the sigma h squared alpha one to the i, minus one, over three e to the sigma h squared minus one, which decays at rate alpha one, like GARCH's at rate alpha one plus beta one. The price is the likelihood: sigma sub t is not known given the past, so the likelihood is an n-dimensional integral over the volatility path. Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo treats the path as unknowns and samples it one h sub t at a time, from Tsay's equation 12.23, a density built from three factors. On Petrobras, stochastic volatility and GARCH share the same persistence, 0.984; stochastic volatility needs thinner-tailed errors, nu about 12 against 7, because its own volatility shock already supplies kurtosis.

The notation. Two sources give two meanings of h sub t. Tsay section 12.7 writes h sub t for the variance, sigma t squared; the site pages and the R package stochvol write h sub t for the log variance. The narration keeps both and says which at every use. r sub t and a sub t are the return and its shock, mean removed, in Tsay's notation; y sub t is the site's demeaned return. Epsilon sub t is the standardized return shock, iid standard normal, or Student t in the Student t model. Sigma t squared is the conditional variance of a sub t given the volatility. Alpha zero, alpha one and sigma v squared are Tsay's log-variance autoregression: intercept, persistence, and shock variance, with the absolute value of alpha one below one. v sub t is the log-variance shock, iid normal with mean zero and variance sigma v squared, independent of epsilon sub t. Mu h and sigma h squared are the stationary mean and variance of the log of sigma t squared. In stochvol, h sub t is the log variance, and mu, phi and sigma are its level, persistence and shock standard deviation. Rho is the correlation between epsilon sub t and u sub t, the leverage parameter, between minus one and one. Nu is the Student t degrees of freedom, above 2. Capital H, the vector h one to h n, is the volatility path, treated as unknowns. And theta collects the static parameters.

Eight point one. Intuition layer: what stochastic volatility adds to GARCH.

Tsay sorts volatility models into two families, Tsay section 3.2, printed page 113. In the first, "an exact function" governs sigma t squared. That is GARCH: given F sub t minus one, sigma t squared, equal to alpha zero plus alpha one a sub t minus one squared plus beta one sigma t minus one squared, is a known number. In the second, "a stochastic equation" describes it. That is stochastic volatility, where sigma t squared has a shock of its own, unknown even after seeing every past return.

Compare the GARCH of order one and one with the stochastic volatility model on six dimensions. First, what drives volatility: past observations for GARCH, which is observation-driven; its own shock v sub t for stochastic volatility, which is parameter-driven. Second, shocks per observation: one for GARCH, epsilon sub t; two for stochastic volatility, epsilon sub t and v sub t. Third, sigma t squared given F sub t minus one: known for GARCH, random for stochastic volatility. Fourth, positivity: GARCH needs alpha zero positive and alpha one and beta one non-negative; for stochastic volatility it is automatic, through the logs. Fifth, the likelihood: for GARCH, a product of one-step densities, exact; for stochastic volatility, an n-dimensional integral, subsection 5. Sixth, estimation: maximum likelihood for GARCH, section 6; for stochastic volatility, quasi-likelihood through the Kalman filter, or Markov chain Monte Carlo, subsection 6.

"Adding the innovation v sub t substantially increases the flexibility of the model in describing the evolution of sigma t squared, but it also increases the difficulty in parameter estimation", Tsay printed page 153.

Leverage. With rho negative, a negative return shock epsilon sub t comes with a positive u sub t on average, so h sub t plus one rises after bad news. Eight point four. The moments of a sub t: kurtosis and the autocorrelation of a sub t squared. Tsay printed page 154, derived.

The one tool is the lognormal moment. If X is normal with mean m and variance s squared, then the expected value of e to the k X is e to the k m plus k squared s squared over two: the normal moment-generating function at k.

The second moment. Epsilon sub t is independent of sigma sub t, and the expected value of epsilon sub t squared is one. So the expected value of a sub t squared equals the expected value of sigma t squared times the expected value of epsilon sub t squared, which is the expected value of e to the log of sigma t squared. Apply the lognormal moment with k equal to one, m equal to mu h and s squared equal to sigma h squared, from equation 3. Equation 5: the expected value of a sub t squared equals the exponential of mu h plus sigma h squared over two.

The fourth moment. The expected value of epsilon sub t to the fourth is 3 for a standard normal. So the expected value of a sub t to the fourth equals the expected value of sigma t to the fourth times 3, which is three times the expected value of e to the two times the log of sigma t squared. Apply the lognormal moment with k equal to two. Equation 6: the expected value of a sub t to the fourth equals three times the exponential of two mu h plus two sigma h squared.

Erratum, Tsay printed page 154. The book prints the expected value of a sub t to the fourth as three times the exponential of two mu h squared plus two sigma h squared. The square on mu h is a typo: by equation 6 the exponent is linear in mu h, and only the linear version makes the kurtosis below free of mu h, as a ratio of scale-free moments must be.

The kurtosis. Divide equation 6 by the square of equation 5. Equation 7: the expected value of a sub t to the fourth over the squared expected value of a sub t squared equals three times the exponential of two mu h plus two sigma h squared, over the exponential of two mu h plus sigma h squared, which is three times e to the sigma h squared, greater than 3.

Stochastic volatility has fat tails even with Gaussian epsilon. A normal with a random variance is a scale mixture of normals, and every such mixture has kurtosis above 3. The excess is three times e to the sigma h squared minus one: it grows with the variability of the log variance, not with its level.

With Student t shocks of kurtosis kappa epsilon, the expected value of epsilon to the fourth, the same steps give kurtosis kappa epsilon times e to the sigma h squared. For a standardized Student t, kappa epsilon equals three times nu minus two, over nu minus four, for nu above 4, from Tsay section 3.16, printed page 166, where the excess is 6 over nu minus 4.

The autocorrelation of a sub t squared. For i at least one, epsilon sub t and epsilon sub t minus i are independent of each other and of the volatility path. So the expected value of a sub t squared times a sub t minus i squared equals the expected value of sigma t squared times sigma t minus i squared, times the expected values of epsilon sub t squared and epsilon sub t minus i squared, which is the expected value of e to the sum of the log of sigma t squared and the log of sigma t minus i squared.

The sum of the two logs is normal with mean two mu h and, by equations 3 and 4, variance sigma h squared plus sigma h squared plus two alpha one to the i sigma h squared. Equation 8: the expected value of a sub t squared times a sub t minus i squared equals the exponential of two mu h plus sigma h squared times one plus alpha one to the i.

Subtract the product of the means, the squared expected value of a sub t squared, which is the exponential of two mu h plus sigma h squared, from equation 5. The covariance of a sub t squared and a sub t minus i squared equals the exponential of two mu h plus sigma h squared, times e to the sigma h squared alpha one to the i, minus one.

The variance is equation 6 minus the same product: the variance of a sub t squared equals three times the exponential of two mu h plus two sigma h squared, minus the exponential of two mu h plus sigma h squared, which is the exponential of two mu h plus sigma h squared, times three e to the sigma h squared minus one.

The ratio cancels the exponential of two mu h plus sigma h squared. Equation 9, boxed: the correlation of a sub t squared with a sub t minus i squared equals e to the sigma h squared alpha one to the i, minus one, over three e to the sigma h squared minus one.

That is Tsay's formula on printed page 154. For small sigma h squared alpha one to the i, e to the x minus one is approximately x, so equation 9 is approximately sigma h squared alpha one to the i over three e to the sigma h squared minus one: geometric decay at rate alpha one, the same shape as the GARCH of order one and one's autocorrelation, rho k equal to rho one times alpha one plus beta one to the k minus one, section 6. With Student t shocks, replace the 3 in the denominator by kappa epsilon.

Eight point five. Why the likelihood is hard. Tsay section 12.7, printed page 637.

Write capital R for the vector of returns r one to r n, and capital H for the latent path h one to h n, with Tsay's h sub t equal to sigma t squared. Given H, the returns are independent normals. Given theta, H is a Gaussian first-order autoregression in logs. The likelihood of theta alone integrates the path out. Equation 10: the density of R given theta equals the integral of the density of R given H and theta, times the density of H given theta, over H.

That is "a mixture over the n-dimensional H distribution", Tsay printed page 637. For daily data n is in the thousands, and there is no closed form, so equation 10 cannot be maximised as the GARCH likelihood is.

Two ways out.

First, quasi-likelihood by linearisation, named on Tsay printed page 153, from Harvey, Ruiz and Shephard, 1994; a graduate item. Square and take logs of a sub t equals sigma sub t epsilon sub t. Equation 11: the log of a sub t squared equals the log of sigma t squared plus the log of epsilon sub t squared. The log of epsilon sub t squared is the log of a chi-squared with one degree of freedom, with mean digamma of one half plus the log of two, which is minus 1.2704, and variance pi squared over two, which is 4.935. So equation 11 plus equation 1 is a linear state-space model: an observed series equal to a first-order autoregressive state plus non-Gaussian noise. The Kalman filter treats the noise as Gaussian and gives a quasi-likelihood. It is consistent but inefficient, because the log chi-squared is far from normal, with a long left tail, and it fails when some a sub t is zero. Tsay's long-memory stochastic volatility, equation 3.41, uses the same decomposition, printed page 154.

Second, Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo, Jacquier, Polson and Rossi, 1994, and Tsay section 12.7: put H among the unknowns and sample theta and H jointly from the posterior, subsection 6. The integral of equation 10 is never computed: the sampler draws H and averages over the draws.

Eight point six point two. h sub t given h sub t minus one and h sub t plus one: three factors, Tsay equation 12.23.

Only three densities in the joint posterior contain h sub t, for t strictly between one and n: the density of a sub t, which has variance h sub t; the transition into the log of h sub t from the log of h sub t minus one; and the transition out of the log of h sub t to the log of h sub t plus one. Take the factors one at a time, writing x for the log of h sub t.

The first factor. a sub t given h sub t is normal with mean zero and variance h sub t, so its density is proportional to h sub t to the minus one half times the exponential of minus a sub t squared over two h sub t.

The second factor. The log of h sub t given the log of h sub t minus one is normal with mean alpha zero plus alpha one times the log of h sub t minus one, and variance sigma v squared, by equation 13. That is a density in x. As a density in h sub t it picks up the Jacobian, d x over d h sub t, which is h sub t to the minus one. So it is proportional to h sub t to the minus one times the exponential of minus x minus a, squared, over two sigma v squared, with a equal to alpha zero plus alpha one times the log of h sub t minus one.

The third factor. The log of h sub t plus one given the log of h sub t is normal with mean alpha zero plus alpha one x and variance sigma v squared. Its density is proportional to the exponential of minus the log of h sub t plus one minus alpha zero minus alpha one x, squared, over two sigma v squared, with no Jacobian in h sub t; that one belongs to h sub t plus one.

Rewrite the third exponent as a square in x. Factor out alpha one: the log of h sub t plus one minus alpha zero minus alpha one x, squared, equals alpha one squared times x minus b, squared, with b equal to the log of h sub t plus one minus alpha zero, over alpha one.

The two exponents then sum to minus the bracket, x minus a squared times A, plus x minus b squared times C, over two sigma v squared, with A equal to one and C equal to alpha one squared. Combine the two squares, Tsay printed page 638, a scalar version of Box and Tiao's Lemma 1. Expand: x minus a squared times A plus x minus b squared times C equals A plus C times x squared, minus two times A a plus C b, times x, plus A a squared plus C b squared. Complete the square with c equal to A a plus C b, over A plus C: the expression equals A plus C times x minus c squared, plus A a squared plus C b squared minus A plus C times c squared. And the constant simplifies: A a squared plus C b squared minus A a plus C b squared over A plus C equals the bracket A plus C times A a squared plus C b squared, minus A a plus C b squared, all over A plus C, which is A C times a minus b squared, over A plus C.

So equation 17: x minus a squared times A, plus x minus b squared times C, equals x minus c squared times A plus C, plus a minus b squared times A C over A plus C.

The last term does not contain h sub t and drops into the proportionality constant. Substitute A equal to one, a equal to alpha zero plus alpha one times the log of h sub t minus one, C equal to alpha one squared, and b equal to the log of h sub t plus one minus alpha zero, over alpha one, into c. Then c equals the bracket alpha zero plus alpha one log h sub t minus one, plus alpha one squared times b, over one plus alpha one squared, which is alpha zero plus alpha one log h sub t minus one plus alpha one log h sub t plus one minus alpha one alpha zero, all over one plus alpha one squared.

Equation 18: mu sub t, which is c, equals alpha zero times one minus alpha one, plus alpha one times the sum of the log of h sub t plus one and the log of h sub t minus one, all over one plus alpha one squared; and sigma squared equals sigma v squared over A plus C, which is sigma v squared over one plus alpha one squared.

Multiplying the three factors, h sub t to the minus one half times h sub t to the minus one is h sub t to the minus 1.5. Equation 19: the conditional density of h sub t is proportional to h sub t to the minus 1.5 times the exponential of minus a sub t squared over two h sub t, minus the log of h sub t minus mu sub t, squared, over two sigma squared. That is Tsay equation 12.23.

Read equation 18. The transition terms alone say the log of h sub t is normal with mean mu sub t and variance sigma squared: a smoother between its two neighbours. Tsay, printed page 640, recovers it as the least-squares estimate of a missing log of h sub t in a first-order autoregression. The factor h sub t to the minus one half times e to the minus a sub t squared over two h sub t then pulls h sub t toward a sub t squared: a large return today raises today's volatility draw. Equation 19 is not a standard density. Tsay draws from it by Griddy Gibbs: evaluate it on a grid and sample from the discretised distribution function. Jacquier, Polson and Rossi use a Metropolis step.

The end points, Tsay printed page 639. For t equal to one, fix h one, or predict h zero backwards using time reversibility, the log of h zero from a two-step backcast. For t equal to n, use only the log of h sub n normal with mean alpha zero plus alpha one log h sub n minus one and variance sigma v squared, or forecast h sub n plus one.

Reading it. First, sigma two hat is a scale, not a standard deviation. A Student t with 2 degrees of freedom has infinite variance, so sigma two hat being below sigma one hat says nothing about which model sees less risk. Second, nu hat equal to 2 means the iid model needs tails heavy enough to have no finite variance. That is the iid model's way of absorbing the 2020 COVID cluster: an iid law can only fit a cluster of large returns by making large returns likely every day. Third, the page's quantile plot puts the empirical quantiles of the returns beside those of the normal with variance sigma one hat squared and of sigma two hat times a Student t with 2 degrees of freedom, at tail probabilities 0.01, 0.03, and so on up to 0.99. The comparison to read is in the outer quantiles, where a Gaussian with the sample variance has the least mass.

The stochastic volatility reading. Volatility clustering is serial dependence in the scale, not a property of the marginal. Once h sub t moves, each day's conditional law can be close to normal, and the marginal still has kurtosis three times e to the sigma h squared, by equation 7. That is why the page then fits SV, SVt, SVl and SVtl and compares the four volatility paths with 0.1 times the absolute return.

Eight point eight. stochvol's default priors. The sp500 page, section 5.2; Kastner, 2016.

What each says. Mu: flat on the scale of log variances. Sigma squared, a graduate detail: stochvol's priorsigma setting, B sigma, means sigma squared is B sigma times a chi-squared with one degree of freedom, which is a gamma with shape one half and rate one over two B sigma; equivalently, sigma is the absolute value of a normal with mean zero and variance B sigma. Why nu differs, 7 against 12. Both models must reproduce the same sample kurtosis of the returns. In stochastic volatility the volatility shock alone supplies the factor e to the sigma h squared, 1.849, of equation 7. With nu equal to 12.13, the Student t shocks supply kappa epsilon equal to three times 12.13 minus two, over 12.13 minus four, which is three times 10.13 over 8.13, which is 3.738; and the total is 3.738 times 1.849, which is 6.91. So GARCH loads more of the kurtosis on epsilon sub t: nu equal to 7, and a total kurtosis of 11.87 by Tiao's formula, section 7.

Fat tails can come from the shock or from the volatility. Stochastic volatility splits them between two sources; GARCH mostly loads them on the shock. A fitted nu is not comparable across the two models.

Part three, section 9. Bayesian GARCH: conjugate warm-ups, a GARCH-t posterior, and what the posterior probability that alpha one plus beta exceeds one says.

This section covers the bayes-garch deck, by Lopes, May 2015, 22 slides: Petrobras log prices and returns 2000 to 2013, models M0 to M3, training-sample priors, Gibbs output, the bayesGARCH package for a GARCH of order one and one with Student t errors, the posterior probability 0.0034, and posterior quantiles of the volatility. It also covers the site page GARCH11-SVar1, sections 4 and 4.1, bayesGARCH against fGarch on standardized Petrobras returns 2000 to 2021; Tsay section 12.7, printed page 637, on augmenting latent variables and the Gibbs logic; and Tsay section 3.4, equation 3.7, printed page 121, the standardized Student t.

The claim, before the derivations. The deck builds up to Bayesian GARCH through three models that are conjugate: iid normal with mean zero, one inverse-gamma update; iid normal with unknown mean; and a first-order autoregression on log prices, both by Gibbs sampling with normal and inverse-gamma conditionals. Their priors are elicited from a training sample, 2001 to 2006, and the posterior uses 2007 to 2013. The GARCH of order one and one with Student t errors is not conjugate: h sub t depends on alpha zero, alpha one and beta through a recursion, so the posterior is sampled by Metropolis-Hastings steps, in bayesGARCH, Ardia and Hoogerheide, 2010. The payoff is probability statements about functions of the parameters: the posterior probability that alpha one plus beta exceeds one is 0.0034 on Petrobras, and there is a posterior band for every sigma sub t. On the 2000 to 2021 Petrobras series the posterior means put the persistence at 0.9791, against the maximum likelihood estimate's 0.9837, because a posterior mean averages along a curved likelihood ridge that the mode does not see.

The notation. The deck writes the GARCH variance as h sub t, Tsay's sigma t squared, not stochvol's log variance of section 8, and the GARCH coefficient as beta, Tsay's beta one. p sub t is the Petrobras price and y sub t, its log, the log price. r sub t, equal to y sub t minus y sub t minus one, is the log return. n zero is the training sample, 2001 to 2006, 1506 days; n is the posterior sample, 2007 to 2013, 1760 days. m r tilde and V r tilde are the training mean and variance of r sub t, and m r tilde squared is the training mean of r sub t squared. Lowercase a, b and s are the training ordinary least squares estimates of the first-order autoregression on log prices, and s a and s b their standard errors. IG of a and b is the inverse gamma, with density proportional to sigma squared to the minus a plus one, times e to the minus b over sigma squared, and mean b over a minus one. Mu and sigma squared are the mean and variance of the iid models. Alpha and beta in M2 are the intercept and slope of y sub t given y sub t minus one. Alpha zero, alpha one and beta in M3 are the GARCH constant, ARCH coefficient and GARCH coefficient. h sub t is the conditional variance of r sub t in M3. Nu is the Student t degrees of freedom, and varrho, equal to nu minus two over nu, is the scaling that makes the conditional variance of r sub t equal to h sub t. Lambda and delta are the rate and shift of the translated-exponential prior on nu. And varpi sub t is the auxiliary mixing variable of the Student t as a scale mixture of normals.

Nine point six. Why there is no conjugacy, and how bayesGARCH samples. Course layer, with one graduate item.

In M0 to M2 every conditional was normal or inverse gamma, because the parameters entered the likelihood linearly, in the mean, or as a single scale. In equation 12, h sub t equals alpha zero times the sum over j below t of beta to the j, plus alpha one times the sum over j below t of beta to the j times r sub t minus one minus j squared. Beta enters through powers, and every h sub t depends on all three coefficients. No prior makes the posterior of alpha zero, alpha one and beta a known family. Three routes.

First, random-walk Metropolis-Hastings, course layer. Propose theta star equal to the previous theta plus noise, and accept with probability the minimum of one and the ratio of the posterior at theta star to the posterior at the previous theta. The posterior is needed only up to its constant, that is, equation 14 times equation 15, one pass of the recursion per evaluation. The Python note runs this sampler on simulated GARCH data with Student t errors. The steps, as an order of operations: for each iteration, draw a proposal equal to the current parameters plus normal noise with a tuned scale times a covariance matrix; reject it at once if it violates alpha zero positive, alpha one non-negative, beta positive, or nu above delta; otherwise compute the log acceptance ratio as the log-likelihood plus log prior at the proposal minus the same at the current value; and accept the proposal with probability the minimum of one and the exponential of that log ratio.

Second, Griddy Gibbs, Bauwens and Lubrano, 1998: draw each coefficient from its conditional evaluated on a grid, as Tsay does for h sub t in section 8, subsection 6.2.

Third, Ardia and Hoogerheide, 2010, the method in bayesGARCH; a graduate item. Write the Student t as a scale mixture of normals. Equation 16: r sub t given varpi sub t and h sub t is normal with mean zero and variance varpi sub t times varrho times h sub t; and varpi sub t is inverse gamma with parameters nu over two and nu over two. Integrating varpi sub t out returns the Student t with scale squared varrho h sub t. Given the vector of varpi's, each varpi sub t has a conjugate inverse-gamma conditional, and alpha and beta are drawn by Metropolis-Hastings with proposals from an approximating generalized least squares regression on the ARMA form of GARCH: r sub t squared equals alpha zero plus alpha one plus beta times r sub t minus one squared, plus w sub t minus beta w sub t minus one, from section 6 and Nakatsuma, 2000. Nu is drawn by an optimised rejection step. This is Tsay section 12.7's device again: augment latent variables until each block is easy.

The run, slide 17: 10,000 burn-in draws discarded, 10,000 kept, one chain, on the returns of the first part of the data.

Nine point seven. Reading the output. Slides 18 to 21.

Traces and marginals, slides 18 and 19. The posterior draws span roughly alpha zero between 1 and 5 times ten to the minus five, alpha one between 0.04 and 0.16, beta between 0.82 and 0.94, and nu between 4 and 12. The autocorrelations of the draws decay slowly and reach zero only after roughly 100 to 150 lags: Metropolis-Hastings chains on GARCH are sticky, so 10,000 draws carry far fewer effective draws.

The persistence, slide 20. Each draw gives alpha one plus beta, and the histogram of these sums is the posterior of the persistence. The deck reports, equation 17: the posterior probability that alpha one plus beta exceeds one, estimated as one over M times the number of draws in which alpha one plus beta exceeds one, is 0.0034. That is 34 of the 10,000 kept draws. Its Monte Carlo standard error is at least the square root of 0.0034 times 0.9966 over 10,000, which is 0.00058, for iid draws, and more with autocorrelation.

This is a direct probability that the model is covariance stationary: 99.66 per cent. A frequentist test of the null alpha one plus beta equal to one gives a p-value, which is a probability about the data under the null, not about the hypothesis, as Class 1, section 8, showed. And because the prior of equation 15 was flat on both sides of the line, the 0.34 per cent is the data's verdict, not the prior's.

The volatility with uncertainty, slide 21. For each draw j, run the recursion of equation 12 to get h sub t for that draw, for t from one to n. The 2.5, 50 and 97.5 per cent quantiles, across draws, of the square root of h sub t at each t are a posterior band for sigma sub t. Why the means move away from the maximum likelihood estimate. With a flat prior, the maximum likelihood estimate is the posterior mode. The GARCH likelihood has a curved ridge: the data pin down the unconditional variance, alpha zero over one minus alpha one minus beta, and the persistence much better than each coefficient, so alpha zero and one minus alpha one minus beta trade off along a ridge, and the posterior is skewed along it. Averaging over a skewed posterior moves the mean off the mode: here toward a larger alpha zero, a larger alpha one and a smaller beta, together about one posterior standard deviation each, and toward lower persistence. Nu, whose posterior is nearly symmetric, barely moves, minus 0.07 standard deviations. Page section 4.1 overlays the two volatility paths, for the full sample and the last 500 days. Expect them to be close: the fitted sigma sub t depends on the coefficients mostly through the well-determined directions, persistence and unconditional variance, not along the ridge.

A reading note. The page's Bayes table labels the columns "t value" and "Pr greater than absolute t", computing the posterior mean over its standard deviation and two times one minus the normal distribution function at that ratio. Those are frequentist labels on posterior summaries; the Bayesian statement is a credible interval or a posterior probability such as equation 17.

Nine point nine. Intuition layer: what the Bayesian route buys here.

Compare maximum likelihood and Bayes on five questions. First, is the model covariance stationary? Maximum likelihood tests alpha one plus beta equal to one, on a boundary, with a nonstandard law; Bayes reports the posterior probability that alpha one plus beta is below one, 0.9966. Second, the uncertainty in sigma sub t, the half-life, or a value at risk: maximum likelihood uses the delta method, case by case; Bayes uses quantiles of draws, one rule for all. Third, prior information: none for maximum likelihood; the training sample for Bayes, in M0 to M2. Fourth, small samples and parameters near a boundary: asymptotic normality fails for maximum likelihood; Bayes gives the exact finite-sample posterior. Fifth, the cost: one optimisation for maximum likelihood; for Bayes, Markov chain Monte Carlo, sticky chains and diagnostics.

Recap of part 2. Returns are uncorrelated, but their squares are not: ARCH makes the conditional variance a function of past squared shocks and produces kurtosis above 3 from normal shocks, 4.227 for Intel. GARCH is an ARMA for a sub t squared, with long-run variance alpha zero over one minus alpha one minus beta one, forecasts that close the gap at rate alpha one plus beta one, and half-lives of 25 months for Tsay's S and P 500 and 34 days for Petrobras; IGARCH is its unit root, and RiskMetrics is exponential smoothing. Asymmetric models give bad news more weight, 2.33 times for Petrobras once fGarch's APARCH output is read correctly; but fat-tailed innovations add 141 log-likelihood points, against 22 for asymmetry, and Tiao's formula splits the kurtosis between innovations and dynamics. Stochastic volatility gives the log variance its own shock, with kurtosis three times e to the sigma h squared, a likelihood that is an n-dimensional integral, and a Gibbs sampler that draws the path one h sub t at a time. And Bayesian GARCH answers "is the model stationary?" with a posterior probability, 0.9966 for Petrobras, and puts the posterior means off the mode because of the likelihood ridge.
