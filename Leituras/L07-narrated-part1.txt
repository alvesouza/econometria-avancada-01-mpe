The one claim. Every model of this class is the normal linear regression with one twist, and the twist is handled by adding something to the Gibbs sampler.

A known covariance matrix, capital Omega, is a rotation: that is generalized least squares. A variance function needs one Metropolis step. Student t errors become normal once each observation carries its own latent variance. First-order autoregressive errors are one parameter, rho, and quasi-differencing.

Hierarchical models put a prior with an estimated mean and an estimated variance on unit-level coefficients, so each unit's estimate is a partial-pooling average of its own data and the population.

Limited dependent variables, the Tobit, the probit, and the ordered and multinomial probits, are regressions on a latent y star that is seen only through a cut. Draw y star from a truncated normal and it is ordinary least squares again. But the coefficients are effects on the latent scale, not effects on what is observed.

Part one, section 1. A general covariance matrix: known Omega is ordinary least squares after a rotation, and unknown Omega costs one Metropolis step.

This section covers GLM3 slides 3 to 15: the general covariance, known Omega, diagonal Omega, Case I with omega i equal to h of z i and alpha, the simulated example on slide 8, and the US airline cost data on slides 11 to 15. It also covers Wooldridge section 8.4, printed pages 254 to 262, equations 8.21 to 8.34, with Example 8.6 and Table 8.1 on printed page 257; and Greenberg section 8.1.1, printed pages 111 to 112, equations 8.1 to 8.3 and Algorithm 8.1. The numbers are re-run in the Python checks, section 1.

The claim, before the derivations. Take y equal to X beta plus epsilon, with epsilon normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared times Omega. Any matrix P with P times Omega times P transposed equal to the identity of size n turns the model into a standard regression of P y on P X. So generalized least squares is ordinary least squares on rotated data, and the Bayesian full conditionals are the familiar normal and inverse-gamma pair with X transposed X replaced by X transposed Omega inverse X. A diagonal Omega is the heteroskedastic case: generalized least squares becomes weighted least squares, with weights one over omega i. When omega i equal to h of z i and alpha is unknown, alpha has no conjugate conditional and is drawn by Metropolis-Hastings. The frequentist twin is feasible generalized least squares, whose first step, regressing the log of the squared residual on z i, is exactly the slides' starting value.

The notation. y is n by one; X is n by k with full column rank; beta is k by one; and epsilon is y minus X beta. Sigma squared, positive, is the scale. Omega, n by n, symmetric and positive definite, is the error correlation or variance pattern, with entries omega i j. P is any n by n matrix with P Omega P transposed equal to the identity; for example, P equal to L inverse, where Omega equals L L transposed is the Cholesky factorization. The prior, slide 4: beta is normal with mean beta zero and variance V zero, and sigma squared is inverse-gamma with parameters nu zero over two and nu zero s zero squared over two. h of z i and alpha, positive, is the variance function; z i, p by one, holds its covariates, and alpha its parameters, slide 7. In Wooldridge's notation, the variance of u given x is sigma squared times h of x, and h sub i is h of x i, equation 8.21.

One point one. Why the error covariance matters. The U layer.

Ordinary least squares stays unbiased when the variance of epsilon is sigma squared Omega, because unbiasedness never used the variance. But two things break. The usual variance formula, sigma squared times the inverse of X transposed X, is wrong. And ordinary least squares is no longer the most precise linear unbiased estimator. Observations with large variance, the noisy ones, carry less information about beta and should count less. Correlated observations repeat part of each other's information and should not be counted twice. Generalized least squares does both, by first transforming the data into something that has spherical errors.

One point two. The known-Omega case. Slides 3 to 5.

The model, slide 3. Equation 1: y equals X beta plus epsilon, with epsilon normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared Omega.

The rotation, slide 4. Take P with P Omega P transposed equal to the identity, and multiply equation 1 by P. Equation 2: P y equals P X beta plus P epsilon.

The new error has mean zero and a variance computed in four steps. Equation 3: the variance of P epsilon equals P times the variance of epsilon times P transposed; which is P times sigma squared Omega times P transposed; which is sigma squared times P Omega P transposed; which is sigma squared times the identity.

Write y star for P y, X star for P X, and epsilon star for P epsilon. Then equations 2 and 3 say that y star equals X star beta plus epsilon star, with epsilon star normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared times the identity. Given Omega, that is a standard linear regression, slide 4.

Generalized least squares is ordinary least squares on equation 2. Ordinary least squares on the starred data is the inverse of X star transposed X star, times X star transposed y star. Substitute X star equal to P X and y star equal to P y. Equation 4: beta hat GLS equals the inverse of X transposed P transposed P X, times X transposed P transposed P y.

From P Omega P transposed equal to the identity, Omega equals P inverse times the inverse of P transposed, so Omega inverse equals P transposed P. Substitute into equation 4. Equation 5: beta hat GLS equals the inverse of X transposed Omega inverse X, times X transposed Omega inverse y; and its variance is sigma squared times the inverse of X transposed Omega inverse X.

P itself never matters; only P transposed P, which is Omega inverse. This was checked numerically in the Python checks, section 1: ordinary least squares on P y and P X equals equation 5 to machine precision.

The full conditionals, slide 5. The likelihood of equation 1, equation 6: the density of y given beta and sigma squared is proportional to sigma squared to the minus n over two, times the exponential of minus one over two sigma squared, times y minus X beta transposed, times Omega inverse, times y minus X beta.

Sigma squared given beta. Multiply equation 6 by the inverse-gamma kernel with parameters nu zero over two and nu zero s zero squared over two, which is sigma squared to the minus nu zero over two minus one, times the exponential of minus nu zero s zero squared over two sigma squared. Collect powers and exponents. The conditional density of sigma squared is proportional to sigma squared to the minus nu zero plus n over two, minus one, times the exponential of minus the sum of nu zero s zero squared and the quadratic form y minus X beta transposed Omega inverse y minus X beta, all over two sigma squared. Beta given sigma squared. Keep the terms of equation 6 times the prior that involve beta: minus one half times the bracket of sigma to the minus two times the quadratic form in Omega inverse, plus beta minus beta zero transposed V zero inverse beta minus beta zero. Expand and keep the quadratic and the linear terms in beta: minus one half times the bracket of beta transposed, times V zero inverse plus sigma to the minus two X transposed Omega inverse X, times beta, minus two beta transposed times V zero inverse beta zero plus sigma to the minus two X transposed Omega inverse y; plus a constant. Completing the square gives equation 8: beta given sigma squared, Omega and y is normal with mean beta one and variance V one, where V one inverse equals V zero inverse plus sigma to the minus two X transposed Omega inverse X, and V one inverse times beta one equals V zero inverse beta zero plus sigma to the minus two X transposed Omega inverse y.

Case I against Case II, slide 6. The slides split diagonal Omega into two cases. Case I: omega i equal to h of z i and alpha, a deterministic function of covariates; that is this section. Case II: omega i random with an inverse-gamma law; that is the Student t model of section 2.

One point four. Case I: omega i equal to h of z i and alpha, and why alpha needs Metropolis-Hastings. Slides 7 and 8.

Variance functions, slide 7. h of z i and alpha can be the square of alpha one z i one plus and so on up to alpha p z i p; or, more commonly, the exponential of alpha one z i one plus and so on up to alpha p z i p. Wooldridge's equation 8.30, printed page 259, is the same exponential form.

The conditional of alpha. Given beta and sigma squared, and writing e i for y i minus x i transposed beta, equation 6 becomes equation 11: the conditional density of alpha is proportional to the prior of alpha times the product over i of h of z i and alpha to the minus one half, times the exponential of minus e i squared over two sigma squared h of z i and alpha.

For h equal to the exponential of z i transposed alpha, substitute and take logs. Equation 12: the log conditional of alpha equals the log prior of alpha, minus one half times the sum over i of z i transposed alpha, minus one over two sigma squared times the sum over i of e i squared times e to the minus z i transposed alpha, plus a constant.

Part one, section 2. Student t errors: the t is a normal with a random variance, and that makes it Gibbs-friendly.

This section covers GLM3 slides 16 to 21: Case II, with omega i inverse-gamma with parameters nu over two and nu over two; the prior of nu; the full conditionals; and the simulated example. It also covers Greenberg section 4.5, printed pages 50 to 52, on exchangeability, equations 4.8 and 4.9, and the marginalization to a t; section 4.6, printed pages 52 to 53, on a hierarchical nu; and section 8.1.2, printed pages 114 to 116, with equation 8.4, the Gibbs conditionals and the Vella and Verbeek results. The numbers are re-run in the Python checks, section 2.

The claim, before the derivations. If epsilon i given omega i is normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared omega i, and omega i is inverse-gamma with parameters nu over two and nu over two, then marginally epsilon i is a t with nu degrees of freedom, location zero and scale sigma squared. The t is a scale mixture of normals. Keeping the omega i in the sampler turns a non-conjugate t regression into the heteroskedastic regression of section 1, with conjugate inverse-gamma omega i. Each observation gets the weight equal to the conditional expected value of omega i inverse, which is nu plus one over nu plus epsilon i squared over sigma squared. That weight is below one exactly when the absolute value of epsilon i exceeds sigma: outliers are downweighted automatically. The degrees of freedom nu get an exponential prior and a Metropolis step.

The notation. Omega i, positive, is the observation-specific variance multiplier, slides 16 to 18; lambda i, equal to omega i inverse, is its precision, which Greenberg writes as lambda i in equation 8.4. Nu, positive, is the degrees of freedom; nu with a superscript zero, which the narration calls nu naught, is the prior mean of nu, slide 17; and sigma squared is the scale, not the variance. The inverse-gamma with parameters a and b has density b to the a over gamma of a, times w to the minus a minus one, times e to the minus b over w. The gamma with parameters a and b has density b to the a over gamma of a, times lambda to the a minus one, times e to the minus b lambda, so b is a rate. Omega is inverse-gamma with parameters a and b if and only if omega inverse is gamma with parameters a and b. The t with nu degrees of freedom, location zero and scale sigma squared has density proportional to one plus e squared over nu sigma squared, raised to the minus nu plus one over two; its variance is sigma squared times nu over nu minus two, for nu above two.

Two point one. Fat tails as "some observations are noisier". The U layer.

A Gaussian likelihood treats a residual of 5 sigma as nearly impossible: phi of 5 over phi of 0 is e to the minus 12.5. So ordinary least squares bends the line toward it. A t likelihood says such residuals happen. The mixture reading gives the mechanism: every observation draws its own variance, sigma squared omega i, and a few draw large ones. After seeing the data, an observation with a huge residual is inferred to have had a large omega i, and it counts little in the beta update. Greenberg, section 4.5, printed page 51, motivates the prior on omega i, his lambda i, by exchangeability: each lambda i belongs to observation i, but nothing tells us in advance which observations are the noisy ones, so they are drawn independently and identically from one distribution.

Two point two. The scale-mixture identity. Slide 16; Greenberg printed page 52.

The claim to prove: integrating the latent variance out of a normal gives a t. Three steps.

Step one, the joint density of epsilon and omega given sigma squared, from the normal with variance sigma squared omega times the inverse-gamma with parameters nu over two and nu over two. Equation 1: f of epsilon and omega given sigma squared equals one over the square root of two pi sigma squared omega, times e to the minus epsilon squared over two sigma squared omega, times nu over two to the nu over two, over gamma of nu over two, times omega to the minus nu over two minus one, times e to the minus nu over two omega.

Step two, collect the powers of omega and the exponents. Equation 2: f of epsilon and omega given sigma squared is proportional to omega to the minus nu plus one over two, minus one, times the exponential of minus one over omega times nu plus epsilon squared over sigma squared, all over two.

As a function of omega, equation 2 is an inverse-gamma kernel with shape nu plus one over two and scale nu plus epsilon squared over sigma squared, over two.

Step three, integrate over omega. The integral of an inverse-gamma kernel is gamma of a over b to the a, with a equal to nu plus one over two and b equal to nu plus epsilon squared over sigma squared, over two. So, equation 3: f of epsilon given sigma squared, the integral from zero to infinity of the joint density over omega, is proportional to nu plus epsilon squared over sigma squared, over two, raised to the minus nu plus one over two; which is proportional to one plus epsilon squared over nu sigma squared, raised to the minus nu plus one over two. That is the kernel of the t with nu degrees of freedom and scale sigma squared. End of proof. It was checked by quadrature at 20 pairs of nu and epsilon in the Python checks, section 2.

Conditionally heteroskedastic, marginally homoskedastic, in Greenberg's words on printed page 52. The variance of epsilon i given omega i, sigma squared omega i, differs across i, but every epsilon i has the same marginal t distribution.

Two point four. The prior of nu, and Geweke's warning. Slide 17.

Nu is gamma with parameters one and one over nu naught, which is the exponential with mean nu naught, so the expected value of nu and its standard deviation both equal nu naught. With nu naught equal to 25: the probability that nu is at most 10 is one minus e to the minus 10 over 25, which is one minus 0.6703, which is 0.3297. The probability that nu is at least 40 is e to the minus 40 over 25, which is 0.2019.

One third of the prior mass sits on fat tails, and one fifth on "practically normal": the t is close to the normal in its centre once nu is around 30 or more, Greenberg printed page 53. That is what "substantial prior on both" means on slide 17.

Geweke, 1993, slide 17. With a flat prior on beta, the posterior mean of beta does not exist unless the prior probability that nu lies between zero and two is zero, and the posterior variance of beta does not exist unless the prior probability that nu lies between zero and four is zero. The t's own moments exist only for nu above one, for the mean, and above two, for the variance; under a flat prior on beta the posterior inherits that tail problem. Medians and quantiles still exist: report them, or use a proper prior on beta.

Part one, section 3. First-order autoregressive errors: one parameter, rho, fills the whole n by n Omega, and generalized least squares is quasi-differencing.

The claim, before the derivations. First-order autoregressive errors, epsilon t equal to rho epsilon t minus one plus u t, give a correlation between epsilon t and epsilon t plus k equal to rho to the k: a full n by n covariance pinned down by one parameter. The inverse of Omega rho is tridiagonal, so generalized least squares is ordinary least squares on quasi-differenced data, y t minus rho y t minus one. Prais-Winsten keeps the first row, rescaled; Cochrane-Orcutt drops it. In the Gibbs sampler, beta and sigma squared keep the section 1 conditionals with Omega rho. Rho is drawn either by Metropolis-Hastings on its exact conditional or, after "a small sin", conditioning on the first error, exactly from a truncated normal, by the inverse-distribution-function formula of slide 26. How much ordinary least squares loses, and how wrong its standard errors are, depends on rho times the persistence of the regressor.

The notation. y t equals x t transposed beta plus epsilon t, for t from one to n; epsilon t equals rho epsilon t minus one plus u t, with u t independent and identically normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared, and the absolute value of rho below one, slide 22. Wooldridge writes u t equal to rho u t minus one plus e t, equation 12.26, and sigma e squared for our sigma squared. Omega rho is the n by n matrix with entries rho to the absolute value of i minus j, over one minus rho squared, so the variance of epsilon is sigma squared Omega rho, slide 23. y tilde t, equal to y t minus rho y t minus one, and x tilde t, equal to x t minus rho x t minus one, are the quasi-differences, Wooldridge equation 12.29. In subsection 3 only, x t is a stationary first-order autoregression with coefficient phi and variance sigma x squared.

Three point one. The covariance of first-order autoregressive errors. Slide 22.

Take variances in epsilon t equal to rho epsilon t minus one plus u t, with u t independent of epsilon t minus one, and with stationarity, so the variances of epsilon t and epsilon t minus one are both gamma zero. Then gamma zero equals rho squared gamma zero plus sigma squared, and so, equation 1: gamma zero equals sigma squared over one minus rho squared. That is Wooldridge's equation 12.27.

Substitute the recursion k times: epsilon t plus k equals rho to the k times epsilon t, plus the sum, for j from zero to k minus one, of rho to the j times u t plus k minus j. The sum is independent of epsilon t. So, equation 2: the covariance of epsilon t and epsilon t plus k equals rho to the k times gamma zero, which is rho to the k times sigma squared over one minus rho squared; and the expected value of epsilon t is zero.

Stacking equation 2 gives epsilon, given sigma squared and rho, normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared Omega rho, where the i j entry of Omega rho is rho to the absolute value of i minus j, over one minus rho squared, slide 23. Three point two. Generalized least squares by quasi-differencing. Wooldridge section 12.3a, printed pages 382 to 383.

That is Wooldridge's equation 12.31. And equation 6: y t minus rho y t minus one equals x t minus rho x t minus one, transposed, times beta, plus u t, for t at least two. That is Wooldridge's equations 12.28 and 12.32.

The errors are now white. u t equals epsilon t minus rho epsilon t minus one, for t at least two; and by equation 1, the variance of the square root of one minus rho squared times epsilon one is one minus rho squared times sigma squared over one minus rho squared, which is sigma squared. Ordinary least squares on equations 5 and 6 is the best linear unbiased estimator, Wooldridge printed page 383. With an intercept, the constant column becomes the square root of one minus rho squared in row one, and one minus rho in rows t at least two, Wooldridge equation 12.33.

Feasible generalized least squares, Wooldridge section 12.3b, printed page 383. Three steps. First, the ordinary least squares residuals u hat t. Second, rho hat from regressing u hat t on u hat t minus one, Wooldridge equation 12.14. Third, ordinary least squares on equations 5 and 6 with rho hat. Prais-Winsten keeps equation 5; Cochrane-Orcutt drops it. Both are usually iterated until rho hat converges. Asymptotically they are the same; in a small sample, the first row can matter.

Worked: Wooldridge's Example 12.4, Table 12.1, printed page 384, the BARIUM data, n equal to 131. Three point three. How much ordinary least squares loses, in closed form. This subsection is part course and part G layer; the map lists equations 10 and 11 as proved but not examinable.

A single demeaned regressor: x t is a first-order autoregression with coefficient phi and variance sigma x squared, independent of the errors. For ordinary least squares, beta hat minus beta equals the sum over t of x t epsilon t, divided by the sum over t of x t squared, and the sum of x t squared is approximately n sigma x squared.

The numerator's variance, equation 7: the variance of the sum over t of x t epsilon t equals the double sum over s and t of the expected value of x s x t times the expected value of epsilon s epsilon t, which is the double sum over s and t of sigma x squared phi to the absolute value of s minus t, times gamma zero rho to the absolute value of s minus t.

For large n, the double sum over s and t of rho phi to the absolute value of s minus t is approximately n times the bracket one plus twice the sum over k from one onward of rho phi to the k, which is n times one plus rho phi over one minus rho phi. Substitute into equation 7 and divide by n sigma x squared, squared. Equation 8: the variance of the ordinary least squares estimator is approximately gamma zero over n sigma x squared, times one plus rho phi over one minus rho phi.

The naive formula, gamma zero hat over the sum of x t squared, which is approximately gamma zero over n sigma x squared, drops the last factor.

Generalized least squares is ordinary least squares on equation 6. x tilde t, equal to x t minus rho x t minus one, has variance sigma x squared times one plus rho squared minus two rho phi, and the error u t has variance sigma squared, which is gamma zero times one minus rho squared by equation 1. So, equation 9: the variance of the generalized least squares estimator is approximately gamma zero times one minus rho squared, over n sigma x squared times one plus rho squared minus two rho phi.

Divide equation 8 by the naive formula, and equation 8 by equation 9. Equation 10: the true ordinary least squares variance over the naive ordinary least squares variance equals one plus rho phi over one minus rho phi. Equation 11: the ordinary least squares variance over the generalized least squares variance equals one plus rho phi over one minus rho phi, times one plus rho squared minus two rho phi over one minus rho squared.

The numbers at rho equal to 0.9. With phi equal to 0.9, equation 10 is 1.81 over 0.19, which is 9.53. With phi equal to zero, equation 10 is one, but equation 11 is one plus 0.81 over one minus 0.81, which is 9.53. Both are asserted in the figure script. The reading: the bias of the usual standard error comes from the product rho phi, with both series persistent in the same direction, the typical macro case; and the efficiency loss exists even when the standard error is right.

Three point four. The conditional of rho. Slides 24 to 26.

Exact, slide 24. With the prior rho normal with mean rho zero and variance V rho, truncated to the interval from minus one to one, and with epsilon equal to y minus X beta: equation 12, the conditional density of rho given epsilon and sigma squared is proportional to the determinant of Omega rho to the minus one half, times the exponential of minus one over two sigma squared times epsilon transposed Omega rho inverse epsilon, times the exponential of minus rho minus rho zero, squared, over two V rho, times the indicator that the absolute value of rho is below one.

The slip on slide 24: its exponent reads minus 0.5 times epsilon transposed Omega rho inverse epsilon, without the sigma to the minus two. The factor is needed, since the variance of epsilon is sigma squared Omega rho. The prior term is written there as rho squared minus two rho rho zero, which is the same up to a constant. Because the determinant of Omega rho is one over one minus rho squared, and equation 3 is quadratic in rho except for its corners, equation 12 is not a standard density: it needs a Metropolis-Hastings step.

"A small sin", slide 25. Treat epsilon one as given, and drop its factor. Then the likelihood is that of the regression epsilon t equal to rho epsilon t minus one plus u t, for t from two to n. Write epsilon tilde for the vector epsilon one up to epsilon n minus one, and epsilon for the vector epsilon two up to epsilon n. The exponent in rho is minus one half times the bracket of epsilon minus rho epsilon tilde, transposed, times epsilon minus rho epsilon tilde, over sigma squared, plus rho minus rho zero, squared, over V rho. Expanded, it is minus one half times the bracket of rho squared times epsilon tilde transposed epsilon tilde over sigma squared plus one over V rho, minus two rho times epsilon tilde transposed epsilon over sigma squared plus rho zero over V rho; plus a constant.

That is a normal kernel restricted to the interval from minus one to one. Equation 13: rho given y, X, beta and sigma squared is normal truncated to the interval from minus one to one, with mean rho one and variance V rho bar, where V rho bar is the inverse of V rho inverse plus sigma to the minus two times epsilon tilde transposed epsilon tilde, and rho one is V rho bar times the sum of V rho inverse rho zero and sigma to the minus two epsilon tilde transposed epsilon.

The "sin" costs one observation's information out of n. It is Wooldridge's Cochrane-Orcutt choice, now in a Bayesian sampler.

Drawing from a normal with mean mu and variance sigma squared truncated to the interval from a to b, slide 26. For y in that interval the distribution function is, equation 14: u, the probability that Y is at most y, equals capital Phi of y minus mu over sigma, minus A, all over B minus A; where A is capital Phi of a minus mu over sigma, and B is capital Phi of b minus mu over sigma.

Solve equation 14 for y. Equation 15: y equals mu plus sigma times capital Phi inverse of u B plus one minus u times A, with u uniform on zero to one.

Equation 15 is exact; it is the inverse-distribution-function method. The Python checks, section 3, confirm that it equals scipy's truncated-normal quantile function on 999 quantiles. One practical caveat: if the interval lies deep in the upper tail, A and B both round to one. Then reflect, sending y to minus y, and work in the lower tail. The truncated-normal helper in the Python file does this, and it is the sampler reused by the Tobit and probit sections.

Recap of part 1. Every model in part one of the notes is the normal regression with a non-spherical Omega, and the same conditional for beta and sigma squared runs with Omega inverse in place of the identity. A known Omega is a rotation: generalized least squares is ordinary least squares on P y and P X, and a diagonal Omega is weighted least squares, 0.787 with standard error 0.063 against 0.821 with standard error 0.104 in Wooldridge's Example 8.6. A variance function costs one Metropolis-Hastings step for alpha; ignoring it costs ordinary least squares a factor of 4.38 at alpha one equal to one, and on the airline data a rise of 0.1 in the load factor doubles the variance. Student t errors are a scale mixture of normals: with latent variances in the sampler, the conditionals are conjugate, each observation carries the weight nu plus one over nu plus its squared standardized residual, an outlier falls to 0.018, and nu needs its own Metropolis step under an exponential prior with mean 25. First-order autoregressive errors fill Omega with rho to the k, its inverse is tridiagonal, Prais-Winsten quasi-differences the data, barium gives 2.94 with standard error 0.63 and rho hat 0.293, the naive standard errors are wrong by one plus rho phi over one minus rho phi, and a small sin makes rho an exact truncated-normal draw.
