A word on notation. The series is r sub t in Tsay and y sub t on the site's pages. The shock is a sub t, white noise with variance sigma squared. B is the back-shift operator: B times r sub t is r sub t minus one. The Greek letter psi names the moving-average weights, the weights with which past shocks enter today's value. And one sign convention matters throughout: Tsay writes the moving-average part with minus signs, so an MA of order one is a sub t minus theta one times a sub t minus one. R's arima function, statsmodels and Wooldridge write plus signs. So a printed R or statsmodels coefficient ma1 equal to minus 0.39 is Tsay's theta one equal to plus 0.39. Every formula in this narration uses Tsay's sign.

The books are cited by short names. Tsay is Analysis of Financial Time Series, third edition, the main reference for this class. Wooldridge is Introductory Econometrics, sixth edition. The decks are ar-ma, unitroot, seasonal and bayes-garch.

The one claim. A linear time-series model is a recipe for the psi weights, and the roots decide everything.

Roots outside the unit circle give a stationary ARMA, whose forecasts revert to the mean with a bounded error variance. Complex roots give stochastic cycles: 10.6 quarters for US GNP.

A root on the unit circle makes every shock permanent. The forecast variance grows like the horizon ell times sigma squared, and the t test of phi equal to one has its own Dickey-Fuller law, with a 5 per cent point of minus 2.86, not minus 1.645.

Between those two cases sit long memory, where the autocorrelation at lag k behaves like k to the power two d minus one, and the seasonal unit roots that the airline model differences away.

The second half of the class moves the same ideas to the variance. Returns are uncorrelated but their squares are not, so GARCH is an ARMA for the squared shock a sub t squared, with persistence alpha one plus beta one near one, and with fat tails produced from Gaussian shocks. Asymmetric variants add leverage. Stochastic volatility gives the variance its own shock and needs Markov chain Monte Carlo. And Bayesian GARCH turns the question "is alpha one plus beta one below one?" into a posterior probability: 0.9966 for Petrobras.

The one result to hold. Write the series as its mean mu plus the sum, over j from zero onward, of psi j times a sub t minus j. Then the variance of the ell-step forecast error at origin h equals sigma squared times the sum of psi j squared, for j from zero to ell minus one. That sum tends to the stationary variance gamma zero when the roots lie outside the unit circle, and it equals ell times sigma squared for the random walk, whose psi weights are all one. And on the variance side: the ell-step variance forecast minus the long-run variance equals alpha one plus beta one, raised to the power ell minus one, times the one-step variance forecast minus the long-run variance.

The same arithmetic runs twice: once on the level, once on the variance. In levels the psi weights decide whether forecast uncertainty is bounded, equation 28 of section 2 against equation 6 of section 3. In variances, GARCH's ARMA form for a sub t squared makes alpha one plus beta one play the role of the autoregressive root, equations 21 and 28 of section 6, and IGARCH is its unit root.

The claim, before the derivations. A second-order autoregression is two first-order autoregressions stacked, and its behaviour is read off one quadratic: one minus phi one x minus phi two x squared equals zero. It is stationary if and only if both solutions lie outside the unit circle, which in the plane of phi one and phi two is the triangle where phi two is less than one minus phi one, phi two is less than one plus phi one, and phi two is greater than minus one. The autocorrelation function obeys the same second-order difference equation as the series, the Yule-Walker equation. So real roots give a mixture of exponential decays, and complex roots give damped waves, with average period k equal to two pi divided by the inverse cosine of phi one over twice the square root of minus phi two. That is how a quarterly GNP third-order autoregression delivers a 10.6-quarter business cycle. The order p is chosen by the partial autocorrelation function, which cuts off after lag p with standard error one over the square root of T, or by AIC and BIC, which can disagree: order 9 against order 1 on the same CRSP returns.

What Class 1, section 7, already settled, and this section uses without repeating: weak stationarity, the autocorrelation function and the Ljung-Box test; linear series and psi weights; the first-order autoregression's mean, variance and autocorrelation, rho ell equal to phi one to the ell; conditional least squares as conditional maximum likelihood; and the autoregression of order p as a regression with a mean.

The notation. r sub t is the series in Tsay, y sub t on the site's pages. a sub t is the white-noise shock with variance sigma a squared, written sigma squared on the ar2-forecast page. Phi zero, phi one, up to phi p are the autoregressive intercept and coefficients. Mu is the stationary mean, the expected value of r sub t. Gamma ell and rho ell are the lag-ell autocovariance and autocorrelation, and gamma zero is the variance of r sub t. B is the back-shift operator: B times r sub t is r sub t minus one, and B times gamma ell is gamma ell minus one; Tsay writes L too. x one and x two are the solutions of the characteristic equation one minus phi one x minus phi two x squared equals zero, complex in general. Omega i, equal to one over x i, are the characteristic roots, Tsay's name for the inverses. k is the average length of the stochastic cycle, in periods. Phi ell comma ell is the lag-ell partial autocorrelation. T is the sample length. And sigma tilde ell squared is the maximum likelihood estimate of sigma a squared in an autoregression of order ell.

One point one. The mean and the centred form. Slide 18; Tsay printed pages 40 to 41.

The model, Tsay equation 2.12. Equation 1: r sub t equals phi zero plus phi one r sub t minus one plus phi two r sub t minus two plus a sub t.

Take expectations of equation 1, with the expected value of a sub t equal to zero and, under stationarity, the expected values of r sub t, r sub t minus one and r sub t minus two all equal to mu. Three steps. First, mu equals phi zero plus phi one mu plus phi two mu. Second, mu times one minus phi one minus phi two equals phi zero. Third, equation 2: mu equals phi zero over one minus phi one minus phi two, provided phi one plus phi two is not equal to one.

The slide prints the proviso as "phi one plus phi two equals one": the inequality sign was lost.

Substitute phi zero equal to one minus phi one minus phi two, times mu, from equation 2, into equation 1. Then r sub t equals one minus phi one minus phi two, times mu, plus phi one r sub t minus one plus phi two r sub t minus two plus a sub t. Rearranged, equation 3: r sub t minus mu equals phi one times r sub t minus one minus mu, plus phi two times r sub t minus two minus mu, plus a sub t.

The intercept is not the mean. As phi one plus phi two tends to one, the denominator of equation 2 vanishes: that is the unit root of section 3.

One point two. The moment equations and Yule-Walker. Slides 18 and 19; Tsay equation 2.13, printed page 41.

Multiply equation 3 by r sub t minus ell minus mu and take expectations. For ell greater than zero, r sub t minus ell depends only on shocks dated t minus ell and earlier, so the expected value of r sub t minus ell minus mu, times a sub t, is zero. The left side becomes the lag-ell autocovariance, and the right side becomes phi one times the lag ell minus one autocovariance, plus phi two times the lag ell minus two autocovariance, plus zero. Equation 4: gamma ell equals phi one gamma ell minus one plus phi two gamma ell minus two, for ell greater than zero.

For ell equal to zero the shock term does not vanish. The expected value of r sub t minus mu, times a sub t, equals the expected value of a sub t squared, which is sigma a squared, because only the a sub t in equation 3 is correlated with itself. So equation 5: gamma zero equals phi one gamma one plus phi two gamma two plus sigma a squared.

Divide equation 4 by gamma zero. This is the Yule-Walker, or moment, equation of the autocorrelation function, Tsay 2.13. Equation 6: rho ell equals phi one rho ell minus one plus phi two rho ell minus two, for ell greater than zero.

At ell equal to one, using rho zero equal to one and rho minus one equal to rho one: rho one equals phi one times rho zero plus phi two times rho minus one, which is phi one plus phi two rho one. Solve. Equation 7: rho one equals phi one over one minus phi two.

At ell equal to two, substituting equation 7 into equation 6. Equation 8: rho two equals phi one rho one plus phi two, which is phi one squared over one minus phi two, plus phi two.

In operator form, equation 6 says that one minus phi one B minus phi two B squared, applied to rho ell, equals zero. The autocorrelation function solves the same difference equation as the series without its shock. That single fact gives the shape of the autocorrelation function, in subsection 4, and of the forecasts, in section 2, subsection 1.

One point three. The variance, step by step. Slide 19.

From equation 7, gamma one equals phi one over one minus phi two, times gamma zero. From equation 4 at ell equal to two, gamma two equals phi one gamma one plus phi two gamma zero.

Step two. Collect: gamma zero equals phi one times one plus phi two, times gamma one, plus phi two squared gamma zero, plus sigma a squared.

One point eight. The partial autocorrelation function. Slides 31 and 32; Tsay printed pages 46 to 48.

The definition. Part one, section 2. Forecasting, moving-average and ARMA models: conditional means, psi weights, and what the autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation identify.

This section covers the ar-ma deck, slides 38 to 52: forecasting, one-step, two-step and multistep forecasts, mean reversion, half-life, the moving average of order one as a constrained infinite autoregression, the moving average of order q, the autocorrelations of the moving averages of orders one and two, estimation, moving-average forecasts, and the summary. It also covers the site page ar2-forecast, with the exact h-step forecast distributions of a second-order autoregression and their Monte Carlo check; Tsay section 2.4.4, printed pages 54 to 57, with Table 2.2; section 2.5, printed pages 57 to 64, equations 2.17 to 2.24 and Table 2.3; section 2.6, printed pages 64 to 71, equations 2.25 to 2.34; and Wooldridge section 11.1, printed pages 345 to 348, equation 11.1.

The claim, before the derivations. The forecast that minimises mean squared error is the conditional mean, and its error is the sum of the shocks still to come, weighted by the psi weights. The ell-step error at origin h is a sub h plus ell, plus psi one a sub h plus ell minus one, and so on, up to psi ell minus one a sub h plus one. So its variance, sigma a squared times the sum of the squared psi weights for i below ell, rises to gamma zero while the point forecast reverts to mu. For the deck's second-order autoregression with 1.75 and minus 0.80, the forecast standard deviation goes 1, 2.02, 3.03, 3.97, and on to 7.12. A moving average of order q is a finite-memory model: always stationary, its autocorrelation cuts off after lag q, its forecasts reach the mean after q steps, and it is identified only up to invertibility, with the absolute value of rho one at most one half for an MA of order one. An ARMA of order one and one has the first-order autoregression's stationarity condition and an autocorrelation like the first-order autoregression's that starts decaying one lag late; neither its autocorrelation nor its partial autocorrelation cuts off.

The notation. h is the forecast origin and ell the forecast horizon, at least one. F sub h is the information available at h, inclusive. r hat sub h of ell is the ell-step-ahead forecast at origin h. e sub h of ell, the forecast error, is r sub h plus ell minus that forecast. Phi i and theta j are the autoregressive and moving-average coefficients, with Tsay's sign: r sub t equals c zero plus a sub t minus theta one a sub t minus one, and so on. c zero is the moving-average constant, which equals the expected value of r sub t. Psi i are the infinite moving-average weights, the impulse response, with psi zero equal to one. Pi i are the infinite autoregressive weights. Phi of B and theta of B are the autoregressive and moving-average polynomials, one minus the sum of phi i B to the i, and one minus the sum of theta j B to the j. And y sub n and y sub n minus one are the last two observations on the ar2-forecast page.

The sign convention, once more. Tsay writes the moving-average part with minus signs; R's arima, statsmodels and Wooldridge's equation 11.1 write plus signs. A printed ma1 equal to minus 0.39 is Tsay's theta one equal to 0.39.

Two point one. The forecast is the conditional mean. Slide 38; Tsay printed page 54.

The forecast minimises the expected squared error given F sub h, over functions g of F sub h. Equation 1: the expected value, given F sub h, of the squared difference between r sub h plus ell and its forecast is at most the minimum over g of the expected value, given F sub h, of r sub h plus ell minus g, squared.

For any g in F sub h, add and subtract m, the conditional mean of r sub h plus ell given F sub h. The cross term has conditional mean zero. So the expected squared error given F sub h equals the conditional variance of r sub h plus ell, plus m minus g, squared. That is minimised at g equal to m. Hence equation 2: the forecast r hat sub h of ell equals the expected value of r sub h plus ell given F sub h.

Forecasts with estimated parameters are conditional forecasts: they ignore parameter uncertainty, which Bayesian forecasting by Markov chain Monte Carlo would include, Tsay printed page 55, Classes 3 and 4, and section 9.

Two point six. Estimation. Slide 49; Tsay printed pages 61 to 62. Course layer, with one graduate item.

Maximum likelihood, with the first shocks handled two ways. Part two, section 3. Unit roots: shocks that never die, forecasts that never revert, and a t ratio that is not normal.

This section covers the unitroot deck, slides 1 to 31: the random walk, mean reversion, predictability, memory, the random walk with drift, the constant term, trend stationarity, ARIMA of orders p, 1 and q, the Dickey-Fuller and augmented Dickey-Fuller tests, the rewrite of a third-order autoregression, US GDP and the S and P 500. It also covers the site page unitroot, with its simulations and adf.test outputs; Tsay section 2.7, printed pages 71 to 81, equations 2.35 to 2.40, equation 2.37 for 3M returns, Example 2.2 on GDP and the S and P 500 example; and Wooldridge sections 18.2 and 18.3, printed pages 574 to 580, equations 18.17 to 18.30, Tables 18.2 and 18.3, and Examples 18.2 to 18.4.

The claim, before the derivations. A random walk is the first-order autoregression at phi one equal to one. Every shock is permanent, with all psi weights equal to one; the point forecast never moves from today's value; and the forecast-error variance grows like ell times sigma squared. Adding a drift mu makes the constant a slope, not a mean. A trend-stationary series has the same straight-line mean but a bounded variance. Whether a series has a unit root is tested with the Dickey-Fuller t ratio, whose null distribution is skewed left and has its own critical values: minus 2.86 at 5 per cent with a constant, and minus 3.41 with a trend, not minus 1.645. Using normal critical values rejects a true unit root almost half the time. US GDP and the S and P 500 do not reject; their differences do. And regressing one independent random walk on another finds a relationship most of the time.

This builds on Class 1, section 7, where the random walk, y sub t equal to y zero plus e one plus, and so on, plus e sub t, its variance t sigma squared, the non-reverting forecast, and the correlation between y sub t and y sub t plus h, the square root of t over t plus h, were derived. This section uses them without repeating them.

The notation. p sub t is the log price in Tsay, or any candidate integrated series. a sub t and e sub t are white-noise shocks with variance sigma squared: Tsay uses a sub t in models and e sub t in test regressions. Mu is the drift, the expected value of p sub t minus p sub t minus one in a random walk with drift. h and ell are the forecast origin and horizon. Psi i are the infinite moving-average weights. Phi zero and phi one are the constant and slope in the Dickey-Fuller regressions. Rho is the autoregressive coefficient in Wooldridge's equation 18.17, and theta equals rho minus one. Lowercase c, equal to beta minus one, is the coefficient of x sub t minus one in the difference form of the augmented regression in Tsay; it is negative or zero. Beta has two uses, stated each time: in Tsay's equation 2.40 it is the levels coefficient, with null value one; on the slides, in the difference form, beta equals minus phi of one, with null value zero. Phi of B is the autoregressive polynomial, and phi of one is one minus the sum of the phi i. c sub t is the deterministic term: zero, omega zero, or omega zero plus omega one t. And I of d means integrated of order d: stationary after d differences.

Three point one. The random walk as a forecasting model. Slides 4 to 12; Tsay section 2.7.1, printed pages 72 to 73.

The random walk, Tsay equation 2.35. Equation 1: p sub t equals p sub t minus one plus a sub t, with p zero given and a sub t white noise. It is the first-order autoregression with phi zero equal to zero and phi one equal to one, which violates the condition that the absolute value of phi one is below one: "a unit-root nonstationary time series", Tsay printed page 72.

Point forecasts. At origin h, equation 1 at t equal to h plus one gives p sub h plus one equals p sub h plus a sub h plus one. Take the expectation given F sub h, with the expected value of a sub h plus one given F sub h equal to zero. Equation 2: the one-step forecast equals p sub h.

For two steps, write p sub h plus two equals p sub h plus one plus a sub h plus two, and condition: the two-step forecast equals the conditional expectation of p sub h plus one plus the conditional expectation of a sub h plus two, which is the one-step forecast plus zero, which is p sub h. The process is not mean reverting. Compare Class 1, where a stationary first-order autoregression's forecast decays as phi to the ell toward mu.

Memory. All psi weights equal to one in equation 4 means a shock from any date enters today's level with weight one: "in economics, the shocks are said to have a permanent effect on the series", Tsay printed page 73. The sample autocorrelation of a random walk approaches one at every fixed lag as T grows; slide 11 shows it at lag 200 from 100,000 simulated steps. That is what makes the autocorrelation function useless for identifying anything once the series is integrated of order one.

The contrast in one sentence. Stationary: shocks fade as the psi weights go to zero, forecasts revert, and the forecast variance converges to gamma zero. Unit root: the psi weights equal one, forecasts are flat at p sub h, and the variance grows without bound.

Three point two. The random walk with drift: the constant becomes a slope. Slides 13 to 15; Tsay section 2.7.2, printed pages 73 to 75.

The trend-stationary alternative, slide 17 and Tsay section 2.7.3, printed page 75. Equation 11: p sub t equals beta zero plus beta one t plus r sub t, with r sub t stationary, for example an autoregression of order p.

Compare the two models on three dimensions. First, the mean: for the random walk with drift it is p zero plus mu t; for the trend-stationary model it is beta zero plus beta one t. Both are straight lines. Second, the variance: for the random walk with drift it is t sigma squared and grows; for the trend-stationary model it is the variance of r sub t, finite and constant. Third, a shock at date t: permanent for the random walk with drift, with psi weights equal to one; it dies out in the trend-stationary model, since the psi weights of r sub t go to zero.

Both have a straight-line mean; only the variance tells them apart. Remove the trend from equation 11 by regression and r sub t is stationary. Remove a trend from equation 8 and the sum of the a i is still a random walk. Differencing is the right transformation for equation 7; detrending is the right one for equation 11.

The figure, in words. On the left, forecast bands against the horizon up to 120 months, with the 3M calibration, mu equal to 0.0103 and sigma equal to 0.0637. The random walk with drift has mean 0.0103 times ell and a plus or minus two sigma times the square root of ell band that keeps widening, to about plus or minus 1.40 at ell equal to 120, since two times 0.0637 times the square root of 120 is 1.40 at ten years. Three point three. What the constant term means. Slide 16; Tsay printed page 74.

Three models. In a moving average of order q, Tsay equation 2.22, the constant is the mean: the expected value of r sub t is c zero. In a stationary autoregression or ARMA, the constant is related to the mean: mu equals phi zero over one minus phi one minus, and so on, minus phi p. In a random walk with drift, the constant is the time slope: the expected value of p sub t is p zero plus mu t, by equation 9.

The third line follows from the second: as the sum of the phi i tends to one, the denominator vanishes and the mean ceases to exist; the constant becomes the slope instead. Tsay adds the contrast with regression: in r sub t equals phi zero plus phi one r sub t minus one plus a sub t, the coefficient must satisfy the absolute value of phi one at most one for the model to be meaningful, while in y sub t equals beta zero plus beta one x sub t plus a sub t the slope can be any real number, Tsay printed page 74.

Three point four. ARIMA of orders p, 1 and q, and differencing. Slides 18 and 19; Tsay section 2.7.4, printed pages 75 to 76.

Allowing the autoregressive polynomial of an ARMA to have a root at one gives an ARIMA model. The definition, Tsay printed page 76. Equation 12: y sub t is ARIMA of orders p, 1 and q if c sub t, equal to y sub t minus y sub t minus one, which is one minus B times y sub t, is a stationary and invertible ARMA of orders p and q.

Write the stationary ARMA for c sub t as phi of B times c sub t equals theta zero plus theta of B times a sub t, and substitute c sub t equals one minus B times y sub t. Equation 13: phi of B times one minus B times y sub t equals theta zero plus theta of B times a sub t.

The autoregressive polynomial of y sub t is phi of B times one minus B, which vanishes at B equal to one: a unit root. As in equation 4, the psi weights of y sub t do not decay, so ARIMA processes have "strong memory", slide 18. The canonical case: log prices are ARIMA, and log returns, r sub t equal to the log of P sub t minus the log of P sub t minus one, are stationary.

Double unit roots. If y sub t and c sub t are both unit-root nonstationary, but s sub t, equal to c sub t minus c sub t minus one, which is one minus B squared times y sub t, which is y sub t minus two y sub t minus one plus y sub t minus two, is stationary, then y sub t is integrated of order two, and ARIMA of orders p, 2 and q if s sub t is an ARMA of orders p and q. A nonzero mean of s sub t then implies a quadratic time trend in y sub t, one more level of integration than equation 8. Tsay's candidate is the US GDP deflator, Tsay printed page 76; that is his Exercise 2.15, not on our list.

Three point five. The Dickey-Fuller test. Slides 20 to 23; Tsay section 2.7.5, printed pages 76 to 77; Wooldridge section 18.2, printed pages 574 to 576.

Five point one. The regressions and the hypothesis.

Equation 14, Tsay equation 2.38: p sub t equals phi one p sub t minus one plus e sub t. Equation 15, Tsay equation 2.39: p sub t equals phi zero plus phi one p sub t minus one plus e sub t. Equation 16: the null hypothesis is phi one equal to one, against the alternative phi one less than one.

The alternative is one-sided: phi one greater than one would be explosive. Wooldridge writes equation 15 as y sub t equals alpha plus rho y sub t minus one plus e sub t, his equation 18.17, and subtracts y sub t minus one from both sides, with theta equal to rho minus one. Equation 17, Wooldridge 18.21: delta y sub t equals alpha plus theta y sub t minus one plus e sub t, with null theta equal to zero against the alternative theta less than zero.

Testing rho equal to one in levels and theta equal to zero in differences is the same test: the two regressions have the same residuals, and theta hat equals rho hat minus one with the same standard error. Note what is null: the unit root, integration of order one. "We do not take the null to be I(0) because y sub t is I(0) for any value of rho strictly between minus one and one", Wooldridge printed page 575.

Five point two. The t ratio, derived.

For equation 14 with p zero equal to zero, least squares gives, equation 18: phi one hat equals the sum over t of p sub t minus one times p sub t, divided by the sum of p sub t minus one squared; and sigma e hat squared equals the sum of squared residuals, p sub t minus phi one hat p sub t minus one, divided by T minus one.

Subtract one and put it over the common denominator: phi one hat minus one equals the sum of p sub t minus one p sub t minus the sum of p sub t minus one squared, all over the sum of p sub t minus one squared, which is the sum of p sub t minus one times the difference p sub t minus p sub t minus one, over the sum of p sub t minus one squared.

Under the null, equation 14 says p sub t minus p sub t minus one equals e sub t. Substitute in place. Equation 19: phi one hat minus one equals the sum of p sub t minus one times e sub t, over the sum of p sub t minus one squared.

The ordinary least squares standard error is sigma e hat over the square root of the sum of p sub t minus one squared. Divide equation 19 by it. Five point three. Why equation 20 is not standard normal.

Wooldridge's argument, printed page 575. In equation 17, under the null, the regressor y sub t minus one is integrated of order one, "and so the usual central limit theorem that underlies the asymptotic standard normal distribution for the t statistic does not apply." The sums in equation 20 are dominated by the late, large values of the walk: the sum of p sub t minus one squared is of order T squared, not T.

Intuition layer: one exact line that shows the skew. Square equation 14 under the null: p sub t squared equals p sub t minus one plus e sub t, squared, which is p sub t minus one squared plus two p sub t minus one e sub t plus e sub t squared. Sum over t from one to T. The p sub t minus one squared terms telescope, with p zero equal to zero. Equation 21: p sub T squared equals two times the sum of p sub t minus one e sub t, plus the sum of e sub t squared. So the sum of p sub t minus one e sub t equals one half of the bracket p sub T squared minus the sum of e sub t squared.

Divide by T sigma squared. From Class 1, p sub T is normal with mean zero and variance T sigma squared for Gaussian shocks, so p sub T squared over T sigma squared is chi-squared with one degree of freedom; and the sum of e sub t squared over T sigma squared tends to one. The numerator of equation 20 therefore behaves like one half of a chi-squared with one degree of freedom minus one, which is negative whenever the chi-squared is below one: probability 0.683. Under the null, phi one hat is below one about two times in three. The estimate is biased toward stationarity, and the t ratio is shifted left.

Graduate layer, named, not examinable: the limit is a functional of standard Brownian motion W. The t ratio converges to one half of W of one squared minus one, divided by the square root of the integral from zero to one of W of r squared, d r. That is Chan and Wei, 1988, and Phillips, 1987, Tsay printed page 77.

Five point four. Three cases, Tsay printed page 77. First, regression 14, with no constant, when the true phi zero is zero: the null law of the t ratio is the Dickey-Fuller Brownian functional, with critical values by simulation. Second, regression 15, with a constant, when the true phi zero is zero, a driftless walk: another nonstandard law, Wooldridge's Table 18.2. Third, regression 15, with a constant, when the true phi zero is not zero, a walk with drift: the t ratio is asymptotically normal, but "large sample sizes are needed". The third case is why the deterministic term has to match the alternative being considered, subsection 6.

Five point five. Critical values, Wooldridge Tables 18.2 and 18.3, printed pages 575 to 577. Reject the null when the t ratio is below the critical value c.

With a constant and no trend, Wooldridge Table 18.2: minus 3.43 at 1 per cent, minus 3.12 at 2.5 per cent, minus 2.86 at 5 per cent, and minus 2.57 at 10 per cent. With a constant and a trend, Wooldridge Table 18.3: minus 3.96 at 1 per cent, minus 3.66 at 2.5 per cent, minus 3.41 at 5 per cent, and minus 3.12 at 10 per cent. "If we use the standard normal critical value to test for a unit root, we would reject H0 much more often than 5 per cent of the time when H0 is true", Wooldridge printed page 575. Worked, from the right panel of the figure: in the seeded simulation, the probability that the t ratio falls below minus 1.645 under the null is 0.465. Six point two. The augmented regression and statistic.

Tsay writes the regression in levels, his equation 2.40. The deterministic term. Use c sub t equal to omega zero plus omega one t when the series trends, so that the alternative is trend-stationary, equation 11. Three point eight. Spurious regression. Wooldridge section 18.3, printed pages 578 to 580.

Let x sub t equal x sub t minus one plus a sub t, and y sub t equal y sub t minus one plus e sub t, Wooldridge 18.27 and 18.28, with independent iid shocks and x zero and y zero equal to zero: the two series are independent. Regress y sub t on x sub t, Wooldridge 18.29, and test beta one equal to zero at 5 per cent. Granger and Newbold, 1974, found frequent significance. In Davidson and MacKinnon's simulation the usual t test rejects 66.2 per cent of the time at n equal to 50, and 84.7 per cent at n equal to 250, Wooldridge printed page 579: it gets worse with more data.

Why. Under the null beta one equal to zero, the model y sub t equals beta zero plus beta one x sub t plus u sub t, Wooldridge 18.30, can hold only with beta zero equal to zero and, equation 30: u sub t equals y sub t, which is the sum of e j for j from one to t. A random walk error. The errors are not even weakly dependent, so neither the t statistic, which diverges as n grows, nor the R squared, which converges to a random variable rather than to a population value, has its usual behaviour, Wooldridge printed pages 579 to 580. Adding a time trend does not fix it. The safe course is to difference integrated variables first, subsection 4; then the regression of delta y sub t on delta x sub t is a regression of white noise on white noise.

Graduate layer, named only: when some combination y sub t minus b x sub t is integrated of order zero, the two integrated series are cointegrated and the levels regression is meaningful, Wooldridge section 18.4. Cointegration is in the syllabus table but not on the class schedule.

Three point nine. Intuition layer: reading it as an economist.

A unit root in log GDP means a recession's output loss is never made up: the level forecast is today's level plus drift, equation 3 with equation 8. A trend-stationary GDP returns to its old trend line. The tests cannot reliably choose between them on post-war data, as Wooldridge's Example 18.4 showed with rho hat equal to 0.79 not rejected, which is why the decks state conclusions as "cannot be rejected" and work with growth rates. For asset prices, the unit root is the statement that returns are unpredictable in level terms, which is where the volatility models of section 6 start: the returns are serially uncorrelated; their squares are not.

Part two, section 4. Long memory: an autocorrelation that decays like a power of the lag, between ARMA and the unit root.

This section covers the unitroot deck, slides 32 to 38: long-memory models, their history, properties one to five, the fractionally integrated ARMA, known as ARFIMA, and references for Bayesian ARFIMA. It also covers Tsay section 2.11, printed pages 101 to 103, equations 2.52 and 2.53, properties one to five and Figure 2.22; and Tsay section 3.13, printed page 154, the long-memory stochastic volatility model, equation 3.41.

The claim, before the derivations. The fractionally differenced process, one minus B to the d times x sub t equal to a sub t, with d between minus 0.5 and 0.5, is stationary, yet its autocorrelations decay hyperbolically, rho k behaving like a constant times k to the power two d minus one, instead of geometrically like every stationary ARMA. Its whole autocorrelation function follows from one ratio: rho k over rho k minus one equals k minus one plus d, over k minus d, starting at rho one equal to d over one minus d. With d equal to 0.4, the correlation at lag 100 is still 0.27, where a first-order autoregression with the same rho one has a value of order ten to the minus 18. Long memory sits between the two cases of section 3: shocks fade, unlike a random walk, but slowly, unlike ARMA. Absolute returns are the financial example.

The notation. x sub t is the series and a sub t white noise with variance sigma a squared. d is the fractional differencing parameter, between minus 0.5 and 0.5. B is the back-shift operator. The generalised binomial coefficient, d choose k, is d times d minus one, and so on, times d minus k plus one, over k factorial. Psi k and pi k are the infinite moving-average and autoregressive weights. Rho k and phi k comma k are the autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation at lag k. f of omega is the spectral density at frequency omega, between zero and two pi. And the factorial of a non-integer x is defined through the gamma function: x factorial equals gamma of x plus one.

Four point one. Intuition layer: where long memory comes from. Slides 33 and 34.

The idea began in hydrology. Hurst, 1951, found that the rescaled range of the Nile's levels grew "anomalously fast". Mandelbrot showed that, if stationarity is kept, new mathematics is needed to explain the Hurst effect, and defined long memory, in Mandelbrot and Van Ness, 1968, and Mandelbrot and Wallis, 1968. The slides cite Palma, 2007, and Beran and others, 2013, for the theory.

The empirical signature, Tsay printed page 102: a sample autocorrelation that is not large but decays very slowly. In Tsay's Figure 2.22, the autocorrelation of absolute daily returns of the CRSP value-weighted and equal-weighted indexes, 1970 to 2008, is small yet still significant at the 5 per cent level after 300 lags, while the returns themselves are nearly uncorrelated. Ding, Granger and Engle, 1993, documented the same for absolute returns; it is the reason long-memory volatility models exist, subsection 6.

Four point two. Fractional differencing: the operator. Slide 32; Tsay equation 2.52, printed page 101; the binomial expansion on printed page 102.

Equation 1: one minus B, to the power d, times x sub t equals a sub t, with d between minus 0.5 and 0.5.

For non-integer d the operator is defined by the binomial series, Tsay printed page 102. Equation 2: one minus B to the d equals the sum over k from zero to infinity of d choose k times minus B to the k; that is, the sum over k of minus one to the k, times d times d minus one, and so on, times d minus k plus one, over k factorial, times B to the k.

The first terms, one at a time. For k equal to zero: one. For k equal to one: minus one to the first, times d over one factorial, times B, which is minus d B. For k equal to two: minus one squared, times d times d minus one over two factorial, times B squared, which is d times d minus one over two, times B squared, which is minus d times one minus d, over two, times B squared. For k equal to three: minus one cubed, times d times d minus one times d minus two, over three factorial, times B cubed, which is minus d times one minus d times two minus d, over six, times B cubed, using d minus one times d minus two equal to one minus d times two minus d.

So equation 3: one minus B to the d equals one, minus d B, minus d times one minus d over two times B squared, minus d times one minus d times two minus d over six times B cubed, and so on.

For d equal to one the series stops after two terms, and equation 3 is the ordinary difference, one minus B. For d between zero and one, every past value enters, with slowly shrinking weights. That is the source of the long memory.

Four point three. The two representations. Slide 35; Tsay properties 1 and 2, printed page 101.

Property 1, the moving-average form. If d is below 0.5, x sub t is weakly stationary and, equation 4: x sub t equals a sub t plus the sum over k from one onward of psi k a sub t minus k, with psi k equal to d times one plus d, and so on, times k minus one plus d, over k factorial; in factorial form, k plus d minus one factorial, over k factorial times d minus one factorial.

These are the coefficients of one minus B to the minus d, the inverse of equation 2: psi one equals d, and psi two equals d times one plus d, over two. For d equal to 0.25: psi one is 0.25, and psi two is 0.25 times 1.25 over two, which is 0.156.

Property 2, the autoregressive form. If d is above minus 0.5, x sub t is invertible. The coefficients of equation 2, written as products, are, equation 5: pi k equals minus one to the k times d choose k, which is minus d times one minus d, and so on, times k minus one minus d, over k factorial; in factorial form, k minus d minus one factorial, over k factorial times minus d minus one factorial; and pi zero equals one. These are Tsay's pi k, printed page 101. So equation 1 reads: the sum over k from zero of pi k x sub t minus k equals a sub t. Four point four. The autocorrelation, the partial autocorrelation, and the spectrum. Slides 36 and 37; Tsay properties 3 to 5, printed page 102.

The asymptotics. Graduate layer: the ratio of gamma functions. For large k, gamma of k plus a over gamma of k plus b is approximately k to the a minus b, by Stirling's formula. With a equal to d and b equal to one minus d, a minus b is two d minus one. Equation 11: rho k is approximately minus d factorial over d minus one factorial, times k to the two d minus one, as k grows, since gamma of one minus d is minus d factorial and gamma of d is d minus one factorial.

Four point five. ARFIMA of orders p, d and q, and the three kinds of memory. Slide 38; Tsay printed page 102.

If the fractionally differenced series, one minus B to the d times x sub t, follows an ARMA of orders p and q, then x sub t is ARFIMA of orders p, d and q: phi of B times one minus B to the d times x sub t equals theta of B times a sub t. It generalises ARIMA, equation 13 of section 3, from integer to non-integer d. The ARMA part shapes the short lags, d the long ones.

Compare the three kinds of memory on three dimensions. First, a stationary ARMA, d equal to zero: the psi weights, the effect of a shock at t minus k, decay geometrically, in proportion to the absolute value of a root to the k; the autocorrelation decays geometrically; the example is returns. Second, long memory, d between zero and 0.5: psi k is approximately k to the d minus one, over d minus one factorial, a hyperbolic decay; rho k behaves like a constant times k to the two d minus one; the examples are absolute returns and volatility. Third, a unit root, d equal to one: psi k equals one; the sample autocorrelation tends to one; the example is log prices. The approximation for psi k follows from equation 4 by the same gamma-ratio step as equation 11.

Estimation of d in the pure model, equation 1: maximum likelihood, or a regression on the logged periodogram at low frequencies, Tsay printed page 102, which uses property 5. Bayesian treatments, by Koop, Ley, Osiewalski and Steel, 1997, Pai and Ravishanker, 1998, Graves and others, 2015, and more, are listed on slide 38; named only. Estimation of the ARFIMA is in the graduate layer.

Part two, section 5. Seasonal models: difference twice, model the remainder with two moving-average terms, and know when the seasonality is deterministic.

This section covers the seasonal deck, slides 1 to 28: seasonal adjustment, seasonal differencing, the airline model and its autocorrelation, multiplicative against non-multiplicative models, seasonal ARIMA, the expansion of the seasonal ARIMA with orders one, one, one by one, one, one at period 12, the Johnson and Johnson and Decile 1 examples, the forecast package, the AirPassengers data, the fits, the root mean squared error study by horizon, and the two best models. It also covers the site page on AirPassengers seasonal models, with the log transform, trend plus dummies, BIC over 16 models, and the split into 108 and 36 months; Tsay section 2.8, printed pages 81 to 90, equations 2.41 and 2.42, Example 2.3 on Johnson and Johnson and Example 2.4 on Decile 1; and Wooldridge section 10.5e, printed pages 336 to 338, on seasonal dummies.

The claim, before the derivations. The airline model, one minus B to the s, times one minus B, times x sub t, equal to one minus theta B, times one minus capital Theta B to the s, times a sub t, removes trend and seasonality by regular and seasonal differencing, and describes what is left with two moving-average parameters. Its differenced series has nonzero autocorrelations only at lags 1, s minus one, s and s plus one, and the two side lags are the product rho one times rho s: the regular and seasonal dynamics are orthogonal. It is the special case of Box-Jenkins seasonal ARIMA with orders zero, one, one by zero, one, one at period s. When the fitted seasonal moving-average coefficient reaches one, it cancels the seasonal difference and the seasonality is deterministic; dummies then do the same job, as in Decile 1's January effect. For the AirPassengers series, BIC picks the airline model, statsmodels reproduces the deck's estimates, and out of sample it forecasts 1958 to 1960 with a root mean squared error of 36.6, against 63.3 for a trend-plus-dummies regression.

The notation. x sub t is the series, usually in logs. s is the periodicity: 4 for quarterly data, 12 for monthly, and at least 3 in subsection 3. Delta, equal to one minus B, is the regular difference, and delta sub s, equal to one minus B to the s, the seasonal difference. w sub t, equal to one minus B to the s times one minus B times x sub t, is the doubly differenced series. Lowercase theta and capital Theta are the regular and seasonal moving-average coefficients, with Tsay's sign, one minus theta B, both below one in absolute value. a sub t is white noise with variance sigma a squared. Gamma ell and rho ell are the autocovariance and autocorrelation of w sub t. Phi of B and theta of B are the regular autoregressive and moving-average polynomials, and capital Phi sub P of B to the s and capital Theta sub Q of B to the s are the seasonal ones.

The sign convention. R's arima and Arima functions and statsmodels print moving-average coefficients with a plus sign, one plus theta B. A printed ma1 equal to minus 0.3864 is Tsay's theta equal to 0.3864. Autoregressive coefficients print with the same sign in all three.

Five point one. Seasonal series and seasonal adjustment. Slides 3 and 4; Tsay section 2.8, printed pages 81 to 82; Wooldridge section 10.5e, printed pages 336 to 337.

A seasonal series repeats a pattern every s periods. Tsay's Figure 2.13 shows the quarterly earnings per share of Johnson and Johnson, 1960 to 1980, growing exponentially with a strong period-4 pattern and rising variability. Seasonal adjustment removes the seasonal component before inference; most US government series, such as GDP growth and unemployment, are published adjusted, Tsay printed page 82 and Wooldridge printed page 336. "In other applications such as forecasting, seasonality is as important as other characteristics of the data and must be handled accordingly", slide 4: the course models it.

Logs first, Tsay printed pages 82 to 83. The log turns exponential growth into a linear trend and stabilises the variance: the widening seasonal swings of the Johnson and Johnson earnings, and of AirPassengers in subsection 6, become swings of constant amplitude. In R this is lambda equal to zero, the Box-Cox transform with lambda zero.

Recap of part 1. A linear time-series model is a recipe for psi weights, and the roots decide its behaviour. The second-order autoregression is stationary inside the triangle; complex roots give cycles, 30.09 periods for the slides' pair and 10.62 quarters for GNP; the partial autocorrelation cuts off at the autoregressive order, and AIC and BIC can disagree, 9 against 1. Forecasts are conditional means whose error variance is sigma squared times the sum of squared psi weights, rising to 7.12 squared for the ar2-forecast model; moving averages cut off in the autocorrelation, obey the one-half bound, and need invertibility. At a unit root the psi weights are all one: forecasts are flat, the variance grows like ell sigma squared, and the Dickey-Fuller ratio has its own critical values, minus 2.86 and minus 3.41, with the normal value giving a 46.5 per cent test. Long memory decays like k to the two d minus one, 0.27 at lag 100 for d equal to 0.4. And the airline model differences twice and keeps two moving-average terms, with side lags rho one times rho s, beating dummies out of sample, 36.6 against 63.3.
