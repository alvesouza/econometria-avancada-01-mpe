Advanced Econometrics, Class 7. More econometric models. Narrated study notes, part 3 of 3: the support material.

How to listen. This file holds the support parts of the Class 7 narration, where audio beats reading. Seven parts, in order. First, the pitfalls: fourteen confusions, each said as "the error is, the correction is", with the counterexample. Second, the spoken formula sheet, built for active recall: you hear a prompt, pause the player, say the result aloud, then hear the answer. Third, the implementation: the reference Python routines described as sequences of steps, never read as code. Fourth, the reading route and the exercises, as addresses only. Fifth, a thirty-item true or false self-test, with a pause cue before each answer. Sixth, a glossary. Seventh, the whole class in five lines, the piece worth replaying.

A reminder on scope. The final exam has no code. Expect derivations, such as full conditionals, likelihoods, the scale mixture, partial pooling and Tobit moments, and the reading of printouts, such as Wooldridge's tables and the slides' posterior summaries.


Support part one. The pitfalls.

This part covers the pitfalls note, part one: the confusions this material produces. Every counterexample uses a number derived in a section note, and the new ones are checked in the Python checks, section 7.

Pitfall one. Heteroskedasticity and bias.
The error: saying "heteroskedasticity biases ordinary least squares."
The correction: ordinary least squares stays unbiased; its variance formula is wrong, and it is inefficient. The counterexample is Wooldridge's Example 8.6: ordinary least squares gives 0.821 and weighted least squares 0.787, and both estimate the same slope. What differs is the standard error, 0.104 against 0.063. Section 1, subsection 3.

Pitfall two. Robust standard errors against efficiency.
The error: saying "robust standard errors make ordinary least squares as good as generalized least squares."
The correction: they make the inference of ordinary least squares valid, not ordinary least squares efficient. At alpha one equal to one, the true ordinary least squares slope variance is 4.38 times the generalized least squares one, whatever standard error you report. Section 1, subsection 4.1.

Pitfall three. The weighted least squares weights.
The error: saying "the weighted least squares weights are one over the square root of h i."
The correction: the variables are divided by the square root of h i; the squared residuals are weighted by one over h i, Wooldridge printed page 256. And the constant column is divided too: the transformed regression has no column of ones. Section 1, equation 9.

Pitfall four. Sigma squared in a t model.
The error: saying "sigma squared in a t model is the error variance."
The correction: it is the scale; the variance is sigma squared times nu over nu minus two. Greenberg's Vella and Verbeek sigma squared falls from 0.165 to 0.105 under the t with 5 degrees of freedom, yet 0.105 times 5 over 3 is 0.175: the spread hardly changed. Section 2, subsection 6.

Pitfall five. The omega i as parameters.
The error: saying "the omega i are random effects to be estimated and reported."
The correction: they are a computational device. The model is the marginal t, and the omega i are integrated out by ignoring their draws, Greenberg printed page 114. Their posterior means are still useful diagnostics: an outlier's weight of 0.018. Section 2, subsection 5.

Pitfall six. A large nu as evidence of normality.
The error: saying "a large posterior nu is evidence of normality."
The correction: nu is weakly identified. The slide 21 chain wanders over 5 to 60 with an autocorrelation of 0.5 at lag 30, and one outlier drags the posterior mean to 3.8. Look at the prior too: the exponential with mean 25 already puts 0.20 on nu at least 40. Section 2, subsection 5.

Pitfall seven. Autoregressive errors and the direction of the standard-error bias.
The error: saying "with first-order autoregressive errors, ordinary least squares standard errors are always too small."
The correction: only if the regressor is persistent in the same direction. The ratio of the true to the naive variance is one plus rho phi over one minus rho phi: equal to one when phi is zero, and below one when rho phi is negative. The efficiency loss of ordinary least squares remains even when the standard error is right: 9.53 at rho equal to 0.9 and phi equal to zero. Section 3, subsection 3.

Pitfall eight. Cochrane-Orcutt against Prais-Winsten.
The error: saying "Cochrane-Orcutt and Prais-Winsten are different estimators of different things."
The correction: same model, same rho iteration; Prais-Winsten keeps the first observation, rescaled by the square root of one minus rho squared. Barium: 2.94 against 2.95. Section 3, subsection 2.

Pitfall nine. The direction of shrinkage.
The error: saying "shrinkage pulls estimates toward zero."
The correction: it pulls them toward the estimated population mean. School C, at minus 2.75, moves up, to 7.02 at tau equal to 5.23. Section 4, subsection 5.

Pitfall ten. Hierarchical, random effects and consistency.
The error: saying "hierarchical equals random effects equals consistent no matter what."
The correction: the exchangeable prior, like random effects, assumes the unit effects are unrelated to the regressors, Wooldridge equation 14.8. Fixed effects, which is individual effects with a flat prior, does not. Section 4, subsection 8.

Pitfall eleven. The site's band.
The error: saying "the site's band is a predictive interval."
The correction: it is the posterior of alpha i plus beta i x; a new measurement adds normal noise with variance sigma y squared. Section 4, subsection 6.

Pitfall twelve. The Tobit coefficient as a marginal effect.
The error: saying "the Tobit coefficient is the marginal effect on hours."
The correction: it is the effect on the expected value of y star. On the expected value of y given x it is beta j times capital Phi of z: at the averages 0.645 times beta, and on average 0.589 times beta, for Mroz. Section 5, subsections 5 and 7.

Pitfall thirteen. Censored against truncated.
The error: saying "censored is the same as truncated."
The correction: censored samples keep the units, with y at the limit; truncated samples lose them, x included. The slides' "probability of truncation" is a censoring probability. Ordinary least squares is biased in both, differently: on the full censored sample the slope is beta times the probability that y is positive, 1.50; on the positive part only, 1.902. Section 5, subsections 1, 6 and 8.

Pitfall fourteen. Comparing probit and logit coefficients.
The error: saying "probit coefficients are comparable to logit ones."
The correction: only after scaling. Logit coefficients are about 1.6 times probit ones: in Mroz, 0.221 over 0.131 is 1.69, and the scale factors give 0.391 over 0.243, which is 1.61. The probabilities agree; the coefficients do not. Section 6, subsections 1 and 2.

The distinctions that cost most points, said once more. Unbiased is not efficient, and valid inference is not efficiency. A scale is not a variance. A computational device is not a parameter. Shrinkage goes toward the population mean, not toward zero. A band for the mean line is not a predictive band. A latent-scale coefficient is not an observable effect. Censoring keeps the units, truncation drops them. And probit and logit coefficients live on different scales.

Support part three. The implementation, described as steps.

This part covers the Python note: every sampler of the class, each followed by the assertions that tie it to a printed number. The file uses NumPy, SciPy, statsmodels and the wooldridge data only; there is no PyMC and no scikit-learn, so every Gibbs and Metropolis-Hastings step is written out, as the slides write it. The lecturer's R is the reference, never the code. The file is the source of truth, and the note copies it section by section. Nothing here is read as code: each routine is described by its inputs, its sequence of steps, and the one trap that breaks it.

How it runs. One command runs the file in about two minutes, most of it in the samplers. It prints one line per section and ends with the line "ALL L07 CHECKS PASSED". The first disagreement aborts with an assertion error naming the quantity and the printed value. The data for Wooldridge's tables come from the wooldridge package.

What is reused rather than rewritten. The figure script supplies the eight-schools data, the conditional means and the exact posterior of tau; the pregnancy data and a line-by-line port of the site's hierarchical Gibbs sampler; the generalized least squares efficiency ratio; the autoregressive variance ratios; the Tobit moments and slide 9's probability of a zero; and the t weight and the mixture density. The companion checking script is cited, not imported, because it checks the pages. And statsmodels supplies the frequentist fits of Wooldridge's tables: ordinary, weighted, probit and logit. What is new, and why: no existing project code samples these models. statsmodels has no Bayesian samplers and no Tobit, and its autoregressive generalized least squares routine iterates Cochrane-Orcutt, not Prais-Winsten, so Prais-Winsten is written here in three lines.

Routine zero, the shared helpers. A check helper passes when a computed number rounds to the printed value at the stated number of decimals, that is, within half a unit in the last printed place. The truncated-normal sampler is slide 26's inverse-distribution-function draw, equation 15 of section 3: standardize the two bounds, draw a uniform, mix the two standard normal probabilities at the bounds with weights u and one minus u, apply the inverse normal distribution function, and rescale. The trap: when the whole interval lies in the upper tail, both probabilities round to one, so the routine reflects the problem into the lower tail first and flips the sign back. The beta sampler is the general-Omega conditional, equation 8 of section 1, the boxed result of the map: it accepts either a vector of weights, the diagonal of Omega inverse, or a full Omega inverse matrix; builds the posterior precision as V zero inverse plus X transposed Omega inverse X over sigma squared; inverts it; forms the mean; and draws one multivariate normal vector.

Routine one, generalized and weighted least squares, and Case I. First, on an arbitrary known covariance, build P as the inverse of the Cholesky factor, confirm that P Omega P transposed is the identity, and confirm that ordinary least squares on P y and P X equals the generalized least squares formula; then confirm that the beta conditional with zero prior precision collapses to generalized least squares, with variance sigma squared times the inverse of X transposed Omega inverse X. Second, reproduce Wooldridge's Table 8.1: keep single people, fit ordinary least squares with robust standard errors and weighted least squares with weights one over income, and check all eight printed numbers and the sample size of 2,017; then confirm that weighted least squares equals ordinary least squares on the transformed variables, with every column, the constant too, divided by the square root of income. Third, Case I on the slide 8 design: simulate with alpha equal to one and one, get the starting alpha from the regression of the log squared ordinary least squares residual on x, which is the feasible step, and confirm that the expected log of a chi-squared with one degree of freedom is minus 1.27. Then alternate a beta draw with weights e to the minus z transposed alpha and sigma squared fixed at one, and a random-walk Metropolis-Hastings step for alpha with step 0.15, accepting on the log-likelihood difference plus the log prior difference; 12,000 iterations, 2,000 burned. The trap: an acceptance rate outside roughly 0.15 to 0.7 means the step is badly tuned; the run gives 0.55. Finally, check the efficiency ratio of 4.38 at alpha one equal to one.

Routine two, Student t errors. First, check the scale-mixture identity numerically, the mixture density against the t density at 20 pairs of nu and residual. Second, check the prior probabilities 0.3297 and 0.2019, and that the variance of a t with 5 degrees of freedom is five thirds. Third, the Gibbs and Metropolis-Hastings sampler of slides 16 to 19. Its inputs are the design, the data, the prior on beta, the prior on sigma squared, the prior mean of nu, the burn-in and the number of draws. Each sweep: compute the residuals; draw each precision, one over omega i, from a gamma with shape nu plus one over two and rate nu plus the squared residual over sigma squared, over two; draw beta with weights one over omega i; draw sigma squared from its inverse-gamma with the weighted residual sum of squares; propose a new nu by a random walk with step 0.25 and accept on the difference of the log conditional of nu, rejecting any non-positive proposal. Keep the draws and the running mean of each weight. The run plants one outlier of plus 15. The traps it asserts: the outlier's mean weight must fall below a fifth of the median weight, and dropping the outlier must move the t fit by less than half of what it moves ordinary least squares.

Routine three, first-order autoregressive errors. First, build Omega rho from its entries rho to the absolute value of i minus j over one minus rho squared, and the closed-form tridiagonal inverse, and confirm they are inverses; build the Prais-Winsten matrix and confirm P transposed P is the inverse and P Omega P transposed is the identity. Second, reproduce Wooldridge's Table 12.1 on the barium data: fit ordinary least squares; then iterate, starting from rho equal to zero, quasi-differencing the data with the first row scaled by the square root of one minus rho squared, fitting, recomputing the residuals on the original data, and re-estimating rho by regressing each residual on its lag, until rho stops changing. It converges in 11 iterations to rho hat 0.293, with 2.94, standard error 0.63, the intercept minus 37.08, and the afdec6 t statistic minus 1.69. Cochrane-Orcutt runs the same loop but drops the first transformed row, and gives rho 0.293 and 2.95. Third, confirm that the inverse-distribution-function draw equals SciPy's truncated-normal quantile function on 999 quantiles, and that draws from deep in the upper tail, above 8, stay valid. Fourth, the slide 27 sampler: each sweep builds the tridiagonal inverse in closed form, with no 200 by 200 inversion, draws beta and sigma squared from the exact Omega rho conditionals, then draws rho from the small-sin truncated normal on minus one to one, using the regression of each residual on its lag. The trap avoided: inverting Omega rho numerically at every sweep, when its inverse is known.

Support part five. Self-test: thirty true or false items.

This part covers the original self-test of the pitfalls note; no textbook exercise is reproduced. Scoring follows the course rule: the number correct minus half the number wrong. Difficulty is marked essential, medium or probing. After each statement, pause the player, decide true or false and say why, then resume for the answer.

Block A. General covariance and heteroskedasticity.

Item 1, essential. If P Omega P transposed is the identity, ordinary least squares on P y and P X equals the inverse of X transposed Omega inverse X, times X transposed Omega inverse y, for every such P.
Pause.
True. Omega inverse equals P transposed P, and only P transposed P appears in the ordinary least squares formula on the rotated data. Section 1, equations 4 and 5.

Item 2, essential. Under heteroskedasticity with known h i, ordinary least squares is biased, which is why weighted least squares is used.
Pause.
False. Ordinary least squares remains unbiased; weighted least squares is used for efficiency and for valid usual standard errors. Section 1, subsection 1, and Wooldridge printed page 255.

Item 3, medium. In the full conditional of beta, setting V zero inverse to zero gives a posterior mean equal to the generalized least squares estimator.
Pause.
True. Then V one inverse beta one equals sigma to the minus two X transposed Omega inverse y, and V one inverse equals sigma to the minus two X transposed Omega inverse X. Section 1, equation 8.

Item 4, medium. For h of z and alpha equal to the exponential of z transposed alpha, with a normal prior on alpha, the full conditional of alpha is normal.
Pause.
False. Alpha appears in minus one half the sum of z i transposed alpha, and inside e to the minus z i transposed alpha: no conjugate form, hence Metropolis-Hastings. Section 1, equation 12.

Item 5, probing. In feasible generalized least squares, the intercept of the regression of the log of u hat squared on x estimates the log of sigma squared consistently.
Pause.
False. It absorbs the expected value of the log of v, minus 1.27 for normal errors. That is harmless, because a constant factor in h cancels from weighted least squares. Section 1, subsection 4.1.

Item 6, medium. A weighted least squares standard error 40 per cent smaller than the robust ordinary least squares one proves the variance model is correct.
Pause.
False. The weighted standard error is computed assuming h equals income; if h is wrong, it is wrong too. Wooldridge printed page 257, and section 1, subsection 4.1.

Block B. Student t errors.

Item 7, essential. If epsilon given omega is normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared omega, and omega is inverse-gamma with parameters nu over two and nu over two, then epsilon is a t with nu degrees of freedom, location zero and scale sigma squared.
Pause.
True. Integrating the inverse-gamma kernel with shape nu plus one over two and scale nu plus epsilon squared over sigma squared, over two, leaves one plus epsilon squared over nu sigma squared, raised to the minus nu plus one over two. Section 2, equations 1 to 3.

Item 8, essential. If omega i inverse is inverse-gamma with parameters nu over two and nu over two, the marginal of epsilon i is a t with nu degrees of freedom.
Pause.
False. The reciprocal relation is the other way: omega i is inverse-gamma, equivalently omega i inverse is gamma. That is the slide 6 slip. Section 2, subsection 2.

Item 9, medium. In the Gibbs sampler, an observation with an absolute residual below sigma gets an expected weight above one.
Pause.
True. Nu plus one over nu plus epsilon i squared over sigma squared exceeds one if and only if epsilon i squared is below sigma squared. Section 2, equation 5.

Item 10, medium. The full conditional of nu is a gamma distribution when nu has an exponential prior.
Pause.
False. Nu enters through the bracket nu over two to the nu over two over gamma of nu over two, raised to the n; the conditional, section 2, equation 6, is non-standard, so Metropolis-Hastings.

Item 11, probing. With a flat prior on beta and nu exponential with mean 25, the posterior median of beta exists but the posterior mean may not.
Pause.
True. Geweke: the mean needs zero prior probability on nu at most two, and the exponential puts mass there; quantiles always exist. Section 2, subsection 4.

Item 12, medium. Under t errors with 5 degrees of freedom, the scale sigma squared equals the error variance.
Pause.
False. The variance is sigma squared times 5 over 3. Section 2, subsection 6.

Block C. First-order autoregressive errors.

Item 13, essential. With first-order autoregressive errors, the covariance of epsilon t and epsilon t plus k is rho to the k times sigma squared over one minus rho squared, for the absolute value of rho below one.
Pause.
True. Section 3, equations 1 and 2.

Item 14, medium. Omega rho is tridiagonal.
Pause.
False. Its inverse is tridiagonal; Omega rho itself is full, with entries rho to the absolute value of i minus j over one minus rho squared. Section 3, equation 3.

Item 15, medium. Cochrane-Orcutt and Prais-Winsten differ only in the treatment of the first observation.
Pause.
True. Prais-Winsten keeps the first observation of y and x multiplied by the square root of one minus rho squared; Cochrane-Orcutt drops it. Section 3, subsection 2, and Wooldridge printed page 383.

Item 16, probing. If the regressor is serially uncorrelated, autoregressive errors leave ordinary least squares both efficient and with correct usual standard errors.
Pause.
False. The standard error is asymptotically right, a ratio of one, but generalized least squares is still more efficient: 9.53 at rho equal to 0.9. Section 3, equations 10 and 11.

Item 17, medium. After conditioning on the first error, the full conditional of rho under a truncated-normal prior is a truncated normal.
Pause.
True. The likelihood becomes that of a regression of epsilon t on epsilon t minus one. Section 3, equation 13.

Block D. Hierarchical models.

Item 18, essential. In the individual-effects model with a hierarchical prior, the posterior mean of alpha i moves toward mu alpha more when T is small.
Pause.
True. The weight on the unit's own data, T V alpha over T V alpha plus sigma squared, falls as T falls. Section 4, equation 6.

Item 19, medium. In the update of mu alpha, the precision of each alpha i is sigma to the minus two.
Pause.
False. It is V alpha inverse: the slide 13 slip. Section 4, equation 7.

Item 20, medium. In the eight-schools model, every posterior mean of theta i given y and tau lies between y i and mu hat at that tau.
Pause.
True. It is the convex combination one minus B i times y i plus B i times mu hat. Section 4, equation 14.

Item 21, probing. Shrinkage always moves the extreme schools toward zero.
Pause.
False. Toward mu hat, about 8: school C, at minus 2.75, moves up. Section 4, subsection 5, and pitfall nine.

Item 22, medium. The individual-effects model with a vague non-hierarchical prior reproduces the dummy-variable, or fixed-effects, regression.
Pause.
True. Each alpha i is learned from its own unit only. Section 4, subsection 2, and Wooldridge printed page 438.

Item 23, probing. The site's 95 per cent band for woman 1 at x equal to 40.143 covers a new weight measurement with 95 per cent probability.
Pause.
False. It omits the sigma y noise: it is a band for the mean line. Section 4, subsection 6.

Block E. The Tobit.

Item 24, essential. In the Tobit, the probability that y is zero given x is one minus capital Phi of x transposed beta over sigma.
Pause.
True. Section 5, equation 2, and Wooldridge 17.21.

Item 25, medium. In the Tobit, the partial derivative of the expected value of y given x with respect to x j is beta j.
Pause.
False. It is beta j times capital Phi of x transposed beta over sigma, section 5, equation 12; beta is the latent-scale effect.

Item 26, medium. In Chib's data augmentation, the latent value of a censored observation is drawn from a normal truncated to the interval from minus infinity to zero.
Pause.
True. Section 5, equation 6.

Item 27, probing. With x normal with mean a and variance one, ordinary least squares of the censored y on x estimates beta times the share of uncensored observations.
Pause.
True. Stein's lemma: the covariance of x and the maximum of zero and y star equals the covariance of x and y star times the probability that y star is positive. Section 5, equation 14.

Block F. The probit family.

Item 28, essential. In the probit, the pair beta, sigma and the pair two beta, two sigma give the same likelihood, so sigma is fixed at one.
Pause.
True. Only x transposed beta over sigma enters. Section 6, equation 1.

Item 29, medium. In the ordered probit Gibbs sampler, each cut-point is drawn from a normal distribution.
Pause.
False. From a uniform between the largest latent value of its lower category and the smallest of its upper one. Section 6, equation 8.

Item 30, probing. In the multinomial probit, fixing one variance of Omega is needed even after differencing utilities from a base alternative.
Pause.
True. Differencing removes the location; c beta and c squared Omega still give the same choices. That is the scale. Section 6, subsection 4.


Support part six. Glossary.

One sentence per term, in the order the class meets them.

Non-spherical errors: errors whose covariance is sigma squared Omega rather than sigma squared times the identity, because they are heteroskedastic, correlated, or both.
Generalized least squares: ordinary least squares after a rotation P with P Omega P transposed equal to the identity, giving the inverse of X transposed Omega inverse X, times X transposed Omega inverse y.
Weighted least squares: generalized least squares with a diagonal Omega, which divides every column, the constant too, by the square root of h i.
Variance function: a positive function h of covariates z and parameters alpha that scales the error variance, usually the exponential of z transposed alpha.
Feasible generalized least squares: generalized least squares with Omega estimated first, for heteroskedasticity by regressing the log of the squared residuals on the covariates.
Heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors: ordinary least squares standard errors valid whatever Omega is, which fix inference but not efficiency.
Metropolis-Hastings step: a draw for a parameter with a non-standard conditional, proposed by a random walk and accepted with probability given by the posterior ratio.
Scale mixture of normals: writing the t as a normal whose variance multiplier omega i is itself drawn from an inverse gamma with parameters nu over two and nu over two.
Latent variance: the observation-specific multiplier omega i, a computational device integrated out by ignoring its draws.
Gibbs weight: the conditional expected value of omega i inverse, nu plus one over nu plus the squared standardized residual, below one exactly when the absolute residual exceeds sigma.
Scale against variance: in a t model sigma squared is a scale, and the variance is sigma squared times nu over nu minus two.
Exchangeability: the assumption that nothing distinguishes the units in advance, so their parameters are drawn independently from one distribution.
Geweke's conditions: with a flat prior on beta, the posterior mean of beta needs zero prior mass on nu at most two, and the posterior variance zero mass on nu at most four.
First-order autoregressive errors: errors with epsilon t equal to rho epsilon t minus one plus white noise, so the correlation at lag k is rho to the k.
Quasi-differencing: replacing y t by y t minus rho y t minus one, and x t likewise, which whitens first-order autoregressive errors.
Prais-Winsten: quasi-differencing that keeps the first observation multiplied by the square root of one minus rho squared.
Cochrane-Orcutt: quasi-differencing that drops the first observation.
Tridiagonal inverse: the inverse of Omega rho, nonzero only on the diagonal and the two neighbouring diagonals.
The small sin: conditioning on the first error, which makes the conditional of rho an exact truncated normal.
Inverse-distribution-function draw: drawing a truncated normal as mu plus sigma times capital Phi inverse of a uniform mixture of the bound probabilities.
Pooled model: one regression line for all units.
Individual-effects model: one intercept per unit with common slopes; with a vague prior it is the fixed-effects, or dummy-variable, regression.
Random-coefficients model: one full coefficient vector per unit.
Hierarchical prior: a prior on unit parameters whose mean and variance are themselves given priors and estimated.
Partial pooling: a unit's estimate as a weighted average of its own data and the population mean, with weight T V alpha over T V alpha plus sigma squared on its own data.
Shrinkage factor: B i, sigma i squared over sigma i squared plus tau squared, the share of the population mean in a school's conditional mean.
Borrowing strength: the gain in precision a unit with little or noisy data gets from the other units.
Wishart prior: the conjugate prior for the precision matrix of the unit coefficient vectors.
Random-intercept model: a regression with an additive unit effect of variance sigma a squared, which makes observations of the same unit equicorrelated.
Random-effects theta: the quasi-demeaning weight, one minus the square root of sigma u squared over sigma u squared plus T sigma a squared, between pooled and fixed effects.
Corner solution: an outcome at zero because zero is a genuine optimal choice.
Censoring: values beyond a limit are recorded at the limit, but the units stay in the sample.
Truncation: units beyond a limit are absent from the sample, x included.
Tobit: the regression y equal to the maximum of zero and x transposed beta plus a normal error.
Data augmentation: adding the hidden latent values to the unknowns so that every other conditional becomes standard.
Inverse Mills ratio: lambda of c, phi of c over capital Phi of c, the correction term in the conditional-on-positive mean.
Average partial effect scale factor: the average of capital Phi of x i transposed beta hat over sigma hat, 0.589 for the Mroz Tobit.
Stein's lemma: for jointly normal x and w, the covariance of x and g of w equals the covariance of x and w times the expected value of g prime of w.
Probit: the binary model with probability capital Phi of x transposed beta and the error variance fixed at one.
Identification of the scale: only beta over sigma is seen through a cut, so a variance must be fixed.
The 1.6 rule: logit coefficients are about 1.6 times probit coefficients, from the density peaks 0.399 and one quarter.
Albert-Chib sampler: the probit Gibbs sampler that draws latent values truncated by the observed category and then beta by a normal regression.
Ordered probit: a latent normal regression cut into ordered categories at increasing cut-points.
Cut-point: a threshold gamma j separating two ordered categories, drawn from a uniform in the Gibbs sampler.
Multinomial probit: a choice model in which the alternative with the largest latent utility is chosen, written as a latent seemingly unrelated regression in utility differences.
Seemingly unrelated regression: a system of regressions with correlated errors, estimated by generalized least squares with the identity Kronecker Sigma.
Truncated multivariate normal: a multivariate normal restricted to the region consistent with the observed choice, drawn one coordinate at a time.
Multivariate probit: several binary decisions with correlated latent errors, whose covariance is a correlation matrix.
Odds ratio: e to the logit coefficient, the multiplicative change in the odds per unit of x, a reading the probit does not have.


Support part seven. The whole class in five lines.

One. Every model of Class 7 is the normal linear regression with one twist, and one conditional does all the work: beta given the rest is normal, with precision V zero inverse plus sigma to the minus two X transposed Omega inverse X; only what else is drawn around it changes.

Two. A known Omega is a rotation and diagonal Omega is weighted least squares, 0.787 with standard error 0.063 against 0.821 with standard error 0.104 in Wooldridge's Example 8.6; a variance function costs one Metropolis step, and ignoring it costs ordinary least squares a factor of 4.38.

Three.
