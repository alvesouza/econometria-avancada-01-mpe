The claim, before the derivations. When the treatment x shares unobserved causes with the outcome y, ordinary least squares converges to beta one plus a confounding term. An instrument z that shifts x, which is relevance, delta one not equal to zero, and touches y only through x, which is exclusion, the covariance of z and u equal to zero, identifies beta one as the covariance of z and y over the covariance of z and x: the reduced form over the first stage. With a binary instrument this is the Wald estimator; with covariates it is two-stage least squares. Simply adding z to the outcome regression does not work. The price is variance, since the instrumental-variables standard error is one over the absolute correlation of x and z times larger, and fragility, since a weak first stage magnifies any exclusion violation. In Card's data: least squares 0.0747, instrumental variables 0.1315 with standard error 0.0550, and a first-stage F of 13.26.

From here on, IV means instrumental variables, OLS means ordinary least squares, and two-stage least squares is said in full.

The notation, the slides', with MHE and Wooldridge letters where they differ. x sub i is the treatment, schooling; MHE calls it s sub i and Wooldridge y two. y sub i is the outcome, the log wage. z sub i is the instrument, for i from one to n. The outcome equation is y sub i equals beta zero plus beta one x sub i plus epsilon y sub i. The treatment, or first-stage, equation is x sub i equals delta zero plus delta one z sub i plus epsilon x sub i. The two errors are bivariate normal with mean zero, variances sigma squared of x and sigma squared of y, and covariance sigma x y, which equals rho sigma x sigma y. z sub i is independent of both errors, and sigma squared of z is its variance. The reduced form is y sub i equals theta zero plus theta one z sub i plus u sub i, with u sub i normal with variance tau squared. Phi is a direct effect of z on y, an exclusion violation, used in point six and in the companion; p is the probability that z equals one for a binary instrument. In MHE, rho is the causal schooling coefficient of equation 4.1.2; in this note rho is always the error correlation, and MHE's coefficient is written beta one.

Five point one. The intuition: the IV graph and the examples. Slide page 17.

The graph: the instrument goes into the treatment T, the treatment goes into the outcome O, and an unmeasured confounder C goes into both T and O. The slide: "IV: Instrumental variable; T: Treatment, Program, Policy, Risk factors; C: Unmeasured confounders; O: Outcome." The instrument opens a causal path into T and has no arrow into O or into C.

The slide's table of examples, read row by row as confounder, treatment, outcome and instrument. First: maternal characteristics; smoking during pregnancy; low birth weight; cigarette taxes. Second: smoking, caffeine and alcohol; body mass index; Parkinson's disease; a variant of the FTO gene. Third: prognostic factors; catheter use; mortality; the share of patients with a catheter at the facility. Fourth: ability; education; earnings; mother's education. Fifth: proximity; a tutoring programme; grade point average; library hours.

The path from the instrument through T to O is a chain, and no path from the instrument to O through anything else exists. The instrument and the outcome are dependent only through T. That is the whole argument.

Five point two. Course layer: the two-equation system and the IV ratio. Slides pages 17 and 18.

Equation 1: y sub i equals beta zero plus beta one x sub i plus epsilon y sub i. Equation 2: x sub i equals delta zero plus delta one z sub i plus epsilon x sub i. The slide's two switches: rho equal to zero means no endogeneity; delta one equal to zero means no instrument.

Why OLS fails. The OLS slope of y on x converges to the covariance of x and y over the variance of x. From equation 2, the variance of x is delta one squared sigma squared of z plus sigma squared of x, and the covariance of x with epsilon y equals the covariance of the two errors, rho sigma x sigma y. Substitute equation 1 into the covariance. Equation 3: the probability limit of the OLS beta one equals the covariance of x with beta zero plus beta one x plus epsilon y, over the variance of x, which is beta one plus rho sigma x sigma y over delta one squared sigma squared of z plus sigma squared of x.

The reduced form. Substitute equation 2 into equation 1, slide page 18. y sub i equals beta zero plus beta one times delta zero plus delta one z sub i plus epsilon x sub i, plus epsilon y sub i. Collect: beta zero plus beta one delta zero, plus beta one delta one times z sub i, plus beta one epsilon x sub i plus epsilon y sub i. So, equation 4: theta zero equals beta zero plus beta one delta zero; theta one equals beta one delta one; and tau squared equals beta one squared sigma squared of x plus sigma squared of y plus two beta one rho sigma x sigma y.

The reduced-form error, beta one epsilon x sub i plus epsilon y sub i, is independent of z sub i. So OLS of y on z is consistent for theta one, and OLS of x on z is consistent for delta one.

The two-stage estimator, slide page 18, in three steps. First, obtain delta one hat from the treatment equation. Second, obtain theta one hat from the outcome-on-instrument regression. Third, since theta one equals beta one delta one by equation 4, with delta one not zero: equation 5, beta one hat IV equals theta one hat over delta one hat. That is the estimated covariance of z and y over the estimated variance of z, divided by the estimated covariance of z and x over the estimated variance of z. The variances cancel, leaving the estimated covariance of z and y over the estimated covariance of z and x. In words: the effect of z on y over the effect of z on x.

The covariance route, Wooldridge equation 15.9, page 465, and MHE equation 4.1.3, page 86. Take the covariance of equation 1 with z: the covariance of z and y equals beta one times the covariance of z and x plus the covariance of z and epsilon y. Exogeneity sets the last term to zero, and relevance makes the covariance of z and x, which is delta one sigma squared of z, non-zero. So, equation 6: beta one equals the covariance of z and y over the covariance of z and x.

The sample analogue, Wooldridge equation 15.10, is beta one hat equals the sum of z sub i minus z bar times y sub i minus y bar, over the sum of z sub i minus z bar times x sub i minus x bar. With z equal to x it is OLS, Wooldridge page 465.

Two assumptions, one checkable. Relevance, delta one not zero, is a regression of x on z: "something you can check in the data", MHE page 86. Exclusion, the covariance of z and epsilon y equal to zero, involves the unobserved error and is argued, not tested. MHE adds that exclusion "really has two parts": z as good as randomly assigned, and no effect on y except through x, page 86.

The slide's page 18 sentence, "z into x into y is a fork", describes a chain. The conclusion, y independent of z given x when rho equals zero, is Rule 1 of section 1.

Five point three. Course layer: why adding z to the outcome regression does not work. Slides pages 18 and 19, and Greenberg page 173.

Three steps. First, condition one error on the other. For the bivariate normal pair, slide page 18, equation 7: epsilon y sub i given epsilon x sub i is normal with mean sigma x y over sigma squared of x times epsilon x sub i, and variance sigma squared of y times one minus rho squared. Equivalently, epsilon y sub i equals sigma x y over sigma squared of x times epsilon x sub i, plus omega sub i, with omega sub i normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared of y times one minus rho squared.

Second, replace epsilon x sub i by its value from equation 2, x sub i minus delta zero minus delta one z sub i. Then epsilon y sub i equals sigma x y over sigma squared of x times x sub i minus delta zero minus delta one z sub i, plus omega sub i.

Third, substitute into equation 1 and collect, slide page 19. Equation 8: y sub i equals beta zero minus sigma x y over sigma squared of x times delta zero; plus beta one plus sigma x y over sigma squared of x, times x sub i; plus minus sigma x y over sigma squared of x times delta one, times z sub i; plus omega sub i.

Omega sub i is independent of x sub i and z sub i, so equation 8 is the population regression of y on x and z. The coefficient on x is beta one plus sigma x y over sigma squared of x, biased whenever rho is not zero. The slide: "In words, by simply including the instrumental variable z sub i in the outcome regression will not suffice to estimate beta one", page 19. Greenberg, section 11.2, page 173, reaches the same expression and concludes that from y given x alone "beta s is not identifiable. We have no way of separating beta s from beta s plus sigma 12 over sigma 22." The instrument must be used through the first stage, not beside it.

Five point four. Course layer: the Wald estimator. MHE section 4.1.2, pages 94 to 96.

Take a binary z with the probability that z equals one equal to p, and the model y sub i equals alpha plus beta one s sub i plus eta sub i, equation 4.1.11.

The covariance with a dummy. The expected value of y z is p times the expected value of y given z equal to one. The expected value of y is p times the expected value of y given z equal to one, plus one minus p times the expected value of y given z equal to zero. So the covariance of y and z, the expected value of y z minus the expected value of y times the expected value of z, equals p times the mean of y at z equal to one, minus p times the quantity p times the mean at one plus one minus p times the mean at zero. That simplifies to the mean of y at z equal to one minus the mean at z equal to zero, times p times one minus p.

The same holds for the covariance of s and z. Substitute both into equation 6; p times one minus p cancels, MHE equation 4.1.12. Equation 9: beta one equals the expected value of y sub i given z sub i equal to one minus the expected value given z sub i equal to zero, over the expected value of s sub i given z sub i equal to one minus the expected value given z sub i equal to zero.

The direct route, equation 4.1.13: the expected value of y sub i given z sub i equals alpha plus beta one times the expected value of s sub i given z sub i, because the expected value of eta sub i given z sub i is zero. Take the difference at z equal to one and z equal to zero, and divide.

The worked example, MHE Table 4.1.2, page 96: Angrist and Krueger's quarter of birth, the 1980 census, 329,509 men. The log weekly wage is 5.8916 for those born in the first or second quarter and 5.9051 for those born in the third or fourth, a difference of minus 0.01349, standard error 0.00337. Years of education are 12.6881 and 12.8394, a difference of minus 0.1514, standard error 0.0162. The Wald estimate is minus 0.01349 over minus 0.1514, which is 0.0891, standard error 0.0210, against OLS of 0.0703, standard error 0.0005.

On the Card data without controls: the mean log wage is 6.311401 near a college and 6.155494 not near; mean schooling is 13.527034 and 12.698015. So the Wald estimate is 6.311401 minus 6.155494, over 13.527034 minus 12.698015, which is 0.155907 over 0.829019, which is 0.1881, against a no-controls OLS of 0.0521, from the Python note, section 5. The controls of point seven matter: region and race shift both college proximity and wages.

Five point five. Course layer: two-stage least squares. MHE section 4.1.1, pages 89 to 91, and Wooldridge sections 15.2 and 15.3, pages 471 to 477.

With exogenous covariates X sub i, Wooldridge's z one to z k minus one, and an instrument z sub i, equation 10 has two parts. The structural equation, MHE 4.1.6: y sub i equals X sub i transposed alpha plus beta one s sub i plus eta sub i. The first stage: s sub i equals X sub i transposed pi one zero plus pi one one z sub i plus xi one i.

Substitute the first stage into the structural equation, MHE 4.1.7. y sub i equals X sub i transposed alpha plus beta one times X sub i transposed pi one zero plus pi one one z sub i plus xi one i, plus eta sub i. Collect: X sub i transposed times alpha plus beta one pi one zero, plus beta one pi one one times z sub i, plus beta one xi one i plus eta sub i.

That is the reduced form, with z coefficient pi two one equal to beta one pi one one, so beta one equals pi two one over pi one one: indirect least squares, MHE 4.1.5. Two-stage least squares: regress s sub i on X sub i and z sub i, form the fitted s hat sub i, then regress y sub i on X sub i and s hat sub i, MHE 4.1.9. With one instrument, two-stage least squares, IV and indirect least squares are exactly equal; the Python note, section 5, checks all three on the Card data. The second-stage OLS standard errors are wrong, because they use y minus beta hat times s hat instead of y minus beta hat times s; software computes the right ones, MHE page 91.

The requirements in Wooldridge's statement, equations 15.29 to 15.31: the covariance of each exogenous variable with the structural error is zero, and pi k is not zero, where pi k is the partial correlation of the excluded instrument with y two given the included exogenous variables.

Five point six. Course layer: the price and the fragility of IV. Wooldridge pages 466 to 470, and 478.

The asymptotic variance, under the expected value of u squared given z equal to sigma squared, Wooldridge equation 15.12. Equation 11: the asymptotic variance of the IV beta one is sigma squared over n times sigma squared of x times rho x z squared. The estimated standard error, equation 15.13, is the square root of sigma hat squared over the total sum of squares of x times the R squared of x on z.

OLS has sigma squared over the total sum of squares of x; IV divides further by an R squared that is less than one. "The IV variance is always larger than the OLS variance (when OLS is valid)", Wooldridge page 466. The ratio of standard errors is one over the absolute correlation of x and z.

The bias with an imperfect instrument, Wooldridge equations 15.19 and 15.20, page 469. Equation 12: the probability limit of the IV beta one is beta one plus the correlation of z and u over the correlation of z and x, times sigma u over sigma x. The probability limit of the OLS beta one is beta one plus the correlation of x and u times sigma u over sigma x.

A small correlation of z and u divided by a small correlation of z and x can exceed the OLS bias. With the correlation of z and x equal to 0.2, IV is less biased only if the correlation of z and u is under one fifth of the correlation of x and u, Wooldridge page 470; the book's sentence repeats "correlation of z and u" where it means the correlation of x and u. In the companion's parametrisation, a direct effect phi of z on y gives a probability limit of the IV estimator equal to beta one plus phi over delta one.

When relevance fails. Wooldridge Example 15.3, page 470: mother's packs per day regressed on the cigarette price gives a coefficient of 0.0003, standard error 0.0008, with an R squared of 0.0000. Using the price as an instrument anyway gives a packs coefficient of plus 2.99, standard error 8.70, which is "meaningless because cigprice fails the one requirement of an IV that we can always test". This is also the slide's example C, page 19.

Weak instruments. The Staiger and Stock, and Stock and Yogo, rule, Wooldridge page 478: proceed with standard IV inference if the first-stage absolute t statistic exceeds the square root of 10, about 3.2, with one instrument, or if the F statistic exceeds 10 with several. And it is "not the overall F statistic": only the excluded instruments count.

The curves are exact probability limits, calibrated to the Card data's moments after partialling out the site's 14 controls, so the marked points reproduce 0.0747 and 0.1315. On the left, the probability limits are plotted against the error correlation rho from minus 0.9 to 0.9. IV is a flat line at 0.1315. OLS and the long regression of y on x and z are rising lines that nearly coincide, crossing IV at rho equal to zero. The Card point sits at rho equal to minus 0.285, with OLS at 0.0747. On the right, the absolute asymptotic bias is plotted against the first-stage coefficient delta one, with a direct effect phi of 0.005. The IV bias, phi over delta one, explodes as delta one goes to zero, while the OLS bias stays near 0.057. The F equal to 10 boundary, at delta one equal to 0.276, and Card's 0.320 are marked.

How to read it. On the left, IV is flat because it does not depend on rho; OLS crosses it only at rho equal to zero. The long regression is no better than OLS. Here they almost coincide, because the instrument explains only delta one squared sigma squared of z, which is 0.017, of a schooling variance of 3.746. The Card point at rho equal to minus 0.285 means the data imply OLS below IV. On the right, with an exclusion violation of only 0.005 log points, IV is less biased than OLS to the right of delta one equal to about 0.09, and much worse to the left. Card's first stage, 0.320, is barely past the F equal to 10 line, which is at 0.276 with 3,010 observations.

Five point eight. G layer: heterogeneous effects, the local average treatment effect. MHE section 4.4.1, Theorem 4.4.1, page 114. Not examinable beyond the statement.

With individual effects and potential treatments d one i and d zero i, the treatment status if z sub i is one or zero, assume four things. A1, independence: z sub i is independent of all potential outcomes and treatments. A2, exclusion: y sub i of d and zero equals y sub i of d and one. A3, a first stage: the expected value of d one i minus d zero i is not zero. A4, monotonicity: d one i is at least d zero i for all i, or the reverse for all i. Then the expected value of y sub i given z sub i equal to one minus that given z sub i equal to zero, over the expected value of d sub i given z sub i equal to one minus that given z sub i equal to zero, equals the expected value of y one i minus y zero i given d one i greater than d zero i.

That is the average effect on compliers, those whose treatment the instrument changes. It is proved in MHE pages 114 and 115, but it is not on the slides. For Card, the compliers are men whose schooling depended on having a college nearby, plausibly those with high marginal returns.

Bayesian IV, also G layer: Greenberg section 11.2, pages 173 and 174, has the system of equations 11.9 and 11.10 with a Wishart prior and a Gibbs sampler; slide page 20 lists Hahn, He and Lopes 2018, and Lopes and Polson 2014. These are tools of Classes 3 and 4, named only.

Recap of section 5. OLS under endogeneity converges to beta one plus rho sigma x sigma y over the variance of x. An instrument that is relevant and excluded identifies beta one as the reduced form over the first stage, the covariance of z and y over the covariance of z and x. Adding z to the outcome regression leaves the bias sigma x y over sigma squared of x. With a binary instrument the ratio is the Wald estimator, 0.0891 in MHE Table 4.1.2; with covariates it is two-stage least squares. Part three, section 6. Difference in differences: subtract the control group's change, and assume it is the treated group's counterfactual change.

The claim, before the derivations. A before and after comparison of the treated mixes the policy with everything else that changed over time. A treated and control comparison mixes it with permanent group differences. The difference of the two differences removes both, under one assumption: without the policy, both groups would have moved in parallel, MHE equation 5.2.1. The four cell means and the saturated regression with an interaction, MHE 5.2.3, give the same number. On the site's Card and Krueger file, New Jersey's employment rose by 0.467 full-time equivalents while Pennsylvania's fell by 2.283, so the difference in differences is 2.750, with standard error 1.731 and t statistic 1.59: no sign that the higher minimum wage cut employment.

From here on, DiD means difference in differences, and FTE means full-time-equivalent employment.

The notation. y sub i s t is the FTE employment of store i in state s and period t. T sub i, the treatment variable, is one for New Jersey, the treated state, and zero for Pennsylvania, the control. P sub t, the time variable, is one for November 1992, after, and zero for February 1992, before. y bar sub T t is the mean of group T in period t. In MHE, gamma sub s is the state effect, lambda sub t the period effect, and d sub s t the treatment dummy, equal to T sub i times P sub t. MHE calls the causal effect beta in equation 5.2.2; here it is written delta, to keep beta for the regression of equation 6. Wooldridge uses d T, d 2, delta zero, beta one and delta one in equation 13.10. And capital Delta is a differential trend, treated minus control, that would have occurred without the policy.

Six point one. The intuition: two back doors, one design. Slides pages 21 and 22.

The slides follow Huntington-Klein. An office switches from cubicles to an open plan on 1 January 2017, and productivity Y rises, but "the economy also improved from 2016 to 2017", page 21. In a graph with X into Y, and Time into both X and Y, the before and after difference picks up "the way that Y may have changed over Time for reasons unrelated to Treatment". Adding a never-treated control group closes the Time back door but opens another. Treatment T now also depends on being in the treated group, and the groups may differ: T comes out of Time and Y comes out of Time; T comes out of In Treated Group and Y comes out of In Treated Group, page 22. "We can close both back-door paths through Time and In Treated Group using Difference-in-Differences": difference within group over time, then across groups.

Six point two. Course layer: the identifying assumption. MHE section 5.2, page 170.

The potential outcomes are y one i s t, under a high minimum wage, and y zero i s t, under a low one. Common trends, equation 1: the expected value of y zero i s t given s and t equals gamma sub s plus lambda sub t.

In MHE's words: "In the absence of a minimum wage change, employment is determined by the sum of a time-invariant state effect and a year effect that is common across states." With a constant effect, the expected value of y one i s t minus y zero i s t given s and t equal to delta, equation 2: y sub i s t equals gamma sub s plus lambda sub t plus delta d sub s t plus epsilon sub i s t, with the expected value of epsilon given s and t equal to zero.

Three steps.

First, the control state's change. Pennsylvania is never treated, so d is zero in both periods. From equation 2, equation 3: the expected value of y in Pennsylvania in November minus the expected value in Pennsylvania in February equals gamma for Pennsylvania plus lambda for November, minus gamma for Pennsylvania plus lambda for February, which is lambda November minus lambda February.

Second, the treated state's change. New Jersey has d equal to one in November only. Equation 4: the expected value in New Jersey in November minus that in New Jersey in February equals gamma for New Jersey plus lambda November plus delta, minus gamma for New Jersey plus lambda February, which is lambda November minus lambda February plus delta.

Third, equation 4 minus equation 3. The state effects cancelled inside each change, and the common trend cancels now. Equation 5: the New Jersey change minus the Pennsylvania change equals delta.

MHE prints the population difference on page 170 with the brackets in the opposite order, the Pennsylvania change minus the New Jersey change, which as written equals minus beta, not beta. The order in equation 5 is the one MHE uses for the regression parameter on page 175.

The sentence to remember: "The key identifying assumption here is that employment trends would be the same in both states in the absence of treatment", MHE page 171. Groups may differ in level, gamma sub s; they may not differ in trend. Like ignorability, this is about an unobserved counterfactual: New Jersey's November employment without the increase.

Six point three. Course layer: the slides' two strategies and the regression. Slide page 22, and Wooldridge Table 13.3.

Strategy one: the change of the treated over time minus the change of the control over time. Strategy two: the treated minus control gap after, minus the treated minus control gap before. They are the same four numbers rearranged. Wooldridge page 410: "the estimate does not depend on how we do the differencing".

The regression, slide page 22, Wooldridge equation 13.10, and MHE equation 5.2.3. Equation 6: y sub t i equals beta zero plus beta one P sub t plus beta two T sub i plus beta three P sub t T sub i, plus x sub t i transposed gamma, plus epsilon sub t i.

The cell means without covariates. Set P sub t and T sub i to zero or one in equation 6, and walk the four cells. The control group before: beta zero. The control group after: beta zero plus beta one. So the control's change is beta one. The treated group before: beta zero plus beta two. The treated group after: beta zero plus beta one plus beta two plus beta three. So the treated's change is beta one plus beta three. The treated minus control gap is beta two before and beta two plus beta three after. And the difference of differences, either way, is beta three.

This is Wooldridge Table 13.3, page 411, where Wooldridge's delta zero is our beta one, his beta one is our beta two, and his delta one is our beta three. Four cells, four parameters: the model is saturated. So least squares reproduces the four sample means exactly, and equation 7, Wooldridge equation 13.11: beta three hat equals y bar one one minus y bar one zero, minus the quantity y bar zero one minus y bar zero zero.

The regression adds standard errors, more groups and periods, and covariates, slide page 23.

Six point four. Course layer: the worked example, the site's Card and Krueger file.

Part three, section 7. Regression discontinuity: when a rule switches treatment on at a cutoff, the jump in the outcome is the effect at the cutoff.

This section covers slides pages 26 to 38, section 11, after Angrist and Pischke, Mastering 'Metrics, chapter 4, pages 147 to 164: birthdays and funerals, pages 26 and 27; the treatment indicator and sharp regression discontinuity, page 28; the regression of y a on alpha plus rho D a plus gamma a, with rho hat equal to 7.7, page 29; nonlinearity mistaken for a discontinuity, page 30; linear against quadratic with interactions, page 31; the R script replicating Figure 4.5, page 33; the Bayesian version, pages 34 and 35; nonparametric regression discontinuity, page 36; the NBA draft, pages 36 and 37; and the references, page 38. It covers the site's example on age, motor-vehicle accidents and internal causes, with its data file of 48 monthly cells. And it covers MHE section 6.1, pages 189 to 195, equations 6.1.1 to 6.1.7, and section 6.2, pages 196 to 198, fuzzy regression discontinuity, equation 6.2.5, G layer only.

From here on, RD means regression discontinuity, MLDA means the minimum legal drinking age, and MVA means motor-vehicle accidents.

The claim, before the derivations. In a sharp design, treatment is a deterministic, discontinuous function of a running variable: D sub a equals one when a is at least 21. So treated and untreated never share a value of a. Ignorability holds trivially, while positivity fails by construction. Identification comes instead from continuity: if the expected value of y zero given a is smooth at the cutoff, the jump in the expected value of y given a there is the expected value of y one minus y zero at a equal to a zero, MHE equation 6.1.7. Estimating it means modelling the trend on each side, linear, quadratic with interactions, or locally linear in a window, and the trend model is where RD goes wrong: curvature can masquerade as a jump. On the site's MLDA data, motor-vehicle deaths jump by 4.53 per 100,000 at 21, standard error 0.77, or 3.41 residual standard deviations; deaths from internal causes do not, 0.39 with standard error 0.60.

The notation, the slides', with MHE's in parentheses where it differs. a, MHE's x sub i, is the running variable, age in years. a zero, MHE's x zero, is the cutoff, 21. D sub a, MHE's d sub i, is the treatment, legal access to alcohol. y sub a, MHE's y sub i, is the death rate per 100,000 in the 30-day cell at age a; y zero and y one are the potential outcomes without and with treatment. Rho is the jump, the causal effect at the cutoff. Gamma, MHE's beta, is the slope of the trend; gamma one, gamma two, delta one and delta two are the quadratic and interaction terms of slide page 31. b, MHE's capital Delta, is the bandwidth, the half-width of the window from a zero minus b to a zero plus b. And sigma hat is the residual standard deviation.

Seven point one. The intuition: birthdays and funerals. Slides pages 26 to 28.

"Human behavior is constrained by rules", page 26: class sizes capped at 32, pensions from age 62, and the United States minimum legal drinking age of 21. Daily deaths of Americans aged 20 to 22, from 1997 to 2003, spike on and right after the 21st birthday: "This spike adds about 100 deaths to a baseline level of about 150 per day", page 27. By month of age, "few rates to the left of the age-21 cutoff are above 95, few of those to the right are below 95", page 28.

Seven point two. Course layer: the sharp design. Slide page 28, and MHE section 6.1, page 189.

Equation 1: D sub a equals one if a is at least 21, and zero if a is below 21. In MHE, equation 6.1.1, d sub i is the indicator that x sub i is at least x zero.

The slide's three properties. Treatment is a deterministic function of a: "once we know a, we know D sub a". It is a discontinuous one: "no matter how close a gets to the cutoff, D sub a remains unchanged until the cutoff is reached". And a is the running variable. Sharp RD: "treatment switches cleanly off or on as the running variable passes a cutoff". The site's code sets the over-21 indicator to one where age is strictly greater than 21; no cell sits exactly at 21, so strictly greater and greater-or-equal agree.

Why regression still matters, slide page 29. "Mortality clearly changes with the running variable, a, for reasons unrelated to the MLDA": internal-cause deaths rise through the late teens, external-cause deaths fall. "RD gets its name from the practice of using regression models to implement this control."

Seven point three. Course layer: the linear model. Slide page 29, and MHE equation 6.1.2, page 190.

Assume a linear untreated trend and a constant effect, MHE page 190. Equation 2: the expected value of y zero given a is alpha plus gamma a, and y one equals y zero plus rho.

The observed mean. By equation 1, the observed outcome is y equals y zero plus y one minus y zero times D sub a, which is y zero plus rho D sub a. Take expectations given a and substitute equation 2. Equation 3: the expected value of y sub a given a equals alpha plus gamma a plus rho D sub a, so y sub a equals alpha plus rho D sub a plus gamma a plus epsilon sub a.

The slide: "The negative slope, captured by gamma, reflects smoothly declining death rates among young people as they mature. The parameter rho captures the jump in deaths at age 21. The regression generates an estimate of rho equal to 7.7", page 29. That is for all deaths in Mastering 'Metrics. The site's file has the two components of Figure 4.5, motor-vehicle accidents and internal causes, worked in point six.

What makes equation 3 identify anything. D sub a is perfectly determined by a, yet it is not collinear with it. RD "distinguishes the nonlinear and discontinuous function, the indicator that x sub i is at least x zero, from the smooth and (in this case) linear function, x sub i", MHE page 190. Everything rests on the trend being correctly modelled on both sides.

Seven point four. Course layer: identification without functional form. MHE equation 6.1.7, page 193.

If the expected values of y zero and of y one given a are continuous at a zero, then just below the cutoff everyone is untreated, and just above everyone is treated. So the expected value of y for a between a zero minus Delta and a zero is approximately the expected value of y zero at a equal to a zero; and the expected value of y for a between a zero and a zero plus Delta is approximately the expected value of y one at a equal to a zero.

Subtract and let the window shrink. Equation 4: the limit, as Delta goes to zero, of the expected value of y just above the cutoff minus the expected value of y just below it equals the expected value of y one minus y zero at a equal to a zero.

The estimand is an average effect at the cutoff, valid without the constant-effect assumption of equation 2, MHE page 193, and valid only there: RD says nothing about 25-year-olds. Since no age has both treated and untreated cells, RD "turns on our willingness to extrapolate across covariate values, at least in a neighborhood of the discontinuity", MHE page 190.

Seven point five. Course layer: polynomials and interactions. Slides pages 30 and 31, and MHE equations 6.1.4 to 6.1.6, page 192.

Different trends on each side. Model both conditional means with polynomials in a tilde, which is a minus a zero. The expected value of y zero given a is alpha plus gamma one a tilde plus gamma two a tilde squared. The expected value of y one given a is alpha plus rho plus gamma one superscript one times a tilde plus gamma two superscript one times a tilde squared.

Combine with the identity: the expected value of y given a equals the expected value of y zero given a, plus the expected value of y one minus y zero given a, times D sub a, MHE page 192. That gives alpha plus gamma one a tilde plus gamma two a tilde squared, plus D sub a times the quantity rho plus gamma one superscript one minus gamma one, times a tilde, plus gamma two superscript one minus gamma two, times a tilde squared.

Name the differences delta k equals gamma k superscript one minus gamma k. That is the slide's quadratic regression with interactions, page 31. Equation 5: y sub a equals alpha plus rho D sub a, plus gamma one times a minus a zero, plus gamma two times a minus a zero squared, plus delta one times a minus a zero times D sub a, plus delta two times a minus a zero squared times D sub a, plus epsilon sub a.

Centring at a zero makes rho the jump at the cutoff. At a tilde equal to some c greater than zero, the effect is rho plus delta one c plus delta two c squared, MHE page 192. The site's code does not centre. It regresses on age, age squared, the over-21 indicator times age, and the over-21 indicator times age squared, and recovers the jump as the right limit minus the left limit: the over-21 coefficient, plus 21 times the coefficient on over-21 times age, plus 441 times the coefficient on over-21 times age squared. It is the same number.

The danger, slide page 30, and MHE Figure 6.1.1, panel C, page 191: "nonlinearity mistaken for a discontinuity". A sharp turn in a smooth expected value of y zero, fitted with straight lines, produces a spurious jump. Gelman and Imbens, 2019, cited on slide page 38, warn the other way: high-order global polynomials give erratic jumps. Hence windows, point seven.

Seven point six. Course layer: the worked example, the site's MLDA data.

There are 48 monthly cells, ages 19.07 to 22.93, deaths per 100,000. These are fits on all cells, and the Python note, section 7, reproduces every number.

The linear model, equation 3, for MVA: the fitted MVA rate is 95.4814 minus 3.1488 a plus 4.534 D sub a. The two fitted limits at 21: the left limit is 95.4814 minus 3.1488 times 21, which is 95.4814 minus 66.1248, which is 29.3566. The right limit is 29.3566 plus 4.534, which is 33.8906.

Now the four fits, one at a time, each with the jump, its standard error, the residual standard deviation, the jump in residual standard deviations, which the site labels "stdevs", and the t statistic.

The readings. Three of them.

First, MVA jumps, robustly. Linear and quadratic agree, 4.53 against 4.66. The curvature terms cost precision, the standard error going from 0.77 to 1.15, without moving the estimate. A 4.5-point rise on a left limit of 29.4 is about 15 per cent.

Second, internal causes do not jump, on the full sample. Deaths from disease should not respond to legal drinking, so a jump of 0.39, standard error 0.60, there is a built-in placebo outcome that supports the design. The quadratic gives 1.07, and some narrow windows give more, as point seven shows.

Third, the slide's "stdevs" legend divides the jump by the residual standard deviation, a scale-free size; it is not a t statistic. In the slide's page 33 code, the linear legend for the internal causes uses delta over sigma one, which is the MVA jump over the internal-cause standard deviation, 4.394. The site page corrects it to delta one over sigma one, which is 0.380.

Seven point seven. Course layer: local linear regression and the bandwidth. Slide page 36, and MHE page 193.

The slide, page 36: "For observations in the window from a zero minus b to a zero plus b, for small b, nonlinear trends need not concern us at all. Strategy: comparing averages in a narrow window just to the left and just to the right of the cutoff. High variance: very narrow window, few observations, too imprecise estimates. Trade-off: bias reduction near the boundary against the increased variance." The estimator is equation 3, or equation 5 with linear terms only, on the cells whose distance from a zero is at most b. MHE, page 193, describes it as weighted least squares with more weight near the cutoff; the uniform window is the simplest kernel.

MVA, linear with separate slopes, by bandwidth, from the Python note, section 7. With b equal to 0.5 years: jump 4.772, standard error 1.291. With b equal to 1.0: jump 4.759, standard error 1.123. With b equal to 2.0, which is all cells: jump 4.534, standard error 0.751.

Placebo cutoffs, with the same estimator and b equal to one, at ages where no rule changes. At a zero equal to 20: 0.828, standard error 1.096. At a zero equal to 22: minus 0.103, standard error 1.106. Neither is distinguishable from zero.

The jump survives every specification. Narrowing the window from all 48 cells to one year on either side moves rho hat by a quarter of a death and widens the standard error by half; fake cutoffs show nothing. That stability, not any one number, is the RD evidence. MHE page 193: "Hopefully, as you zero in on x zero with fewer and fewer controls, the estimated effect of d sub i remains stable."

The figure for this section. On the left are the monthly death rates against age from 19 to 23: motor-vehicle accidents near 27 to 36, and internal causes near 16 to 24, with circles left of 21 and squares to the right. Solid linear fits and dashed quadratic-with-interactions fits are drawn on each side; this is the site's two figures in one. The MVA fit jumps up at 21; the internal-cause fit barely moves. How to read it. On the left, the discontinuity is visible by eye for MVA and absent for internal causes, and the two specifications nearly coincide. On the right, the bias and variance trade-off of slide page 36 is literal: narrow windows give wide bands. The MVA band stays well above zero at every bandwidth; its lower edge never falls below 1.5. The internal-cause band covers zero at most bandwidths, but not for b between 0.90 and 1.10, which is 22 to 26 cells, where rho hat is about 1.6 to 1.9 with a lower edge of 0.17 to 0.28. Scanning 33 bandwidths of a noisy placebo outcome turns up a "significant" window. Report the whole curve, not the bandwidth that looks best.

Seven point eight. G layer: beyond the sharp parametric design. Not examinable. Three items.

Second, fuzzy RD is IV, MHE section 6.2, pages 196 to 198. When crossing the cutoff changes only the probability of treatment, the jump in y divided by the jump in the probability that d equals one given x, MHE equation 6.2.5, is a Wald estimator, as in section 5, point four, with the indicator of x at least x zero as the instrument. Recap of section 7. In a sharp design, treatment switches on at a cutoff of the running variable, so positivity fails and identification rests on continuity of the untreated mean. The jump at the cutoff is the average effect there, and only there. The linear model gives 4.534 for motor-vehicle deaths at 21, or 3.411 residual standard deviations; quadratic with interactions gives 4.663; local linear with bandwidth one gives 4.76. Internal causes and fake cutoffs show nothing on the full sample, but one narrow window of the placebo outcome does, so report the whole bandwidth curve.

Recap of part 2. Each design replaces the unavailable list of confounders with an assumption about variation. Instruments assume the instrument moves the treatment and nothing else: Card, 0.1315 against OLS 0.0747. Difference in differences assumes parallel trends: Card and Krueger, plus 2.75 FTE.
