Support part one. The pitfalls.

This part covers the pitfalls note, part one: the confusions this material produces. Every number here is asserted in the Python checks.

Pitfall one. Unbiased against low mean squared error.
The error: treating "unbiased" as "best".
The counterexample: the uniform on zero to theta, with theta equal to 3 and n equal to 10. The biased maximum likelihood estimator, the sample maximum, has mean squared error 0.136. The unbiased twice the sample mean has 0.300. And within the family of multiples c times the maximum, the unbiased choice, c equal to 11 over 10, gives theta squared over 120, while the biased optimum, c equal to 12 over 11, gives theta squared over 121.
The correction: mean squared error is variance plus squared bias. Unbiasedness removes one term. It does not minimise the sum.

Pitfall two. Same likelihood against same sampling distribution.
The error: thinking that if two experiments give proportional likelihoods, every inference must agree.
The counterexample: one success in ten trials. Both designs give theta hat equal to 0.1, the same observed information, the same Wald interval and the same beta posterior. But the expected value of theta hat at theta equal to 0.1 is 0.100 under the binomial and 0.256 under the negative binomial; and the p-value against theta equal to one half is 0.0107 under the binomial and 0.0020 under the negative binomial.
The correction: what depends only on the observed likelihood agrees: the estimate, the observed information, the Wald interval and the posterior. What averages over unobserved outcomes disagrees: bias, exact variance, coverage and p-values.

Pitfall three. Observed against expected information.
The error: treating "the information" as a property of the data alone.
The counterexample: the observed information, minus the second derivative of the log-likelihood, is computed from the realised likelihood and is the same function under every sampling rule. The expected information averages over the design. At theta equal to one half, a binomial with n equal to 10 has expected information 10 over 0.25, which is 40, so the asymptotic standard deviation is 0.158. A negative binomial with one success has expected information one over 0.25 times 0.5, which is 8, so the standard deviation is 0.354. The plug-ins coincide at theta hat; the functions of theta do not.
The correction: the observed information is random and design-free; the expected information is fixed and design-dependent. Using the observed information at the estimate for the standard error is what makes the Wald interval blind to the stopping rule.

Pitfall four. The p-value against the probability of the null given the data.
The error: reading "p equal to 0.036" as "a 3.6 per cent chance that the null is true".
The counterexample: X normal with mean theta and variance one, null theta equal to zero, alternative theta equal to 4, and x equal to 1.8. The p-value is 0.036, yet with equal priors the posterior probability of the null is one minus 0.310, which is 0.690. The data favour the null: the Bayes factor for the null is e to the 0.8, which is 2.23.
The correction: the p-value is a tail area under the null. It never looks at the alternative, so it cannot be a posterior probability. Every x between 1.645 and 2 is significant and also favours the null.

Pitfall five. Profiling against integrating.
The error: treating the profile weight w of tau as the posterior of the change point.
The counterexample: a toy series, 6 followed by seven 1s, with a uniform prior on the cut and exponential priors with rate one half on both rates for the integrated version. The weight on "the break is after year one" is 0.783 profiled and 0.653 integrated. The one-year segment fits its own count exactly, with rate estimate 6. Profiling keeps that perfect fit and ignores how uncertain a rate estimated from one observation is. Integrating charges for that uncertainty. On the coal data both routes give 0.251 at 1891, because both segments are long.
The correction: profiling plugs in the peak height; integrating keeps the area. They agree only when every segment is long and the prior is weak.

Pitfall six. Overdispersion against a change point.
The error: saying "the variance to mean ratio is 1.59 with p below 0.001, so the rate changed."
The counterexample: the ratio does not depend on the order of the years. Shuffle the 112 coal counts and the dispersion index is still exactly 1.592, with p below 0.001. But the best-cut likelihood-ratio statistic of the shuffled series has a 95 per cent quantile of about 14 across permutations, against 70.0 in calendar order. Overdispersion also arises from year-to-year rate heterogeneity with no break at all.
The correction: overdispersion says "not one Poisson rate". Only the time structure, through the profile likelihood and the segment fits, says "a change around 1890".

Pitfall seven. A searched likelihood-ratio statistic referred to a chi-squared with one degree of freedom.
The error: saying "LR equals 70.0, so the p-value is the probability that a chi-squared with one degree of freedom exceeds 70."
The counterexample: simulate iid Poisson series with rate 1.705 and length 112, so there is no change, and maximise the statistic over all 111 cuts. The 95 per cent quantile is 9.9, not the chi-squared's 3.84. A nominal 5 per cent test with the chi-squared cut-off would reject far too often. Here 70.0 lies above every one of 4,000 null draws and 4,000 permutation draws, so the conclusion survives. The chi-squared p-value does not.
The correction: Wilks needs a regular problem. A cut that is searched, discrete, and unidentified under the null is not one.

Pitfall eight. The R squared against out-of-sample fit.
The error: believing a higher R squared means a better model.
The counterexample: Boston, interaction order 5, with 2380 coefficients and only 253 training observations. The in-sample R squared is 1.0000, the out-of-sample R squared is 0.052, and the test root mean squared error is 47.13 against 4.48 for the plain model.
The correction: the R squared cannot fall when a column is added, so it cannot choose between nested models. The training error is optimistic by two p sigma squared over n.

Pitfall nine. Significant against important.
The error: believing a joint F of 13.22, with p below 0.0001, means the variables matter a lot.
The counterexample: abstention, model 4 against model 2. The size dummies are jointly significant, yet the adjusted R squared moves from 0.754 to 0.756. With 5,565 observations, a tiny share of variance is still precisely estimated.
The correction: significance and explanatory share answer different questions.

Pitfall ten. Stationary against unit root, and persistent against nonstationary.
The error: treating a very persistent first-order autoregression as nonstationary, or treating a vector autoregression with a coefficient above one as explosive.
The counterexamples: phi equal to 0.9 is stationary, with equilibrium variance 5.26 sigma squared and half-life 6.6. Only phi equal to one has variance t sigma squared and no equilibrium. Tsay's Example 8.3 has a diagonal coefficient of 1.1, yet its eigenvalues are 0.8 and 0.5, so it is stationary.
The correction: stationarity is decided by the absolute value of phi being below one, or by the eigenvalues of Phi. It is not decided by how slowly the autocorrelation decays, or by any single coefficient.

Pitfall eleven. Consistency against unbiasedness.
The error: treating the two as the same property, or assuming one implies the other.
The counterexamples, all for the uniform on zero to theta. The sample maximum is not unbiased, its bias is minus theta over n plus one, but it is consistent, because its mean squared error, two theta squared over n plus one times n plus two, goes to zero. Twice the first draw is unbiased but not consistent: its mean squared error is theta squared over three for every n. The sample mean is neither unbiased for theta nor consistent: it converges to theta over two, and its mean squared error tends to theta squared over four. Also, s over N in inverse sampling is biased, 0.256 at theta equal to 0.1 with one success, but consistent as s grows.
The correction: unbiasedness is about the centre at a fixed n; consistency is about the limit. A mean squared error that goes to zero gives consistency, with or without bias.

Pitfall twelve. The nonparametric bootstrap for the maximum.
The error: believing the bootstrap always works if n is large.
The counterexample: the resampled maximum equals the observed one with probability one minus the quantity one minus one over n to the n: 0.651 at n equal to 10, 0.634 at n equal to 100, and tending to 0.632. The true law of the maximum is continuous. In the deck the bootstrap standard deviation of the maximum is too small at n equal to 50, 0.041 against 0.058, and too large at n equal to 100, 0.036 against 0.029. The shape is wrong at every n.
The correction: the empirical distribution has no mass beyond its own maximum. A statistic that lives on the edge of the support cannot be bootstrapped from it. Resample from the fitted uniform on zero to theta hat instead.

Pitfall thirteen. Uncorrelated against independent.
The error: concluding independence from zero autocorrelation.
The counterexample: IBM monthly returns. Ljung-Box on returns gives Q of 30 equal to 38.2, with p equal to 0.144: no autocorrelation. On squared returns it gives Q of 30 equal to 182.1, with p about 10 to the minus 23.
The correction: the returns are uncorrelated but not independent; their size is predictable. That is volatility clustering, Class 8.

Pitfall fourteen. Asymptotics where they do not apply.
The error: applying the regular large-sample recipe everywhere.
The counterexamples. With one success in ten trials, the Wald interval runs from minus 0.086 to 0.286, which leaves the interval from zero to one. The uniform maximum likelihood estimator is not a root of the score: the derivative of the log-likelihood, minus n over theta, never vanishes, and the maximum is at the boundary, the sample maximum. For iid Poisson data with a zero total, the maximum likelihood estimator does not exist on positive lambda.
The correction: check the regularity before using the recipe.

Pitfall fifteen. A confident posterior against a right model.
The error: reading a tight posterior as evidence that the model is right.
The counterexample: on all 112 coal years the posterior mean is 1.707, with a tight posterior around it. It is a precise estimate of a single rate that section 3 showed does not exist.
The correction: the prior regularises the estimate. It does not test the likelihood.

Pitfall sixteen. 0.81 as the false-positive rate of a specification search.
The error: saying "32 tries means an 81 per cent false-positive rate."
The counterexample: one minus 0.95 to the 32nd, 0.806, assumes independent tests. With 32 one-sided z tests sharing a common component, a pairwise correlation of 0.5, like re-analyses of the same 30 students, the family-wise rate is 0.405. That is still eight times the nominal 0.05, but it is not 0.81. And the real choice count of the deck's setup is 162, not 32.
The correction: the independence formula is an upper bound for positively correlated tests; the true rate lies between 0.05 and that bound.

The ten distinctions that cost most points, each as a pair.
Likelihood against sampling distribution: the observed curve, against the average over outcomes that did not happen.
Observed information at the estimate against expected information: realised curvature, design-free, against expected curvature, design-dependent.
Unbiased against minimum mean squared error: the centre alone, against centre and spread together.
Unbiased against consistent: a fixed-n statement, against a limit.
Profile against integrated likelihood: the peak height, against the area under the curve.
Overdispersion against change point: "not one rate", against "two rates, in this order".
p-value against Bayes factor against posterior: a tail area under the null, against a ratio of heights, against a probability of the hypothesis.
R squared against test error: can only rise with p, against rises once noise is fitted.
Persistent against unit root: the absolute value of phi below one but near one, against phi equal to one.
Nonparametric against parametric bootstrap: resample the data, against resample the fitted model.

Support part five. Self-test: thirty true or false items.

This part covers the original self-test of the pitfalls note. Scoring follows the course rule: the true or false score is the maximum of zero and the number correct minus half the number wrong, times the item value; blank items do not count as wrong. Difficulty is marked easy, medium or hard. The numbers are illustrative unless a note is cited. After each statement, pause the player, decide true or false and say why, then resume for the answer.

Block A, likelihood and sampling rules. The setting for items 1 to 5: two Anvisa inspectors estimate the defect rate theta of a vaccine line. Inspector A audits a fixed batch of 20 vials and finds 2 defective. Inspector B audits vials until the second defective appears, which happens at the 20th vial.

Item 1, easy. Both inspectors' maximum likelihood estimates equal 0.10. Pause.
Answer: true. Both likelihoods are proportional to theta squared times one minus theta to the 18th. The score vanishes at 2 over 20, which is 0.10, whichever binomial constant multiplies the kernel. The estimate uses only the realised likelihood, so the sampling rule cannot change it.

Item 2, medium. Because the two estimates coincide, B's estimator 2 over N is unbiased for theta, just as A's S over 20 is. Pause.
Answer: false. The estimate is the same function of the data; its sampling distribution is not. Under inverse sampling N is random and one over x is convex, so Jensen gives an expected value of 2 over N above theta. At theta equal to 0.1 the exact mean is 0.165. Haldane's s minus one over N minus one, here one over N minus one, is the unbiased one. A's S over 20 is unbiased. The converse trap is "same likelihood implies same bias".

Item 3, medium. With a uniform beta prior with parameters 1 and 1, A and B obtain the same posterior for theta. Pause.
Answer: true. The posterior is beta with parameters 1 plus 2 and 1 plus 18, beta of 3 and 19, with mean 0.136, under either rule. The binomial coefficients cancel in the normalisation. This is the likelihood principle at work: the stopping rule does not enter Bayesian inference.

Item 4, hard. Testing the null theta equal to 0.25 against theta below 0.25 at the 5 per cent level, A and B necessarily reach the same decision, since their likelihoods are proportional. Pause.
Answer: false. Extreme is defined by each sample space. For A, the p-value is the probability that S is at most 2 with n equal to 20 and theta equal to 0.25, which is 0.091: do not reject. For B, it is the probability that N is at least 20 with s equal to 2, which equals the probability that a binomial with 19 trials and probability 0.25 is at most 1, which is 0.031: reject. Identical vials, opposite verdicts.

Item 5, medium. The Wald 95 per cent intervals of A and B, built from the observed information at the estimate, are identical. Pause.
Answer: true. The observed information is n over theta hat times one minus theta hat, 20 over 0.1 times 0.9, which is 222.2, in both cases, and the plug-in variances coincide. The interval is also poor in both cases: 0.10 plus or minus 1.96 times 0.067, from minus 0.031 to 0.231, which leaves the interval from zero to one. Being the same is not being right.

Block B, the Poisson estimator. The setting for items 6 to 9: an analyst models the yearly number of offshore oil-spill incidents reported to the ANP as iid Poisson with rate lambda, an illustrative example.

Item 6, easy. If the model is right, no unbiased estimator of lambda has a smaller variance than the sample mean. Pause.
Answer: true. The variance of the sample mean, lambda over n, equals the Cramér-Rao bound, so the sample mean is efficient. Under the bound's regularity conditions no unbiased competitor can do better. It is also the minimum variance unbiased estimator, a graduate-layer fact.

Item 7, medium. The sample variance is also unbiased for lambda. Both it and the sample mean are consistent, so for large samples they are equally precise. Pause.
Answer: false. Both are unbiased and consistent, but the variance of s squared is lambda over n plus two lambda squared over n minus one. Its efficiency tends to one over one plus two lambda, 0.2 at lambda equal to 2, so s squared needs about five times the data. Consistency is a limit statement; it says nothing about precision at any n.

Item 8, medium. For any data set, the Poisson likelihood has a maximiser on the positive half-line. Pause.
Answer: false. If every year has zero incidents, the log-likelihood is log c minus n lambda, which increases as lambda falls to zero, and there is no maximum on the open space. This happens with probability e to the minus n lambda, not negligible for small n lambda.

Item 9, hard. At lambda equal to 2 and n equal to 50, Chebyshev's lower bound on the probability that the sample mean is within 0.25 of lambda is a close approximation to the exact probability. Pause.
Answer: false. Chebyshev gives one minus 2 over 50 times 0.25 squared, which is 0.36. The exact probability is 0.789, from the total being Poisson with rate 100. The bound proves consistency but is far too crude to quantify it.

Block C, the change point and the first prior. The setting for items 10 to 14: yearly counts of track incidents on a metro network, illustrative, and the coal-mining series.

Item 10, medium. A dispersion index of 1.6 with a dispersion test p-value below 0.001 shows that the incident rate changed at some date. Pause.
Answer: false. The index ignores the order of the years. Shuffling the coal series leaves it at exactly 1.592, yet the shuffled best-cut likelihood ratio has a 95 per cent quantile near 14, against 70 in calendar order. Overdispersion rules out one Poisson rate. A change point is one explanation; year-to-year heterogeneity is another. Locating a break needs the time structure.

Item 11, hard. In the coal data, the likelihood ratio is 70.0 at the profile-maximising cut, so the correct p-value is the probability that a chi-squared with one degree of freedom exceeds 70. Pause.
Answer: false. Tau was chosen to maximise the statistic over 111 candidates; it is not identified when the two rates are equal; and it is discrete, so Wilks does not apply. Under an iid Poisson null with no break, the maximised statistic has a 95 per cent quantile of 9.9, not 3.84. The single-rate model is still rejected, because 70 exceeds every simulated null draw.

Item 12, easy. The single-rate model can never attain a higher maximised likelihood than the best two-regime model. Pause.
Answer: true. One rate is the special case of equal rates in the two-regime model. Maximising over a larger set cannot give a lower maximum, so the single-rate line in the profile figure lies below every cut.

Item 13, easy. With an exponential prior with rate a and n iid Poisson counts summing to s sub n, the posterior of lambda is gamma with shape s sub n plus one and rate a plus n. Pause.
Answer: true. The prior kernel e to the minus a lambda times the likelihood kernel lambda to the s sub n e to the minus n lambda gives lambda to the s sub n e to the minus a plus n lambda, the gamma kernel with shape s sub n plus one and rate a plus n. The exponential is a gamma with shape one, so the update adds the count to the shape and the exposure to the rate.

Item 14, medium. With the exponential prior with rate one half, the posterior mean, s sub n plus one over n plus one half, always exceeds the estimate s sub n over n, because the prior adds one pseudo-event. Pause.
Answer: false. The prior adds one event and half a year. The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean, one over a, which is 2, and the sample mean, so it exceeds the estimate only when the sample mean is below 2. On the 1851 to 1891 data, the sample mean is 3.098, above 2, and the posterior mean is 3.084, below the estimate. The pull is toward the prior centre, not always upward.

Block D, mean squared error and the bootstrap. The setting for items 15 to 19: a procurement auditor at the TCU models winning discounts in a sealed-bid auction as iid uniform on zero to theta and wants the upper bound theta, an illustrative example.

Item 15, easy. An unbiased estimator of theta always has a smaller mean squared error than a biased one. Pause.
Answer: false. At theta equal to 3 and n equal to 10, the biased maximum has mean squared error 0.136 and the unbiased twice the sample mean has 0.300. Mean squared error is variance plus squared bias: a small bias can buy a large cut in variance.

Item 16, medium. The maximum likelihood estimator of theta is found by setting the score to zero. Pause.
Answer: false. The likelihood is theta to the minus n times the indicator that theta is at least the maximum, so the score, minus n over theta, never vanishes. The likelihood is decreasing wherever it is positive, so the maximum sits on the boundary, theta equal to the sample maximum. Bolfarine Example 3.1.3.

Item 17, medium. Among all estimators of the form c times the maximum, the unbiased one, c equal to n plus one over n, has the smallest mean squared error. Pause.
Answer: false. The mean squared error is minimised at c star equal to n plus two over n plus one, below n plus one over n. The optimum shrinks slightly and accepts a bias of minus theta over n plus one squared. At n equal to 10: theta squared over 121, below theta squared over 120.

Item 18, medium. With 2 bids, the maximum has strictly smaller mean squared error than twice the sample mean. Pause.
Answer: false. At n equal to 2 both equal theta squared over 6, because n minus one times n minus two is zero. The strict ranking needs n of at least 3. That is why the handwritten "n at least 2" is wrong.

Item 19, hard. As n grows, the nonparametric bootstrap distribution of the resampled maximum approaches the true sampling distribution of the maximum. Pause.
Answer: false. The resampled maximum equals the observed maximum with probability one minus the quantity one minus one over n to the n, which tends to 0.632. That point mass never disappears, while the true law of the maximum is continuous. The bootstrap is inconsistent here. The parametric bootstrap from the uniform on zero to theta hat is not.

Block E, Gaussian linear regression. The setting for items 20 to 24: an IPEA analyst regresses municipal outcomes on several regressors by least squares with an intercept, as in the abstention study.

Item 20, easy. Adding a regressor can never lower the R squared. Pause.
Answer: true. The smaller model is the larger one with the extra coefficient set to zero, so the minimised residual sum of squares cannot rise. That is exactly why the R squared cannot select a model.

Item 21, medium. Adding a regressor raises the adjusted R squared only if its t statistic is significant at 5 per cent. Pause.
Answer: false. The exact condition is an absolute t above one, Wooldridge page 181, far below the 5 per cent critical value. The adjusted R squared is a lenient criterion: in the seeded best-subset simulation it keeps 5 predictors where BIC picks the true 3.

Item 22, medium. Under Gaussian errors, the maximum likelihood estimator RSS over n is unbiased for sigma squared. Pause.
Answer: false. The expected RSS is sigma squared times n minus q, so the expected value of RSS over n is sigma squared times n minus q over n, below sigma squared. The unbiased estimator divides by n minus q. The maximum likelihood and unbiased variance estimators differ here, while the beta hats are the same.

Item 23, hard. For a fixed design and a correct linear model with p columns, the expected training mean squared error understates the expected test mean squared error at the same design by two p sigma squared over n. Pause.
Answer: true. The expected training error is sigma squared times one minus p over n, and the expected test error is sigma squared times one plus p over n. The gap is two p sigma squared over n: the training error is optimistic in proportion to the number of parameters, and C p adds exactly that back.

Item 24, medium. Since the four size dummies are jointly significant, with F equal to 13.22 and p below 0.0001, adding them must raise the adjusted R squared substantially. Pause.
Answer: false. The adjusted R squared moves from 0.754 to 0.756. With 5,565 observations, a very small share of variance is estimated precisely. Significance and explanatory share answer different questions. The group's F above one guarantees only that the adjusted R squared rises, not by how much.

Block F, autoregressions. The setting for items 25 to 28: a Banco Central analyst models a monthly inflation-gap series.

Item 25, easy. A first-order autoregression with phi equal to 0.95 is nonstationary because shocks to it are very persistent. Pause.
Answer: false. Stationarity needs only an absolute phi below one. At phi equal to 0.95 the equilibrium variance is sigma squared over one minus 0.9025, which is 10.26 sigma squared, finite, and the half-life is about 13.5 periods. Persistent is not the same as a unit root. Only at phi equal to one does the variance grow like t sigma squared.

Item 26, medium. For a Gaussian first-order autoregression with absolute phi below one, the distribution of y at t given any starting value y zero converges to a normal with mean mu and variance sigma squared over one minus phi squared. Pause.
Answer: true. The conditional mean, mu plus phi to the t times y zero minus mu, tends to mu, and the conditional variance, sigma squared times one minus phi to the 2 t over one minus phi squared, tends to sigma squared over one minus phi squared. The start is forgotten geometrically.

Item 27, hard. A first-order vector autoregression whose coefficient matrix has an entry greater than one is necessarily explosive. Pause.
Answer: false. Stationarity depends on the eigenvalues of Phi, not on its entries. Tsay's Example 8.3 has a diagonal entry of 1.1 and eigenvalues 0.8 and 0.5, so it is stationary, with mean 1 and 2.

Item 28, medium. Least squares of y at t on one and y at t minus one gives the exact Gaussian maximum likelihood estimates of a first-order autoregression. Pause.
Answer: false. Least squares maximises the conditional likelihood given the first observation. The exact likelihood also contains the density of the first observation, normal with mean mu and variance sigma squared over one minus phi squared, which depends on phi and imposes an absolute phi below one. The two estimates differ slightly and coincide asymptotically.

Block G, p-values and evidence.

Item 29, easy. Under the null, the p-value of a test with a continuous statistic is uniformly distributed on zero to one. Pause.
Answer: true. The p-value is one minus F zero of T, and F zero of T is uniform under the null by the probability integral transform, so the null probability that p is at most alpha is alpha. This is what makes the minimum of many p-values misleading: 32 independent uniforms fall below 0.05 at least once with probability 0.806.

Item 30, hard. With X normal with mean theta and variance one, the null theta equal to zero, the alternative theta equal to 4, and equal prior probabilities, an observation whose one-sided p-value is 0.03 is evidence in favour of the alternative. Pause.
Answer: false. A p-value of 0.03 means x equal to 1.881. By the log Bayes factor 4 x minus 8, B F one zero is e to the 4 times 1.881 minus 8, which is 0.621, below one, so the data favour the null, and the posterior probability of the alternative is 0.383. Any x between 1.645 and 2 is significant at 5 per cent yet closer to zero than to 4. The p-value never looks at where the alternative is.


Support part six. Glossary.

Likelihood: the joint probability or density of the observed sample, read as a function of the parameter.
Log-likelihood: the natural log of the likelihood; constants that do not involve the parameter drop out on differentiation.
Score: the first derivative of the log-likelihood; setting it to zero gives the likelihood equation.
Maximum likelihood estimator: the parameter value that maximises the likelihood, sometimes at a root of the score, sometimes on the boundary.
Observed information: minus the second derivative of the log-likelihood, a random, design-free curvature.
Expected, or Fisher, information: the expected value of the observed information, fixed and dependent on the design.
Likelihood principle: proportional likelihoods should give the same inference; likelihood-based and Bayesian methods obey it, frequentist sampling properties do not.
Sampling rule: the design that decides which quantities are random, such as fixed trials or fixed successes.
Negative binomial, or inverse, sampling: sampling until the s-th success, so the number of trials is random.
Sufficient statistic: a statistic such that the conditional law of the sample given it does not depend on the parameter.
Factorisation criterion: a statistic is sufficient when the likelihood factors into a function of the statistic and the parameter times a function of the data alone.
Unbiased: an estimator whose expected value equals the parameter at every sample size.
Consistent: an estimator that converges in probability to the parameter as the sample grows.
Cramér-Rao lower bound: one over the Fisher information, the smallest variance an unbiased estimator can attain under regularity.
Efficiency: the ratio of the Cramér-Rao bound to an estimator's variance; one means efficient.
Equidispersion: the Poisson property that the mean equals the variance.
Overdispersion: a variance larger than the mean in count data, a symptom of mixed or changing rates.
Dispersion test: the statistic n minus one times s squared over the sample mean, referred to a chi-squared with n minus one degrees of freedom.
Profile likelihood: the likelihood of one parameter with the others replaced by their maximising values.
Integrated likelihood: the likelihood of one parameter with the others integrated against a prior.
Generalized likelihood-ratio test: compares the maximised likelihood under the null with the unrestricted maximum; Wilks gives its chi-squared limit under regularity.
Change point: the time at which a model's parameter, here a Poisson rate, switches value.
Prior: a density for the parameter that encodes knowledge before the data.
Posterior: the density of the parameter given the data, proportional to likelihood times prior.
Conjugate prior: a prior whose posterior stays in the same family, such as gamma for a Poisson rate or beta for a Bernoulli probability.
Shrinkage: the pull of a Bayesian estimate toward the prior centre, with weight that vanishes as data accumulate.
Mean squared error: the expected squared distance between an estimator and the parameter, equal to variance plus squared bias.
Order statistics: the sorted sample; the largest is the sample maximum.
Monte Carlo: approximating a sampling distribution by simulating many samples from a known model.
Nonparametric bootstrap: approximating a sampling distribution by resampling the observed data with replacement.
Parametric bootstrap: approximating a sampling distribution by simulating from the fitted model.
Normal equations: X transposed X times beta hat equals X transposed y, the first-order conditions of least squares.
Hat matrix: the projection matrix that maps y to the fitted values.
R squared: one minus the residual sum of squares over the total sum of squares, the share of variance explained.
Adjusted R squared: the R squared with both sums of squares replaced by unbiased variance estimates.
Mallows' C p, AIC and BIC: training error plus a price per parameter, with BIC charging log n instead of two.
Best subset selection: an exhaustive search for the best model of each size.
Overfitting: fitting noise, so that training error falls while test error rises.
Optimism: the expected gap between test and training error, two p sigma squared over n for a correct linear model.
HC1 standard errors: White's heteroskedasticity-robust variance times n over n minus q.
Omitted-variable bias: the bias from leaving out a regressor, equal to its effect times its slope on the included regressor.
Weak stationarity: a constant mean and autocovariances that depend only on the lag.
Autocorrelation function: the correlation between a series and its own lags, as a function of the lag.
Ljung-Box test: a portmanteau test of zero autocorrelations up to lag m.
White noise: iid shocks with finite mean and variance.
First-order autoregression: a regression of the series on its own first lag.
Equilibrium, or stationary, distribution: the limiting law a stationary autoregression settles into, whatever its start.
Half-life: the horizon at which the expected deviation from the mean has halved.
Unit root: phi equal to one, a random walk, with variance growing like t and no equilibrium.
Vector autoregression: a system in which each series is regressed on the lags of all series.
Kronecker product and vec: the matrix tools that turn the vector autoregression's covariance equation into a linear system.
p-value: the null probability of a result at least as extreme as the one observed, where extreme is defined by the sample space.
p-hacking: searching over analyses until one gives p below 0.05.
Family-wise error rate: the probability of at least one false rejection across a family of tests.
Bonferroni correction: testing each of m hypotheses at alpha over m to keep the family-wise rate at most alpha.
Bayes factor: the ratio of the data's probability under two hypotheses; for point hypotheses, the likelihood ratio.
Jeffreys-Lindley paradox: a significant p-value alongside a Bayes factor that favours the null.


Support part seven. The whole class in five lines.

One. A likelihood picks the estimator, but the sampling rule sets its bias and its p-values: one success in ten trials gives theta hat equal to 0.1 either way, yet a mean of 0.256 and a p-value of 0.0020 under inverse sampling.

Two. Judge estimators by mean squared error, variance plus squared bias, worked out exactly, by Monte Carlo, or by bootstrap; the biased maximum beats twice the mean, and the nonparametric bootstrap fails on the edge of the support with a 63 per cent point mass.

Three. Judge models by what they predict and assume: the coal data's overdispersion rejects one Poisson rate, two rates fit with a break near 1891 and a likelihood ratio of 70.0 that is not chi-squared, and a confident posterior does not make a model right.

Four. Least squares is a projection and the Gaussian maximum likelihood estimator, the R squared cannot fall and so cannot select, and a first-order autoregression settles into a normal with variance sigma squared over one minus phi squared only when the absolute value of phi is below one.

Five. A p-value is not evidence: 32 tries make significance likely, and a p-value of 0.036 can sit beside a posterior of only 0.31 for the alternative.
