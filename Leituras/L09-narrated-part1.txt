How to listen.

This is a listening version of the finished study notes for Class 9 of the course Econometria Avançada, in the professional master's in economics at Insper, taught by Hedibert Freitas Lopes. It carries the same content as the written notes, re-voiced for the ear. Every formula is spoken in words, the way you would dictate it to someone writing on a board, and then its meaning is said in plain language. Matrices are read entry by entry, row by row.

The narration comes in three files. Part 1, this file, covers the map of the class, then the vector autoregression and what it can say: estimation and forecasting; the moving-average form, impulse responses and the forecast-error variance decomposition; and the structural vector autoregression. Part 3 holds the support material: the pitfalls, the spoken formula sheet, the implementation described as a sequence of steps, the reading route and exercises, a true or false self-test, a glossary, and the whole class in five lines.

Each time a G item comes up, the narration says so out loud.

Part zero. The map of Class 9.

This part covers the map note: the one claim of the class, the reading order, the one result to hold, the numbers to memorize, the scope, and the position in the course.

The one claim. A VAR is a multivariate regression. OLS equation by equation is its MLE, the companion matrix decides stationarity, and impulse responses and forecasts are powers of it.

Its shocks are correlated, so every structural reading needs restrictions, q times q minus one over two of them, and the familiar Cholesky ordering is one such set, not a neutral device.

Its parameters grow like q squared times p, and the two answers of the lecture are shrinkage, the Minnesota prior, whose posterior is ridge regression toward a random walk, and factors, a few common components carrying the cross-section, in a FAVAR or in the covariance.

When coefficients and volatilities move, each is a state and the Kalman filter updates it. When only covariances move, DCC models correlations directly, while factor SV derives them from the factor's volatility.

Part one, section 1. A VAR p is a multivariate regression: OLS is the MLE, the companion matrix decides stationarity, and forecasts are powers of it.

This section covers slides 3 to 9: VAR at a glance, the model set-up, the multivariate regression, the matrix normal, the multivariate normal, the h-step forecast, and stationarity; slide 13, the simulated VAR 2; and slides 27 and 45, the parameter counts. It covers the site's VAR example, sections 2 to 3.1: OLS step by step, the MTS fit, and VARorder. The claim, before the derivations. Stack the lags in x t and a VAR p is y t equals B x t plus u t, a multivariate regression with the same regressors in every equation. That one fact makes equation-by-equation OLS the conditional MLE and the SUR, or GLS, estimator at once, and it gives the estimated covariance of beta hat as Sigma hat Kronecker the inverse of X transposed X. Writing the VAR p as a VAR 1 in q times p dimensions, the companion form, settles both remaining questions: the process is stationary if and only if every eigenvalue of the companion matrix lies inside the unit circle, and the h-step forecast is its h-th power applied to the current state.

The notation. y t is the q by one vector of series at time t, for t from one to n; Tsay calls it r t, with dimension k. B i is the q by q autoregressive matrix at lag i, for i from one to p; Tsay calls it Phi i. B, the matrices B one to B p side by side, is q by q p. x t is the q p by one vector of stacked lags, y t minus one down to y t minus p. Mu, which Tsay calls phi zero, is the intercept; slide 13 calls it mu. u t is the innovation, iid normal with mean zero and covariance Sigma, a q by q positive definite matrix. Y, X and U are the data matrices, n by q, n by q p and n by q, with rows y t transposed, x t transposed and u t transposed. Beta, the vec of B transposed, is q squared p by one: equation one's coefficients, then equation two's, and so on, as on slide 22. A is the q p by q p companion matrix. And Psi i are the moving-average weights, derived in section 2, with Psi zero equal to the identity.

One point one. The intuition: why a VAR.

Slide 3 quotes Del Negro and Schorfheide, 2011: VARs "appear to be straightforward multivariate generalizations of univariate autoregressive models. They turn out to be one of the key empirical tools in modern macroeconomics." Sims, in 1980, proposed them in place of large structural models whose exclusion restrictions he called "incredible". The VAR makes no exclusion: every variable depends on every lag of every variable. That buys flexibility and costs parameters, the tension the whole of Class 9 is about, in point eight below and then in section 4.

One point two. Course layer: the model. Slide 4, and Tsay equation 8.13, page 403.

Equation 1: y t equals mu plus B one y t minus one plus and so on up to B p y t minus p, plus u t, with u t iid normal with mean zero and covariance Sigma.

The slide writes equation 1 without mu, "the (basic) VAR(p)"; the simulated example on slide 13 and Tsay keep it.

The stationary mean. If y t is weakly stationary with expected value m, take expectations of equation 1, in three steps. First, m equals mu plus B one m plus and so on up to B p m. Second, the identity minus B one minus and so on minus B p, times m, equals mu. Third, equation 2: m equals the inverse of the identity minus B one minus and so on minus B p, times mu. Tsay page 403 writes this as mu equals the inverse of Phi of one times phi zero. It requires the identity minus the sum of the B i to be invertible.

One point three. Course layer: the multivariate regression form. Slides 5 to 7.

Stack the lags. With B equal to B one through B p and x t the stacked lags, equation 1 without mu is equation 3: y t equals B x t plus u t, with u t normal with mean zero and covariance Sigma. A constant is one more column of x t.

Transpose equation 3 and stack the n rows. Equation 4: Y equals X B transposed plus U, with U matrix normal with mean zero, row covariance the n by n identity, and column covariance Sigma. That is, the rows of U are independent across t, each row with covariance Sigma. Slide 6 writes Y equals X B plus U, with B already transposed. Column i of equation 4 is equation i: y superscript i equals X beta i plus u superscript i, where beta i transposed is row i of B and y superscript i holds the n observations of series i.

Stack the columns, using the vec of X B transposed equal to the identity of order q Kronecker X, times the vec of B transposed. Equation 5: y, the vec of Y, equals the identity Kronecker X, times beta, plus u, with u normal with mean zero and covariance Sigma Kronecker the n by n identity. That is slide 7.

Sigma Kronecker the identity says two things: errors of different equations at the same date are correlated, with covariance Sigma i j; errors at different dates are not. That is exactly the seemingly unrelated regression of Greenberg section 9.1, page 134, equations 9.1 and 9.2: "the covariances permit nonzero correlations for disturbances with the same second subscript". Here the same regressor block, x t transposed, appears in every equation.

The block-determinant rule: the determinant of the block matrix with blocks P, Q, R, S equals the determinant of S times the determinant of P minus Q S inverse R, for lambda not zero. Take P equal to lambda I minus B one, Q equal to minus B two, R equal to minus I, and S equal to lambda I. Three steps. Second, that is lambda to the q times the determinant of lambda I minus B one minus lambda inverse B two. Third, equation 15: that is lambda to the 2 q times the determinant of I minus B one lambda inverse minus B two lambda to the minus 2.

With z equal to one over lambda, a nonzero eigenvalue lambda of A is exactly the reciprocal of a root z of the determinant of I minus B one z minus B two z squared equal to zero. Hence the absolute value of lambda is below one if and only if the absolute value of z is above one. The same Schur step, repeated, gives the general p; Tsay page 405 states that the determinant of I minus Phi B equals the determinant of Phi of B.

The claim, before the derivations. A stationary VAR is a moving average of its innovations: y t equals the sum over s of Psi s u t minus s, with Psi s equal to B one Psi s minus one plus and so on up to B p Psi s minus p. The i j entry of Psi s is the response of variable i, s periods later, to a unit innovation in variable j. But innovations are correlated, so "holding the others constant" is not an experiment the data contain. A Cholesky factor P of Sigma makes the shocks orthogonal, Theta s equal to Psi s P, and then the h-step forecast-error variance splits exactly into shares, one per shock. Both objects depend on the ordering of the variables, which is an identifying assumption, not a detail, as section 3 shows.

The notation, as in section 1, plus the following. Psi s is the q by q moving-average weight, the reduced-form impulse response at horizon s, with Psi zero equal to the identity. P is the lower-triangular Cholesky factor, with P P transposed equal to Sigma and a positive diagonal. Tsay writes Sigma as L G L transposed, with L unit lower triangular and G diagonal, so that P equals L times the square root of G. Epsilon t, P inverse u t, are the orthogonalised shocks, standard normal with identity covariance. Theta s, Psi s P, is the orthogonalised impulse response, the response to a one-standard-deviation shock. And omega i j of h is the share of variable i's h-step forecast-error variance due to shock j.

Part one, section 3. Structural VARs: the reduced form cannot tell orthogonal rotations apart, so q times q minus one over two restrictions must come from economics.

This section covers slide 31, the Rubio-Ramírez, Waggoner and Zha 2010 quote; slide 32, the trivariate structural VAR 1 with commodity prices, output and the interest rate; slide 33, the SVAR p set-up; slide 34, exact identification; slides 35 and 36, Illustrations 1 and 2, the zero patterns of A zero; and slide 37, the monetary policy shock, a figure. It also covers Tsay section 8.2.1, pages 399 to 401, equations 8.9 and 8.10 and Example 8.3, and page 413, on the dependence on the ordering.

The claim, before the derivations. A structural VAR, A zero y t equals A one y t minus one plus and so on plus u t, with orthogonal unit-variance shocks, maps to the reduced form by B i equal to A zero inverse A i, and Sigma equal to the inverse of A zero transposed A zero. Any orthogonal matrix Q turns A zero and A into Q A zero and Q A with exactly the same B and Sigma. So the data identify Sigma's q times q plus one over two numbers and nothing more, while A zero has q squared. The gap, q times q minus one over two, must be filled by restrictions. The Cholesky ordering of section 2 is one such set, a recursive SVAR, and Tsay's Example 8.3 shows two orderings giving two different, equally valid "structural" equations from one reduced form.

The notation. A zero is the q by q contemporaneous structural matrix, invertible. A i, and A equal to A one through A p, are the structural lag matrices. u t are the structural shocks, standard normal with identity covariance, slide 33. B i and Sigma are the reduced-form coefficients and innovation covariance. e t, equal to A zero inverse u t, is the reduced-form innovation, called u t in section 1. Q is a q by q orthogonal matrix, with Q transposed Q equal to the identity. And L and G are Tsay's unit-lower-triangular and diagonal matrices, with Sigma equal to L G L transposed.

Three point one. The intuition: the question.

Slide 31 quotes Rubio-Ramírez, Waggoner and Zha, 2010: "Since the seminal work by Sims (1980), identification of structural vector autoregressions (SVARs) has been an unresolved theoretical issue. Filling this theoretical gap is of vital importance because impulse responses based on SVARs have been widely used for policy analysis and to provide stylized facts for dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models." A reduced-form VAR forecasts. A policy question, such as "what does a monetary shock do to output?", needs a shock with an economic name, and naming it is identification.

Three point three. Course layer: why the reduced form cannot identify A zero.

Then, equation 6: A zero tilde inverse A tilde equals Q A zero inverse times Q A, which is A zero inverse Q inverse Q A, which is A zero inverse A, which is B. And, equation 7: the inverse of A zero tilde transposed A zero tilde equals the inverse of A zero transposed Q transposed Q A zero, which is the inverse of A zero transposed A zero, which is Sigma.

So g of Q A zero and Q A equals g of A zero and A: the likelihood is flat along the orbit of Q A zero. Since B is pinned down once A zero is, because A equals A zero B by equation 3, everything reduces to recovering A zero from Sigma.

The order condition, by counting. Sigma is symmetric: q times q plus one over two free numbers. A zero has q squared. The shortfall, equation 8: q squared minus q times q plus one over two equals q times q minus one over two. It is the dimension of the orthogonal group. With q equal to 2, one restriction; q equal to 3, three; q equal to 5, ten; q equal to 6, fifteen. Restrictions are typically zeros in A zero: a variable does not enter an equation contemporaneously.

The definition, slide 34, verbatim: "The SVAR is exactly identified if and only if, for almost any reduced-form parameter point (B, Sigma), there exists a unique structural parameter point (A zero, A) in R such that g of A zero and A equals B and Sigma." Counting is necessary, not sufficient. The zeros must also be placed so that no rotation other than Q equal to the identity, up to sign, preserves them: the rank condition that Rubio-Ramírez, Waggoner and Zha make operational. "Much of the SVAR literature involves exactly identified models", slide 33. Waggoner and Zha, 2003, give an MCMC algorithm for a restricted A zero, slide 34; named only.

Three point four. Course layer: recursive identification is the Cholesky factor.

Restrict A zero to be lower triangular with a positive diagonal: q times q minus one over two zeros above the diagonal, exactly the count of equation 8. Then A zero inverse is lower triangular with a positive diagonal and, by equation 4, A zero inverse times A zero inverse transposed equals Sigma. The Cholesky factor is unique, so, equation 9: A zero inverse equals P, the lower Cholesky factor of Sigma, and Psi s A zero inverse equals Psi s P, which is Theta s.

The orthogonalised impulse response of section 2 is the structural impulse response of a recursive SVAR. The economic content of the ordering: variable one's equation contains no other current variable, so it reacts to nothing within the period; variable q's equation contains them all.

Recap of section 3. Structure maps to the reduced form by B i equal to A zero inverse A i and Sigma equal to the inverse of A zero transposed A zero. Any orthogonal rotation of A zero leaves B and Sigma unchanged, so q times q minus one over two restrictions are needed: 3, 10 and 15 for q equal to 3, 5 and 6. Recap of part 1. A VAR is a multivariate regression, estimated by OLS equation by equation, stationary when its companion eigenvalues lie inside the unit circle, and forecast by powers of the companion matrix. Its moving-average weights give impulse responses, the Cholesky factor orthogonalises them and splits the forecast-error variance into shares, and all of that depends on an ordering that is a structural identifying assumption. Part 2 takes up what happens when the VAR has too many parameters, and when its coefficients and volatilities move over time.
