Support part one. The pitfalls.

This part covers the pitfalls note, part one: the confusions this material produces, each with its minimal counterexample. Every number here is asserted in the Python checks, and equations are cited by each section note's own numbering.

Pitfall one. The probability of the data given the state against the probability of the state given the data.
The error: saying "a test with 95 per cent sensitivity means a positive patient is 95 per cent likely to be ill."
The counterexample: Diego, section 1 equation 8. The probability of a positive test given ill is 0.95, but the probability of ill given a positive test is 0.847 with a 70 per cent prior, and it would be far lower with a rare disease: in Stone's example, smallpox gets 0.011 despite the better likelihood.
The correction: the likelihood is a forward probability; the posterior needs the prior and the predictive. Section 1, subsections 1 and 6.

Pitfall two. Sequential against batch updating.
The error: saying "updating one test at a time double-counts or loses information."
The counterexample: section 1, subsection 4. Sequentially, 0.70 goes to 0.8471 and then to 0.992762. In batch, on X equal to one and Y equal to one together, the answer is 0.992762.
The correction: the two are equal whenever the data are conditionally independent given theta, section 1 equation 9. Section 1, subsection 4, and section 2, subsection 3.

Pitfall three. Posterior mode, mean and maximum likelihood estimate under a uniform prior.
The error: saying "a flat prior makes the Bayes estimate equal the maximum likelihood estimate."
The counterexample: section 4, subsection 7. With a uniform prior, one success in ten trials, the mode is 0.100, which equals the maximum likelihood estimate; but the mean, the quadratic-loss Bayes estimator, is 2 over 12, which is 0.167, and the median is 0.148.
The correction: "flat" fixes the mode, not the estimator. Section 2 equation 7, and section 4.

Pitfall four. Plug-in prediction against the predictive distribution.
The error: saying "the probability of the next success is the maximum likelihood estimate."
The counterexample: section 2 equation 11. After zero successes in n trials, the maximum likelihood estimate predicts zero forever; the predictive under a uniform prior is one over n plus two.
The correction: the predictive averages over theta's uncertainty. Section 2, subsection 4.

Pitfall five. A normal prior's pull against a heavy-tailed prior's pull.
The error: saying "two priors with the same mean and variance give the same posterior."
The counterexample: section 3, subsection 6. The normal with mean 900 and standard deviation 20, and the t with 5 degrees of freedom, location 900 and scale squared 240, which also has variance 400. At x equal to 850: 890.0 against 891.1. At x equal to 1100: 940.0 against 1022.0.
The correction: the normal pull is proportional to the conflict; the t pull is bounded and fades. Section 3, equations 11 and 12.

Pitfall six. "The Bayes estimator is biased, the maximum likelihood estimator is not."
The error: taking the contrast on the deck's slide 13 as a general rule.
The counterexample: section 4 equation 14. For exponential data, the expected value of n over the sum of the x i is n theta over n minus one, so the maximum likelihood estimator is biased too.
The correction: Bayes estimators are optimal for Bayes risk, one seventy-second below one sixtieth in Bolfarine example 4.4.1, not for unbiasedness. Section 4, subsections 8 and 9.

Pitfall seven. Credible against confidence interval.
The error: saying "95 per cent probability that theta is in my confidence interval."
The counterexample: section 4, subsection 10. A credible interval conditions on the data and is a statement about theta; a confidence interval is a statement about the procedure over repeated samples, Greenberg printed page 31. For the skewed Beta of 2 and 10 posterior, the equal-tail and highest-posterior-density intervals differ: 0.023 to 0.413, against 0.006 to 0.368.
The correction: say which interval you report and what it is a probability of. Section 4, subsection 10.

Pitfall eight. Maximised likelihood against marginal likelihood.
The error: saying "the bigger model always wins because it fits at least as well."
The counterexample: section 5, subsection 4. Michaela and Lila both at 0.5 with m equal to 100: the two-probability model's maximised likelihood equals the one-probability model's, yet the log base ten Bayes factor is plus 0.756 for the simpler model.
The correction: the marginal likelihood averages over the prior and charges one half log n per extra parameter. Section 5, subsection 6.

Pitfall nine. Improper priors in Bayes factors.
The error: saying "non-informative priors are safe everywhere."
The counterexample: section 5 equation 9. With c times pi of theta improper, the Bayes factor scales by c one over c two, an arbitrary number.
The correction: an improper prior is fine for one model's posterior and meaningless for comparing models. Section 5, subsection 7.

Pitfall ten. Accept-reject against Metropolis-Hastings.
The error: saying "both reject proposals, so both give independent draws."
The counterexample: section 6 subsection 5, and section 8 subsection 1. Accept-reject retries until acceptance, so the accepted values are independent draws from f. Metropolis-Hastings keeps the current value on rejection, so the chain repeats values and its draws are correlated.
The correction: only accept-reject gives independent draws. Section 6 equation 7, and section 8 subsection 2.

Pitfall eleven. A printed Monte Carlo standard error against a valid one.
The error: saying "any simulation's standard error formula can be trusted."
The counterexample: section 6, subsection 6. Greenberg's importance sampler for the truncated exponential has an infinite integral of g squared f squared over h, because h, the Beta of 2 and 3, vanishes at zero. A seeded run gives 0.800 with a "standard error" of 0.009, against the truth 0.830. And for Markov chain Monte Carlo, the naive standard error ignores autocorrelation, by a factor of the square root of the inefficiency factor.
The correction: check the variance condition, and use batch means for chains. Section 6 equation 11, and section 7 equations 19 and 20.

Pitfall twelve. High acceptance rate against good mixing.
The error: saying "a Metropolis-Hastings sampler accepting 95 per cent of proposals works well."
The counterexample: section 8 equation 9. On a standard normal with s equal to 0.1, acceptance is 0.968, but every step is tiny and the chain crawls; s equal to 2.38 accepts 0.445 and mixes far better.
The correction: tune the scale for mixing, not for acceptance. Section 8, subsection 4.

Pitfall thirteen. Many Gibbs draws against many independent draws.
The error: saying "5000 draws give a Monte Carlo error of sigma over the square root of 5000."
The counterexample: section 7, equations 18 and 19. For the bivariate normal with rho equal to 0.999, the inefficiency factor is 999.5, so 5000 draws carry the information of about 5. Greenberg's means of 1.084 and minus 0.623 from two starts show it.
The correction: divide the number of draws by the inefficiency factor. Section 7, subsection 8.

Pitfall fourteen. Lasso zeros against posterior zeros.
The error: saying "the Bayesian lasso sets coefficients exactly to zero."
The counterexample: section 10, subsection 2. The lasso estimate is the posterior mode under a Laplace prior; the posterior mean is never exactly zero, and at large absolute y it stays one over b below y: 4.59 at y equal to 6, against the horseshoe's 5.66.
The correction: exact zeros belong to the mode, not to the posterior. Section 10, subsections 2 and 5.

Pitfall fifteen. Complete pooling, no pooling, partial pooling.
The error: saying "either all hospitals share one rate or each has its own estimate."
The counterexample: section 9, subsection 4. The hierarchical posterior means run from 0.057 to 0.089, between the separate estimates, 0.029 to 0.130, and the pooled 0.069, with weights eta over eta plus n i estimated from the data.
The correction: partial pooling is the third option, and the data decide how much to pool. Section 9.

Recap of the pitfalls. Do not invert a conditional without the prior. Sequential equals batch. Flat priors fix the mode, not the estimator. Predict with the predictive, not a plug-in. Same variance does not mean same pull. Bias is not the Bayesian criterion; Bayes risk is. Credible is not confidence. Marginal is not maximised likelihood, and improper priors break Bayes factors. Chains are correlated; check standard errors, mixing and the inefficiency factor. Lasso zeros are modes. And pooling has a third, estimated, option.

Support part three. The implementation, described.

This part covers the Python note, which presents the runnable script Notas, L03, L03 python checks, split into its sections. The script uses numpy, scipy and statsmodels, with no R, and no PyMC or ArviZ, which are not installed. It checks every computation with an assertion against the number printed in the deck, the site page, the book or the section note. It runs in about one minute, prints one "ok" line per check, and ends with "ALL L03 CHECKS PASSED", which was the output of the run on October 29th, 2026. The code is not read here; each routine is described by its inputs, its steps, and the trap that breaks it.

What is reused rather than rewritten. The Class 1 companion check script supplies the coal-mining series, loaded from the project's agent notes, and it is imported for the switch point in routine seven. The lecturer's hierarchical Gaussian linear model page holds the 68 by 7 weight-gain and week matrices inside its R code; routine nine parses them at run time and never copies them. The figure script draws the eight figures, each with its own assertions; it is cited, not imported, because importing it would redraw every figure and rerun the samplers. And the companion check script, a separate file, compares the two companions' read-outs with their JavaScript, using the same formulas as routines three and five.

The conventions. A helper called near passes when a computed value matches a printed one to a stated tolerance, by default half a unit in the third decimal. Seeds are fixed. Support part five. Self-test: twenty-five true or false items.

This part covers the original self-test of the pitfalls note; no textbook exercise is reproduced. Scoring follows the course rule: the true or false score is the maximum of zero and the number correct minus half the number wrong, times the item value; blank items do not count as wrong. Difficulty is marked easy, medium or hard. The numbers are illustrative unless a note is cited. After each statement, pause the player, decide true or false and say why, then resume for the answer.

Block A, Bayes' rule. The setting for items 1 to 5: a Brazilian bank's fraud filter flags 98 per cent of fraudulent card transactions and 3 per cent of legitimate ones. One transaction in a hundred is fraudulent.

Item 1, easy. A flagged transaction is more likely fraudulent than not. Pause.
Answer: false. By section 1, equations 3 and 4: 0.98 times 0.01, over 0.98 times 0.01 plus 0.03 times 0.99, is 0.0098 over 0.0395, which is 0.248. The filter's 98 per cent is the probability of a flag given fraud, not of fraud given a flag; pitfall one.

Item 2, medium. The probability that a random transaction is flagged is 0.0395. Pause.
Answer: true. The predictive, section 1 equation 4: 0.0098 plus 0.0297 is 0.0395.

Item 3, medium. If a second, independent filter with the same error rates also flags it, the probability of fraud exceeds 0.9. Pause.
Answer: true. Each flag multiplies the odds by the likelihood ratio 0.98 over 0.03, which is 32.67. The prior odds are one in 99; after two flags the odds are 32.67 squared over 99, which is 10.78, so the probability is 10.78 over 11.78, which is 0.915. Section 1 equation 9: sequential equals batch.

Item 4, hard. If the fraud rate doubled to 2 per cent, the probability of fraud given one flag would double. Pause.
Answer: false. The odds double, not the probability: the odds are 0.02 over 0.98, times 32.67, which is 0.667, so the probability is 0.400, up from 0.248. The posterior ratio is the likelihood ratio times the prior ratio, section 1 subsection 6.

Item 5, easy. With the two filters, the posterior depends on which filter is consulted first. Pause.
Answer: false. With conditionally independent data the joint likelihood is a product, and products commute. Section 1 equation 9; pitfall two.

Block B, conjugate updating and Bayes estimators. The setting for items 6 to 10: a startup models the conversion probability theta of its landing page with a Beta prior with parameters 2 and 8. Of 30 visitors, 6 sign up.

Item 6, easy. The posterior is Beta with parameters 8 and 32, and its mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the maximum likelihood estimate, with weight 0.25 on the prior. Pause.
Answer: true. Section 2 equation 4: 2 plus 6, and 8 plus 24. By equation 6, w is 2 plus 8 over 2 plus 8 plus 30, which is 0.25. Both centres equal 0.2 here, 2 over 10 and 6 over 30, so the mean is 8 over 40, which is 0.20.

Item 7, medium. Under absolute-error loss the Bayes estimate is 0.20. Pause.
Answer: false. Absolute loss gives the median, section 4 equation 8. The Beta of 8 and 32 is right-skewed, so the median, 0.195, is below the mean, 0.200. The mode is 7 over 38, which is 0.184.

Item 8, medium. The probability that the next visitor signs up, given the data, is 0.20. Pause.
Answer: true. The predictive equals the posterior mean, section 2 equation 11: 8 over 40.

Item 9, hard. Had the startup recorded visitors until the sixth sign-up, which came at visitor 30, its posterior would differ. Pause.
Answer: false. Inverse sampling multiplies the likelihood by a different constant only; the kernel, theta to the 6 times one minus theta to the 24, and hence the posterior, are unchanged. That is the likelihood principle, Class 1 section 1, subsection 10. Frequentist bias and p-values would change.

Item 10, medium. As the number of visitors grows with the conversion share fixed, the prior's weight in the posterior mean falls like one over n. Pause.
Answer: true. w is alpha zero plus beta zero over alpha zero plus beta zero plus n, approximately 10 over n. Section 2, equation 6 and subsection 7.

Block C, the normal model and Bayes factors. The setting for items 11 to 15: an analyst's prior for next year's inflation, in per cent, is theta normal with mean 4 and standard deviation 0.5. A survey gives one reading x, normal with mean theta and variance one, and x equals 6.

Item 11, easy. The posterior mean is 4.4 per cent. Pause.
Answer: true. w is one over one plus 0.25, which is 0.8; by section 3 equation 8, 0.8 times 4 plus 0.2 times 6 is 4.4.

Item 12, medium. Because the reading conflicts with the prior, the posterior standard deviation exceeds the prior standard deviation of 0.5. Pause.
Answer: false. In the normal-normal model the posterior variance is w tau zero squared, 0.8 times 0.25, which is 0.2, standard deviation 0.447, whatever x is, section 3 equation 8. Conflict does not widen a conjugate normal posterior; it does widen a t-prior posterior, section 3 subsection 6.

Item 13, hard. A Student t prior with 5 degrees of freedom and the same centre and variance would put the posterior mean closer to 6 than the normal prior does. Pause.
Answer: false. The conflict is 2 over the square root of 1.25, which is 1.79 predictive standard deviations, too mild for the t prior's bounded influence to show. Near its centre a t prior with the same variance is more peaked, so it pulls slightly harder: quadrature gives 4.387 against 4.400. The t posterior overtakes the normal one only for larger conflicts, as the right panel of the four-priors figure shows; pitfall five.

Item 14, hard. Comparing model one, theta equal to 4, with model two, theta normal with mean 4 and standard deviation 0.5, the reading favours the sharp hypothesis, model one. Pause.
Answer: false. m one is the standard normal density at 2, which is 0.0540; m two is the predictive density, the normal with mean 4 and variance 1.25 evaluated at 6, which is 0.0720. The Bayes factor is 0.75: weak evidence for model two. Section 5, equations 3 and 4.

Item 15, medium. Making model two's prior very diffuse, normal with mean 4 and standard deviation 100, moves the Bayes factor toward model one, even though x equal to 6 is unchanged. Pause.
Answer: true. m two becomes the normal density with mean 4 and variance 10,001 at 6, about 0.0040, so the Bayes factor is about 13.5: a diffuse prior spreads model two's predictive mass over values never seen. Section 5 subsection 7, the Jeffreys-Lindley effect.

Block D, Monte Carlo and Markov chain Monte Carlo. The setting for items 16 to 21: a team computes posterior summaries by simulation.

Item 16, easy. To halve the Monte Carlo error of an average of independent draws, they need four times as many draws. Pause.
Answer: true. The numerical standard error is the square root of V over M, section 6 equation 2.

Item 17, medium. An accept-reject sampler with c equal to 2.5 needs on average 2.5 proposals per accepted draw. Pause.
Answer: true. Each proposal is accepted with probability one over c, section 6 equation 7, so the number of proposals is geometric with mean c.

Item 18, medium. A random-walk Metropolis sampler that accepts 90 per cent of its proposals is mixing well. Pause.
Answer: false. High acceptance usually means tiny steps: on a standard normal, s equal to 0.1 accepts 96.8 per cent and crawls. Section 8 equation 9; pitfall twelve.

Item 19, hard. In a two-block Gibbs sampler on a bivariate normal posterior with correlation 0.95, each chain's lag-one autocorrelation is about 0.90, and about 20 draws carry the information of one independent draw. Pause.
Answer: true. The lag-one autocorrelation is rho squared, 0.9025, and the inefficiency factor is one plus 0.9025 over one minus 0.9025, which is 19.5. Section 7, equations 18 and 19.

Item 20, medium. The Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability requires the posterior's normalising constant. Pause.
Answer: false. The posterior enters only through f of y over f of x, where the constant cancels. Section 8 equation 4.

Item 21, easy. Gibbs sampling is Metropolis-Hastings with every proposal accepted. Pause.
Answer: true. With the full conditional as the proposal, alpha equals one. Section 8 equation 10.

Block E, hierarchical and sparsity priors. The setting for items 22 to 25: a regulator compares death rates across hospitals with a Beta-Binomial hierarchy; a data scientist chooses priors for a regression with 200 candidate predictors.

Item 22, medium. In the hierarchical model, a hospital with more cases is pulled more strongly toward the group mean. Pause.
Answer: false. The weight on the group mean is eta over eta plus n i, section 9 equation 5: more cases, less pull. Harlem, with 35 cases, moves from 0.029 to 0.065; NYP Columbia, with 353, stays at 0.071.

Item 23, hard. The population precision eta does not enter the binomial likelihood of the counts given the p i; the data inform it only through how dispersed the p i appear. Pause.
Answer: true. f of y given p and eta equals f of y given p: eta is not identified through the likelihood, Greenberg printed page 53 and section 9 subsection 3, and it is learned from the spread of the p i relative to binomial noise.

Item 24, medium. With a Gaussian prior, the ridge estimate is both the posterior mode and the posterior mean. Pause.
Answer: true. The posterior is normal, so mode equals mean, section 10 equation 2; under a Laplace prior they differ.

Item 25, medium. Under a horseshoe prior, a very large observation is shrunk by roughly a constant amount, as under the lasso. Pause.
Answer: false. The Laplace prior keeps a constant bias, one over b; the horseshoe's posterior mean of kappa tends to zero, so large observations are left almost unshrunk: 5.66 at y equal to 6, against 4.59. Section 10, subsection 5.


Support part six. Glossary.

Prior: the distribution of the unknown before the data, which can be read as data seen earlier.
Likelihood: the probability of the observed data under each value of the unknown, read as a function of the unknown; it does not sum to one over theta.
Posterior: the distribution of the unknown given the data, proportional to likelihood times prior.
Predictive: the probability of the data before they are seen, the likelihood averaged over the prior; it is also the normalising constant of the posterior.
Forward and inverse probability: from the state of nature to the data, and from the data back to the state.
Base rate: the prior frequency of a state, which a slightly better likelihood cannot overcome when it is small.
Sequential updating: using yesterday's posterior as today's prior; with conditionally independent data it equals updating on everything at once.
Sufficient statistic: a summary of the data, such as the number of successes and trials, through which alone the posterior depends.
Conjugate prior: a prior whose posterior stays in the same family, with parameters updated by counts.
Pseudo-data: the reading of a conjugate prior's parameters as observations already seen, such as alpha zero heads and beta zero tails.
Rule of succession: Laplace's s plus one over n plus two, the predictive probability of a success under a uniform prior.
Precision: the inverse of a variance; in the normal model, posterior precision is prior precision plus data precision.
Bounded influence: the property of a heavy-tailed prior whose pull on the posterior vanishes when the data contradict it strongly.
Quadrature: computing a one-dimensional posterior exactly by evaluating its kernel on a fine grid and normalising.
Loss function: the cost of reporting a when the truth is theta.
Bayes estimator: the value that minimises the posterior expected loss: mean, median, mode or quantile, depending on the loss.
Maximum a posteriori estimate: the posterior mode, the Bayes estimator under zero-one loss.
Bayes risk: the frequentist risk averaged over the prior, minimised by the Bayes estimator.
Credible interval: an interval with a stated posterior probability of containing theta, given the observed data.
Highest posterior density interval: the shortest credible interval, whose endpoints have equal posterior density.
Marginal likelihood: the likelihood averaged over a model's prior, the evidence for that model.
Bayes factor: the ratio of two models' marginal likelihoods; posterior odds equal prior odds times the Bayes factor.
Jeffreys' scale: conventional bands for the log base ten Bayes factor, from weak evidence below one half to decisive support above 2.
Occam's razor, built in: the marginal likelihood's penalty, about one half log n per parameter, for freedom the data did not need.
Improper prior: a prior that does not integrate to one; harmless for a posterior, fatal for a Bayes factor.
Jeffreys-Lindley effect: a very diffuse prior on the bigger model makes its marginal likelihood small and favours the smaller model regardless of fit.
Bayesian model averaging: weighting each model's predictive by its posterior probability.
Monte Carlo integration: estimating an expectation by the average of independent draws.
Numerical standard error: the standard error of a Monte Carlo average, the square root of the variance over the number of draws for independent draws.
Inverse distribution function method: drawing from F by applying F inverse to a uniform draw.
Composition: drawing a mixture by first drawing the mixing variable and then the conditional.
Accept-reject: proposing from a dominating density and accepting with probability f over c times g; the acceptance rate is one over c.
Importance sampling: reweighting draws from a proposal density by f over h; it needs proposal tails at least as heavy as the target's.
Sampling importance resampling: resampling proposals with weights proportional to f over g to get approximate draws from f.
Effective sample size: the square of the sum of the weights over the sum of the squared weights, or the number of draws divided by the inefficiency factor for a chain.
Markov chain: a process whose next state depends only on the current one.
Invariant distribution: a distribution that one step of the chain leaves unchanged.
Ergodic average: a chain average, which converges to the expectation under the invariant distribution.
Full conditional: the distribution of one block of parameters given all the others and the data.
Gibbs sampler: drawing each block in turn from its full conditional; its invariant distribution is the joint posterior.
Burn-in: the initial draws discarded while the chain forgets its starting point.
Data augmentation: adding latent variables, such as a switch point or latent volatilities, so that the full conditionals become easy.
Switch point: the last year of the first regime in the coal-mining model, 1891 at the posterior mode.
Inefficiency factor: one plus twice the sum of a chain's autocorrelations; the number of chain draws worth one independent draw.
Batch means: a numerical standard error for a chain computed from the variance of means of consecutive batches.
Reversibility: the balance condition f of x times p of x and y equals f of y times p of y and x, which implies invariance.
Metropolis-Hastings: a sampler that proposes a move and accepts it with probability the minimum of one and the ratio of f times q in the two directions; otherwise it stays.
Random-walk kernel: a symmetric proposal centred at the current value, accepted with probability the minimum of one and the density ratio.
Independence kernel: a proposal that does not depend on the current value, accepted on the ratio of importance weights.
Tailored kernel: a fat-tailed proposal centred at the posterior mode, scaled by the negative inverse Hessian.
Hamiltonian Monte Carlo: a G-layer sampler, used by Stan, that follows gradient-driven trajectories before a Metropolis accept or reject.
Exchangeability: invariance of a joint distribution to permuting the indices; the justification for a common prior across units.
Hierarchical model: a prior whose parameters have their own prior, so units share information.
Partial pooling: estimating each unit by a weighted average of its own estimate and the group mean, with weight eta over eta plus n i on the group.
Shrinkage: pulling an estimate toward a prior or group centre, with weight equal to the prior precision over the total precision.
Ridge: the posterior mode, and mean, under a Gaussian prior, with penalty sigma squared over tau squared.
Lasso: the posterior mode under a Laplace prior, with penalty two sigma squared over b; its exact zeros belong to the mode only.
Scale mixture of normals: a prior in which each coefficient is normal given its own variance, which is itself random.
Normal-gamma prior: a scale mixture with gamma-distributed variances and excess kurtosis three over lambda.
Horseshoe: a scale mixture with half-Cauchy local scales, whose shrinkage factor kappa has the U-shaped Beta of one half and one half distribution.
Shrinkage factor: kappa, equal to one over one plus lambda squared; near one it kills noise, near zero it keeps signals.
Global-local prior: a prior with one global scale tau for overall sparsity and local scales that let individual coefficients escape.
Spike-and-slab: a G-layer prior with positive probability on exact zeros, turning variable selection into model comparison.


Support part seven. The whole unit in five lines.

One. Bayesian learning is one rule, posterior proportional to likelihood times prior, normalised by the predictive: Diego goes from 0.70 to 0.847 to 0.993, three heads leave the fair coin with 0.0674, and sequential updating equals batch updating.

Two. Conjugate priors add data to pseudo-data, so the posterior mean is a weighted average whose prior weight falls like one over n, 0.357 then 0.315 in Greenberg's Figure 2.2; in the normal model precisions add, physicist A ends at 890 and 17.9, and a heavy-tailed prior gives way under conflict, 1022 against 940.

Three. A point estimate is a decision: quadratic, absolute and zero-one losses give the mean, median and mode, 0.167, 0.148 and 0.100 for the Beta of 2 and 10; and a model is judged by its marginal likelihood, which builds in a penalty of one half log n per parameter, minus 1.200 and plus 0.756 in Table 3.2, but only under proper priors.

Four. When integrals have no closed form, simulate: Monte Carlo errors fall like one over the square root of M; accept-reject accepts at rate one over c; Gibbs and Metropolis-Hastings chains have the posterior as invariant distribution, putting the coal switch at 1891 with probability 0.240, at the price of dependence, an inefficiency factor of 999.5 at rho equal to 0.999, and a random-walk acceptance of 0.445 at s equal to 2.38.

Five. Priors are models: a hierarchy learns how much to pool, taking 13 hospital rates from 2.9 to 13.0 per cent into 5.7 to 8.9 per cent, and sparsity priors give each coefficient its own variance, so the horseshoe estimates an observation of 6 at 5.66 against 4.59 for the Laplace and 3.00 for the normal.
