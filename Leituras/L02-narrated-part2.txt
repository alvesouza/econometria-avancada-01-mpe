Part three, section 4. The logit model, its maximum likelihood estimator by iteratively reweighted least squares, and what the default and spam outputs actually say.

This section covers the logistic part of the logistic regression and discriminant analysis deck, by Lopes and Marques, slides 4 to 38; the graduate school admission script and its seven pages of R output; ISLP section 4.3, printed pages 139 to 144, with equations 4.1 to 4.9, Tables 4.1 to 4.3 and Figure 4.3, and the confusion matrix vocabulary of section 4.4.2, printed pages 152 to 156, Tables 4.4 to 4.7; Wooldridge section 17.1, printed pages 525 to 531, with equations 17.2, 17.3, 17.7 and 17.10 to 17.12, and McFadden's pseudo R squared on page 531; and AME section 7.4, printed pages 138 to 139, with Tabela 7.1, and section 8.1.2, printed pages 142 to 143.

The claim. A binary y is Bernoulli with an individual probability pi i, and logistic regression makes the log-odds, the log of pi i over one minus pi i, linear in x i. The log-likelihood is concave; its score is X transposed times y minus pi; its Hessian is minus X transposed W X; and one Newton-Raphson step is one weighted least squares fit on an adjusted response z. That is iteratively reweighted least squares, IRLS for short. Coefficients are read as odds ratios, e to the beta j. A probability cut-off c maps to a covariate cut-off, x star equal to the logit of c minus beta zero, over beta one. And a classifier is judged by its confusion matrix, not by one error rate. Six slips in the deck, on slides 9, 10, 15, 18, 22 and 38, are corrected where they arise, plus a reversed comment in the admission script.

The notation. y i, zero or one, is the response of individual i, with one meaning "yes": default, spam, admitted. x i is the covariate vector with a leading one, and X the n by p plus one matrix with rows x i transposed. Beta is the coefficient vector, and eta i, equal to x i transposed beta, is the linear predictor, any real number. Pi i, the probability that y i equals one given x i, lies between zero and one; the deck also writes it g of x i transposed beta. g of mu, e to the mu over one plus e to the mu, is the logistic function; Wooldridge writes capital Lambda; and its inverse, the log of pi over one minus pi, is the logit. l of beta is the log-likelihood; W, the diagonal matrix of pi i times one minus pi i, is the weight matrix; z is the IRLS adjusted response. c is a probability cut-off, and x star the covariate value where the fitted probability equals c. TN, FP, FN and TP are the confusion matrix counts: true negatives, false positives, false negatives and true positives.

Four point one. Intuition: why a straight line fails on a zero-one response.

The default data, from ISLR, with 10,000 customers, slide 4, has 9,667 non-defaulters and 333 defaulters, slide 12. Regressing the zero-one indicator on balance by least squares, the linear probability model, ISLP equation 4.1, page 139, and Wooldridge section 17.1, page 525, gives, slide 13: the fitted probability equals minus 0.07519 plus 0.0001299 times balance.

It crosses zero at 0.07519 over 0.0001299, which is 578.8, so every customer with a balance under 579 dollars gets a negative "probability". At a balance of 2000 it says minus 0.07519 plus 0.2598, which is 0.185, where the logit says 0.586. Wooldridge's two complaints, page 525, are exactly these: fitted values outside zero to one, and a constant partial effect. The fix is to model a transformation of pi that is free to range over the whole real line.

The figure has two panels, drawn from the printed coefficients only, since the data are not on disk. On the left, the logistic curve for the probability of default given balance, with coefficients minus 10.6513 and 0.0055, against the least squares line, marked at balance 1000, 0.58 per cent, at 2000, 58.6 per cent, and at the 50 per cent cut-off, 1936.6. On the right, the balance plus student logit curves for students and non-students, with 50 per cent cut-offs at 1998 and 1873, and the probabilities at balance 1500, 5.4 and 10.5 per cent.

The logit is flat near zero for balances below about 1,000, rises steepest where the fitted probability is one half, and saturates near one. The straight line cannot bend, so it is too high at small balances, too low at large ones, and wrong in sign at the left end. On the right the two curves are horizontal translates of each other: the dummy moves the intercept, which is all a dummy can do in a logit without interactions.

Four point two. The model: the logistic function, the odds, and the logit. Slides 5 and 6.

The model, slide 5, ISLP equation 4.2, page 139, Wooldridge equations 17.2 and 17.3, pages 525 to 526. Equation 1: pi i, the probability that y i equals one given x i, equals e to the x i transposed beta, over one plus e to the x i transposed beta, which is g of x i transposed beta.

From equation 1 to the log-odds, one step at a time, writing eta i for x i transposed beta. Equation 2: one minus pi i equals one minus e to the eta over one plus e to the eta, which is one plus e to the eta minus e to the eta, over one plus e to the eta, which is one over one plus e to the eta. Divide equation 1 by equation 2. Equation 3: pi i over one minus pi i equals e to the eta i. These are the odds, ISLP equation 4.3, page 140. Take logs of equation 3. Equation 4: the log of pi i over one minus pi i equals x i transposed beta: the log-odds, or logit, ISLP equation 4.4, page 140.

The generalized linear model reading, slide 6. y i is Bernoulli with probability pi i, with equation 5: the conditional mean of y i is pi i, which is g of x i transposed beta, and the conditional variance is pi i times one minus pi i. The logit is the link: it maps the mean to the scale on which the model is linear. Section 6 uses the same template with a log link.

Four point two point one. Reading a coefficient: the odds ratio and the partial effect.

Raise x j by one unit, everything else fixed. From equation 3 the odds go from e to the eta to e to the eta plus beta j. Equation 6: the odds at x j plus one over the odds at x j equals e to the beta j: the odds ratio, ISLP page 140.

The effect on the probability is not constant. Differentiate equation 1 with the quotient rule. Equation 7: the derivative of g with respect to eta is e to the eta times one plus e to the eta minus e to the eta times e to the eta, over one plus e to the eta squared, which is e to the eta over one plus e to the eta squared, which is g of eta times one minus g of eta. So by the chain rule, equation 8: the derivative of pi i with respect to x i j is beta j times pi i times one minus pi i, which is at most beta j over four. The maximum, beta j over four, is at pi i equal to one half. Wooldridge equation 17.7, page 527, writes the partial effect as g of the linear predictor times beta j, and for the logit g at zero is 0.25.

Worked, on default, with slide 15's coefficient of 0.0055 per dollar of balance. Four quantities. The odds ratio per dollar is e to the 0.0055, which is 1.0055. The odds ratio per 100 dollars is e to the 0.0055 times 100, e to the 0.55, which is 1.733, plus 73 per cent. The partial effect at the 50 per cent cut-off is 0.0055 times 0.5 times 0.5, which is 0.001375 per dollar, or 13.75 percentage points per 100 dollars. The partial effect at a balance of 1000, where the fitted probability is 0.005759, is 0.0055 times 0.005759 times 0.994241, which is 0.0000315 per dollar, or 0.31 percentage points per 100 dollars.

The odds ratio is the same everywhere; the probability effect is 44 times larger at the cut-off than at a balance of 1,000. "A one-unit increase in balance raises the log-odds by 0.0055", the caption of ISLP Table 4.1, page 142, is exact; "raises the probability by 0.0055" is wrong.

Four point three. The likelihood, the score and the Hessian. Slides 7 and 8.

The likelihood, slide 7, ISLP equation 4.5, page 141, Wooldridge equation 17.10, page 528. Independent Bernoulli draws. Equation 9: the probability of y one to y n given the x's and beta is the product over i of pi i to the y i times one minus pi i to the one minus y i, which is the product of pi i over the individuals with y equal to one, times the product of one minus pi i over those with y equal to zero.

This is the Bernoulli likelihood of Class 1 section 1 with one change: theta is no longer common; each pi i is tied to x i through equation 1.

The log-likelihood, Wooldridge equation 17.11. Equation 10: l of beta equals the sum over i of y i log pi i plus one minus y i times log of one minus pi i.

Rewrite each term in eta i. Group the y i terms: y i log pi i plus one minus y i log of one minus pi i equals y i times the log of pi i over one minus pi i, plus log of one minus pi i. Substitute equation 4 for the log-odds and equation 2 for one minus pi i. Equation 11: l of beta equals the sum over i of y i times x i transposed beta, plus the log of one over one plus e to the x i transposed beta; which is the sum of y i x i transposed beta minus the log of one plus e to the x i transposed beta.

The score, slide 8. Differentiate equation 11; the derivative of the log of one plus e to the eta is e to the eta over one plus e to the eta, which is pi. Equation 12: the score equals the sum over i of y i x i minus pi i x i, which is the sum of x i times y i minus pi i, which is X transposed times y minus pi. Setting equation 12 to zero gives p plus one equations, nonlinear in beta because pi i is.

The Hessian, slide 8. Differentiate equation 12 once more; only pi i depends on beta, and by equation 7, the derivative of pi i with respect to beta transposed is pi i times one minus pi i times x i transposed. Equation 13: the Hessian is minus the sum over i of x i x i transposed times pi i times one minus pi i, which is minus X transposed W X, with W the diagonal matrix of pi i times one minus pi i.

Two consequences.

First. Every pi i times one minus pi i is positive, so X transposed W X is positive definite when X has full column rank: the log-likelihood is strictly concave, and a stationary point is the unique maximum. A G-layer caution: if some linear combination of x separates the zeros from the ones perfectly, the log-likelihood keeps rising as beta grows without bound and no finite maximum likelihood estimate exists. That is the "unstable when classes are well-separated" bullet on slide 40.

Second. The first row of equation 12 at beta hat, the intercept's column of ones, reads: the sum of y i minus pi i hat is zero. The average fitted probability equals the sample share of ones, which is 0.0333 in default, ISLP page 140.

Four point four. Newton-Raphson is iteratively reweighted least squares. Slides 8 and 9.

Newton-Raphson, slide 8. Starting from beta old, equation 14: beta new equals beta old minus the inverse of the Hessian times the score, both evaluated at beta old.

Restating the goal: show that this step is a weighted least squares fit. Four steps.

Step one. Substitute equations 12 and 13 into equation 14. Equation 15: beta new equals beta old minus the inverse of minus X transposed W X, times X transposed times y minus pi, which is beta old plus X transposed W X inverse times X transposed times y minus pi.

Step two. Factor X transposed W X inverse out of both terms, writing beta old as X transposed W X inverse times X transposed W X times beta old. Then beta new equals X transposed W X inverse times the bracket X transposed W X beta old plus X transposed times y minus pi.

Step three. Insert W times W inverse, the identity, in front of y minus pi; W is diagonal with positive entries. Equation 16: beta new equals X transposed W X inverse, times X transposed W, times the bracket X beta old plus W inverse times y minus pi, which is X transposed W X inverse times X transposed W z.

Step four. Equation 17 defines the adjusted response: z equals X beta old plus W inverse times y minus pi; entry by entry, z i equals eta i old plus y i minus pi i over pi i times one minus pi i.

Equation 16 is the weighted least squares estimator of a regression of z on X with weights W. Equation 18: beta new is the minimiser over beta of z minus X beta transposed, times W, times z minus X beta; its normal equations, X transposed W times z minus X beta equal to zero, give equation 16. Slide 9 prints the objective as z minus X beta transposed times z minus X beta; the weight matrix is needed, because without it the minimiser is X transposed X inverse times X transposed z, not equation 16. Recompute pi and W at beta new and repeat: the weights are re-weighted each pass, hence the name. R's glm reports "Fisher scoring iterations"; for the logit link Fisher scoring and Newton-Raphson coincide, because the Hessian does not depend on y.

Standard errors come from the same matrix at convergence: the estimated variance of beta hat is X transposed W hat X inverse, the inverse observed information. That is the "Std. Error" column of every glm printout below, and the z statistic is beta j hat over its standard error, ISLP page 141, Wooldridge page 529.

Four point seven. Course and graduate layers: deviance and residuals. Slide 10.

The Pearson residual, r i, is y i minus pi i hat over the square root of pi i hat times one minus pi i hat. The deviance residual, equation 22: d i equals the sign of y i minus pi i hat, times the square root of two times the bracket y i log of y i over pi i hat, plus one minus y i times log of one minus y i over one minus pi i hat.

For zero-one data the deviance is minus twice the log-likelihood. With y i either zero or one, y i log y i is zero and one minus y i times log of one minus y i is zero, with the convention zero log zero equals zero. So equation 23: d i squared equals two times minus y i log pi i hat minus one minus y i times log of one minus pi i hat, and the deviance, the sum of the d i squared, equals minus twice the log-likelihood at beta hat, by equation 10.

A check on the null model: with a fitted probability of 0.0333 for everyone, minus two times the quantity 333 log 0.0333 plus 9667 log 0.9667 is 2920.6, the printed null deviance.

Two slips on slide 10: the statistics are the Pearson chi-squared, the sum of r i squared, and the deviance, the sum of d i squared; the squares are missing. A G-layer caution: with one observation per covariate pattern, the chi-squared reference with n minus p degrees of freedom for the deviance itself is unreliable; differences of deviances between nested models, as in section 4.5.3, are the safe use.

Four point nine. The spam confusion matrix. Slides 28 and 36 to 38.

The data: 4,601 e-mails labelled spam, y equal to one, or ham, y equal to zero; 40 per cent spam; 57 word-frequency predictors, from Efron and Hastie, 2016, pages 113 to 115, cited on slide 28. Trained on 2,300, tested on 2,301, slide 33.

The vocabulary, ISLP Tables 4.6 and 4.7, page 156, AME Tabela 7.1, page 138, with "positive" meaning spam. A ham predicted ham is a true negative. A ham predicted spam is a false positive: a real e-mail sent to the spam folder. A spam predicted ham is a false negative: a spam let through. A spam predicted spam is a true positive. The false negative rate is FN over FN plus TP, which is one minus sensitivity. The false positive rate is FP over FP plus TN, which is one minus specificity, the Type I error. And the false discovery rate is FP over FP plus TP, which is one minus precision.

Part three, section 5. The Bayes rule, the Gaussian cut-offs, and what unequal costs do to them.

This section covers the discriminant part of the logistic regression and discriminant analysis deck, slides 40 to 65, after Mardia, Kent and Bibby, Multivariate Analysis, chapter 11, which is not on disk; ISLP section 4.4, printed pages 146 to 158, with equations 4.15 to 4.19, 4.24 and 4.26 to 4.28, Tables 4.4 and 4.5, the ROC curve and AUC on pages 154 to 155, and the parameter count on page 157; ISLP section 4.5.1, printed pages 161 to 163, equations 4.31 to 4.33; AME chapter 7, printed pages 134 to 135 and 138 to 139, with equation 7.1, Teorema 9 on page 135 and Tabela 7.1 on page 138; AME section 8.1.4, printed pages 147 to 149; and AME section 9.1, printed pages 169 to 171, on the ROC curve and the cost-weighted cut-off on page 170.

The claim. Discriminant analysis models the density of x given y equal to k and turns it into the probability that y equals k given x with Bayes' theorem. The Bayes rule allocates to the class with the largest pi k times the density of x given class k, and no rule has a smaller probability of error. With two classes everything runs through h of x, the log density of x under class one minus the log density under class two: allocate to class one if and only if h of x exceeds the log of pi two c one two over pi one c two one. Equal Gaussian variances make h linear: one cut-off at the midpoint, linear discriminant analysis, LDA for short, and the Mahalanobis distance. Unequal variances make it quadratic: in the deck's example class one wins both tails, with cut-offs 1.680 and 10.001 and a total error of 0.251, against 0.369 for the equal-variance case.

The notation. y, from one to kappa, is the class label, with kappa equal to two unless stated; x is the predictor vector. Pi k, the probability that y equals k, is the prior probability, and the priors sum to one; here pi k is a prior, not the Bernoulli mean of section 4. p k of x is the class-conditional density or probability mass function, with mean mu k and variance sigma k squared in one dimension, or mean vector mu k and covariance Sigma, or Sigma k, in several. R k is the allocation region of class k, and phi k of x, the indicator that x is in R k, is the allocation function. h of x is the discriminant function for two classes. p i j, the integral of phi i times p j, is the probability of allocating to class i an individual from class j. c i j, positive for i different from j, is the cost of allocating to i when the truth is j, and c k k is zero. Capital Phi is the standard normal distribution function. Alpha is Sigma inverse times mu one minus mu two, and mu bar is the average of the two means.

Five point one. Intuition: two routes to the same posterior. Slide 40.

Logistic regression, section 4, models the probability that y equals k given x directly. Discriminant analysis models how x is distributed within each class and inverts with Bayes' theorem. When the classes are Gaussian the two give posteriors of the same form, section 5.7.1, so why bother? Slide 40 and ISLP page 146 give three reasons: the logistic maximum likelihood estimate is unstable when the classes are well separated, and may not exist, as section 4.3 noted; it is unstable when n is small; and it is clumsy for more than two classes. Discriminant analysis has none of these problems, at the price of assuming a distribution for x.

Five point two. Discriminant rules and the Bayes rule. Slides 41 and 42.

A discriminant rule, slide 41: a partition of the predictor space into disjoint regions R one to R kappa whose union is the whole space; allocate x to class k if and only if x is in R k. The slide prints an intersection; a partition needs the union.

Bayes' theorem, slide 42, ISLP equation 4.15, page 147. Equation 1: the probability that y equals k given x equals pi k times p k of x, divided by the sum over j of pi j times p j of x.

The Bayes discriminant rule. Equation 2: allocate to k star, the class that maximises the posterior probability, which is the class that maximises pi k times p k of x, because the denominator of equation 1 is the same for every k. Equivalently, phi k of x is one if and only if pi k p k of x is the largest of the pi j p j of x. The maximum likelihood discriminant rule is equation 2 with equal priors, one over kappa each: the class that maximises p k of x.

Five point two point one. Why equation 2 minimises the probability of error. AME Teorema 9, page 135.

Two classes. An error happens when x is in R two but y is one, or x is in R one but y is two. So the probability of error is pi one p two one plus pi two p one two, which is pi one times the integral of phi two times p one, plus pi two times the integral of phi one times p two. Put both under one integral. Equation 3: the probability of error is the integral of phi two of x times pi one p one of x, plus phi one of x times pi two p two of x.

At each x exactly one of phi one and phi two is one, so the integrand is either pi one p one of x, if we allocate to two, or pi two p two of x, if we allocate to one. The integral is smallest when, pointwise, we pay the smaller of the two: allocate to one if and only if pi one p one of x exceeds pi two p two of x, which is equation 2. AME writes the same rule as the probability of class one given x being at least one half, page 135; this is the zero-one loss risk, the probability that Y differs from g of X, of AME equation 7.1, page 134.

Five point three. Two classes: the discriminant function. Slide 49.

Equation 4: h of x equals the log of the density of x given y equal to one, minus the log of the density given y equal to two.

The maximum likelihood rule: allocate to one if h of x is positive, to two if it is negative.

The Bayes rule from equation 2. Equation 5: allocate to one if and only if pi one p one of x exceeds pi two p two of x, if and only if p one over p two exceeds pi two over pi one, if and only if h of x exceeds the log of pi two over pi one. That is, divide by pi one p two of x, which is positive, then take logs, which preserves order.

The posterior is a logistic function of h. Divide the numerator and denominator of equation 1, with k equal to one, by pi one p one of x. Rule 5 is the posterior exceeding one half, written on the h scale.

Five point four. Worked: the zero-one predictor and the multinomial. Slides 43 and 44.

Bernoulli, slide 43. x given class one is Bernoulli with probability one half; x given class two is Bernoulli with probability three quarters. By equation 4: h of zero is the log of one half over one quarter, which is log 2, which is 0.693, positive, so class one. h of one is the log of one half over three quarters, which is log of two thirds, which is minus 0.405, negative, so class two.

The maximum likelihood misallocation probabilities: p two one, the probability that x equals one given class one, is one half; and p one two, the probability that x equals zero given class two, is one quarter. With equal priors, the probability of error is one half times the quantity one half plus one quarter, which is three eighths.

Priors move the rule. x equal to zero goes to class one if and only if log 2 exceeds the log of pi two over pi one, if and only if pi two over pi one is less than 2, if and only if pi one exceeds one third. x equal to one goes to class one if and only if the log of two thirds exceeds the log of pi two over pi one, if and only if pi one exceeds three fifths. Hence three regimes. With pi one below one third: everything to class two. With pi one between one third and three fifths: the maximum likelihood rule, zero to class one and one to class two. With pi one above three fifths: everything to class one.

Multinomial, slide 44. x given class one is multinomial with n trials and probabilities alpha one to alpha kappa; given class two, with probabilities beta one to beta kappa. The factor n factorial over the product of the x k factorials is common and cancels in equation 4. So h of x is the sum of x k log alpha k minus the sum of x k log beta k, which is the sum over k of x k log of alpha k over beta k. The maximum likelihood rule allocates to class one if and only if that sum is positive. Slide 44 prints "less than zero", a sign slip. The check: with kappa equal to 2, n equal to one, alpha equal to one half and one half, and beta equal to one quarter and three quarters, which is the Bernoulli example, x equal to one and zero gives log 2, positive, and must go to class one.

Five point nine. Admissibility. Slides 50 and 52.

A rule d is as good as d prime if p k k is at least p prime k k for every class k; better if at least one inequality is strict; and admissible if no rule is better.

Theorem, slides 50 and 52: all Bayes rules, the maximum likelihood rule included, are admissible. Theorem: under priors pi k, no rule has a larger posterior probability of correct allocation, or a smaller Bayes risk, than the Bayes rule for those priors; section 5.2.1 is its proof for two classes.

Worked on the Bernoulli example of section 5.4. The four possible rules, each stated as where x equal to zero goes and where x equal to one goes, with p one one and p two two.

Zero to class one and one to class two, the maximum likelihood rule: p one one one half, p two two three quarters; admissible, the Bayes rule for pi one between one third and three fifths.
Both to class two: p one one zero, p two two one; admissible, the Bayes rule for pi one below one third.
Both to class one: p one one one, p two two zero; admissible, the Bayes rule for pi one above three fifths.
Zero to class two and one to class one, the reversed rule: p one one one half, p two two one quarter; inadmissible, because the maximum likelihood rule has the same p one one and a larger p two two.

Admissible is a weak property: "all to class two" is admissible. It only rules out rules that are beaten everywhere; choosing among admissible rules needs priors and costs.

ISLP reports an AUC of 0.95 for LDA on default, page 155. AME insists the curve be drawn on validation or test data, page 170.

The figure shows the exact ROC curves for the deck's Gaussian example, with class two as the "positive" class, so the false positive rate is p two one and the true positive rate is one minus p one two, each point a threshold k in "allocate to two if h of x is below k". The unequal-variance curve, AUC 0.802, bows above the equal-variance curve, AUC 0.681, with the maximum likelihood operating points at 0.388 and 0.885, and at 0.369 and 0.631, and the diagonal. Part four, section 6. Counts with a log link: the blog visitor example, rate ratios, and the mean-variance check.

This section covers the page Gaussian versus Poisson regression, by Lopes, dated October 27th, 2025, with data from Albert and Hu, 2020, Probability and Bayesian Modeling, section 8.8: 28 daily blog visitor counts and the linear and Poisson outputs; ISLP section 4.6, printed pages 167 to 173, with equations 4.35 to 4.42, the rate ratio reading on page 171, the mean-variance tie and non-negativity on page 172, and footnote 5 on overdispersion; and Wooldridge section 17.3, printed pages 543 to 546, with equations 17.31 to 17.35, the exponential of beta minus one reading on page 544, and the sigma hat squared adjustment on page 546.

The claim. A count is modelled as Poisson with log lambda i equal to x i transposed beta, so a coefficient is a rate ratio, e to the beta j; fitted means are always positive; and the variance is tied to the mean. On the site's 28 days of blog visits, Gaussian and Poisson regressions on a weekend dummy give the same fitted means, 109 and 83.33 visitors, because the model is saturated in two groups. They differ in the variance they assume, constant at 140.4 against equal to the mean, and therefore in their standard errors. The data are mildly overdispersed, with sigma hat squared equal to 1.44, which widens the Poisson standard error by 20 per cent and changes no conclusion.

The notation. y i is the number of visitors on day i, for 28 days in June 2018. w i is the page's weekend dummy, equal to one for the first three days of each week. Lambda i, the conditional mean of y i, is the Poisson mean, and alpha and beta are the intercept and slope of log lambda i equal to alpha plus beta w i, the page's letters. beta zero and beta one are the least squares intercept and slope of the Gaussian model, and s its residual standard error. n zero, 16, and S zero, 1744, are the number of weekdays and their total; n one, 12, and S one, 1000, are the same for the weekend group. Sigma squared is the dispersion factor in the variance of y given x equal to sigma squared times the mean, Wooldridge equation 17.35.

Six point one. Intuition: the data and the question.

Visitors to a baseball analytics blog, 28 consecutive days, in four weeks of seven. Week one: 95, 81, 85, 100, 111, 130, 113. Week two: 92, 65, 78, 96, 118, 120, 104. Week three: 91, 91, 79, 106, 91, 114, 110. Week four: 98, 61, 84, 96, 126, 119, 90.

The page labels the weekdays Friday, Saturday, Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, and sets the weekend dummy to one for the first three: Friday, Saturday and Sunday. June 1st, 2018 was a Friday.

The group summaries, page output recomputed. Weekdays, Monday to Thursday, with the dummy zero: 16 days, total 1744, mean 109.00, standard deviation 12.15, variance 147.73, variance over mean 1.36. The "weekend", Friday to Sunday, with the dummy one: 12 days, total 1000, mean 83.33, standard deviation 11.42, variance 130.42, variance over mean 1.57.

The question: how much lower is traffic on weekend days, and how sure are we? A count is non-negative, integer, and typically noisier when its mean is higher: three things the Gaussian linear model ignores, ISLP section 4.6.1, pages 167 to 169, Wooldridge page 543.

Six point two. The Gaussian regression.

Equation 1: y i equals beta zero plus beta one w i plus epsilon i, with the errors iid normal with mean zero and variance sigma epsilon squared.

With one dummy, least squares reproduces the group means. The normal equations are: the sum of y i minus beta zero minus beta one w i is zero; and the sum of w i times y i minus beta zero minus beta one w i is zero. Subtracting the second from the first leaves the sum over weekdays of y i minus beta zero equal to zero, so beta zero hat is S zero over n zero; the second alone gives beta zero hat plus beta one hat equal to S one over n one. So beta zero hat is 1744 over 16, which is 109.000, and beta one hat is 1000 over 12 minus 109, which is 83.333 minus 109, which is minus 25.667.

Standard errors, from the pooled residual variance with 28 minus 2, or 26, degrees of freedom. The page prints s equal to 11.85. The standard error of beta one hat is s times the square root of one over n zero plus one over n one, which is 11.8495 times the square root of one over 16 plus one over 12, which is 11.8495 times 0.38188, which is 4.525. The standard error of beta zero hat is s over the square root of n zero, which is 11.8495 over 4, which is 2.962.

t equals minus 25.667 over 4.525, which is minus 5.67, with p equal to 5.8 times 10 to the minus 6, and the R squared is 0.553, all as printed. The reading: weekend days have 25.7 fewer visitors, in absolute terms, with one common spread, s squared equal to 140.4, for both groups.

Six point three. The Poisson regression.

The model, ISLP equations 4.35 to 4.37, pages 169 to 170, Wooldridge equations 17.31 and 17.32, page 544. Equation 2: y i given w i is Poisson with mean lambda i; the probability that y i equals k is e to the minus lambda i times lambda i to the k, over k factorial; and log lambda i equals alpha plus beta w i.

The log-likelihood, ISLP equation 4.38, page 170, Wooldridge equation 17.33, page 545. Take logs of the probability mass function and sum. Equation 3: l of alpha and beta equals the sum over the 28 days of y i log lambda i minus lambda i minus log y i factorial, which is the sum of y i times alpha plus beta w i, minus e to the alpha plus beta w i, minus log y i factorial.

The score. Differentiate equation 3; the derivative of e to the alpha plus beta w i with respect to alpha is lambda i, and with respect to beta is w i lambda i. Equation 4: the derivative with respect to alpha, the sum of y i minus lambda i, is zero; and the derivative with respect to beta, the sum of w i times y i minus lambda i, is zero. In matrix form, X transposed times y minus lambda is zero: the same shape as the logit score of section 4, equation 12. Both are canonical-link generalized linear models.

Solve equation 4 in closed form. The second equation involves only weekend days, where lambda i is e to the alpha plus beta: S one minus n one times e to the alpha plus beta is zero, so e to the alpha hat plus beta hat is 1000 over 12, which is 83.333. Subtract it from the first; what remains are the weekdays, where lambda i is e to the alpha: S zero minus n zero times e to the alpha is zero, so e to the alpha hat is 1744 over 16, which is 109. Take logs: alpha hat is log 109, which is 4.69135; and beta hat is the log of 83.333 over 109, which is the log of 0.764526, which is minus 0.26850. These are the page's estimates. With no covariate at all, equation 4 would give lambda hat equal to y bar, the Poisson maximum likelihood estimator of Class 1 section 2; here each group gets its own sample mean.

The Hessian and standard errors. Differentiate equation 4 again. Minus the Hessian is the sum over i of lambda i times the matrix with first row one and w i, and second row w i and w i; which is the matrix with first row n zero e to the alpha plus n one e to the alpha plus beta, and n one e to the alpha plus beta; and second row n one e to the alpha plus beta, and n one e to the alpha plus beta.

At the estimate, n zero e to the alpha hat is 1744 and n one e to the alpha hat plus beta hat is 1000. So the information matrix has first row 2744 and 1000, and second row 1000 and 1000. Its determinant is 2744 times 1000 minus 1000 squared, which is 1,744,000. Its inverse is one over 1,744,000 times the matrix with first row 1000 and minus 1000, and second row minus 1000 and 2744. So the standard error of alpha hat is the square root of one over 1744, which is 0.02395, and the standard error of beta hat is the square root of one over 1744 plus one over 1000, which is 0.03967. These are the printed 0.02395 and 0.03967: each standard error is built from the group totals alone. The z statistic is minus 0.26850 over 0.03967, which is minus 6.77. The page's "Number of Fisher Scoring iterations: 4" is IRLS with W the diagonal matrix of the lambda i and z i equal to eta i plus y i minus lambda i over lambda i: section 4.4 with the log link.

Six point four. Reading the two slopes.

Gaussian: additive, in visitors: beta one hat is minus 25.667 per day.

Poisson: multiplicative. From equation 2, lambda at w equal to one over lambda at w equal to zero is e to the alpha plus beta over e to the alpha, which is e to the beta: the rate ratio. ISLP page 171: "an increase in X j by one unit is associated with a change in E of Y by a factor of exp of beta j". Here e to the minus 0.26850 is 0.7645, so weekend traffic is 100 times 0.7645 minus one, which is minus 23.5 per cent relative to weekday traffic.

Wooldridge's shortcut, 100 beta hat, minus 26.85 per cent, page 544, is the log approximation; for a dummy the exact change is 100 times the exponential of beta hat minus one, minus 23.55 per cent, on the same page. At an absolute beta near 0.27 the two differ by 3.30 points.

Same means, different variance. The page's comparison. The Gaussian weekday mean, beta zero hat, is 109.0000, and the weekend mean, beta zero hat plus beta one hat, is 83.333. The Poisson weekday mean, e to the alpha hat, is 109.0002, and the weekend mean, e to the alpha hat plus beta hat, is 83.33345. The fourth-decimal gaps come from exponentiating the five-decimal printed coefficients.

With two groups and two parameters both models are saturated in the means; what differs is the variance each assumes, hence t equal to minus 5.67 against z equal to minus 6.77. Compare the two models in four dimensions. The variance on weekdays: Gaussian, s squared equal to 140.4; Poisson, lambda equal to 109. The variance on the weekend: Gaussian, 140.4; Poisson, 83.3. Fitted values: Gaussian, can be negative in general; Poisson, always positive, ISLP page 172. The slope reading: Gaussian, minus 25.7 visitors; Poisson, times 0.7645, minus 23.5 per cent.

Six point five. The mean-variance check and overdispersion.

The Poisson model says the variance of y given x equals its mean, Wooldridge equation 17.34, page 545, ISLP page 172. The sample says 147.7 against 109, and 130.4 against 83.3: ratios of 1.36 and 1.57.

The dispersion estimate, Wooldridge page 546: sigma hat squared equals one over n minus k minus one, times the sum of u i hat squared over y i hat, with u i hat equal to y i minus lambda i hat; that is, the Pearson chi-squared over its degrees of freedom. Here sigma hat squared is 37.546 over 26, which is 1.444, and sigma hat is 1.202.

The quasi-Poisson correction, Wooldridge equation 17.35 and page 546: multiply the standard errors by sigma hat. The corrected standard error of beta hat is 1.202 times 0.03967, which is 0.0477, and the corrected z is minus 0.26850 over 0.0477, which is minus 5.63.

The deviance view. The residual deviance is 38.324 on 26 degrees of freedom, a ratio of 1.47; the probability that a chi-squared with 26 degrees of freedom exceeds 38.324 is 0.056: borderline evidence of lack of fit. The likelihood-ratio test of the weekend effect is 85.141 minus 38.324, which is 46.82, on one degree of freedom, Wooldridge equation 17.12; under equation 17.35, divide by sigma hat squared: 46.82 over 1.444 is 32.4. The AIC is 221.76.

The figure has two panels. On the left, the 28 daily counts by day, with weekday and Friday-to-Sunday markers, the common fitted step function at 109 and 83.3, the constant-width Gaussian band and the mean-dependent Poisson band, each plus or minus 1.96 standard deviations. On the right, group variance against group mean, with the lines variance equals mean, variance equals 1.444 times the mean, and variance equals 140.4.

On the left the Gaussian band has the same width in both groups, while the Poisson band is narrower for the lower weekend mean: the square root of 83.3 is 9.1, against the square root of 109, 10.4. The weekday 130 and the weekend 65 and 61 sit outside the Poisson band, 109 plus or minus 20.5 and 83.3 plus or minus 17.9: three of 28, where about 1.4 are expected, which is the overdispersion. On the right both sample points lie above the Poisson line, variance equals mean, and close to the quasi-Poisson line, 1.444 times lambda; the Gaussian constant, 140.4, sits between them.

With means near 100 the Poisson and Gaussian models nearly agree: a Poisson with a large mean is close to a normal. The log link earns its keep with small counts, where linear fits go negative, as in 9.6 per cent of ISLP's Bikeshare fitted values, page 168, and the variance visibly grows with the mean, ISLP page 172, recalling Figure 4.14. And Poisson standard errors are only right if the variance equals the mean: always check, and inflate by sigma hat when it fails. ISLP footnote 5, page 172: overdispersion "causes the Z-values to be inflated".

Six point six. Graduate layer: one template for linear, logistic and Poisson regression. ISLP section 4.6.3, pages 172 to 173. Not examinable.

ISLP equation 4.42, page 173: a link function eta applied to the conditional mean of Y equals beta zero plus beta one X one up to beta p X p.

Three models in five dimensions. The linear model: Gaussian distribution, identity link, constant variance sigma squared, score X transposed times y minus X beta over sigma squared; Class 1 section 6. The logistic model: Bernoulli distribution, logit link, the log of mu over one minus mu, variance function mu times one minus mu, score X transposed times y minus pi; section 4. The Poisson model: Poisson distribution, log link, variance function mu, score X transposed times y minus lambda; this section.

Each link is the canonical one for its distribution, which is why the score is always X transposed times the data minus the fitted mean, and why Newton-Raphson, Fisher scoring and IRLS coincide.

Recap of part 2. Logistic regression makes the log-odds linear; its score is X transposed times y minus pi, its Hessian minus X transposed W X, and a Newton-Raphson step is a weighted least squares fit, which converges on the student table to minus 3.50413 and 0.40489. Coefficients are odds ratios; a probability cut-off is a covariate cut-off, 1936.6 at one half; confounding flips the student sign; and a confusion matrix needs several rates, with a false discovery rate of 9.06 per cent for the spam model. Discriminant analysis uses the Bayes rule, which minimises the error probability; equal Gaussian variances give a midpoint and a linear rule, unequal ones give a quadratic rule with cut-offs 1.680 and 10.001 and total error 0.2515; unequal costs move the threshold to the log of pi two c one two over pi one c two one. Poisson regression reads coefficients as rate ratios, 0.7645 for the blog weekend, and ties the variance to the mean, a tie that fails mildly here with a dispersion of 1.444.
