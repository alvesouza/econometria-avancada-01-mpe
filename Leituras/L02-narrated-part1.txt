Part zero. The map of Class 2.

This part covers the map note: the one claim, the reading order, the numbers to memorize, the scope, the errata, and the place of the class in the course.

The one claim, in three sentences. First: under squared loss the target is the regression function, the conditional expectation of Y given X, and every learned estimate of it misses a new Y by squared bias plus variance plus irreducible noise. Flexibility trades the first two, so the test error is U-shaped while the training error only falls, which is why the training error can never choose the model, and cross-validation, by hold-out, leave-one-out or k-fold, estimates the test error instead. Second: classification is the same problem with zero-one loss. Logistic regression models the probability that y equals one given x directly; discriminant analysis builds it from the density of x given y by Bayes' theorem; and the cut-off is a statement about costs, not a constant one half. Third: counts get a log link. Poisson regression reads coefficients as rate ratios and ties the variance to the mean, a tie that must be checked.

The one result to hold. The expected squared prediction error equals the integral of the squared bias of psi hat at x over the distribution of X, which falls with flexibility; plus the integral of the variance of psi hat at x, which rises with flexibility; plus sigma squared, which is untouchable. Every other section is a way to estimate this or to trade its terms. Section 3 estimates the left side from held-out data. The choice between linear and quadratic discriminant analysis, in section 5, is a bias-variance choice. And the classification version replaces squared loss by zero-one loss, whose minimiser is the Bayes rule, the class k that maximises pi k times p k of x: the classification analogue of the conditional expectation.

The reading order. Part one, regression as prediction: section 1, prediction error and the bias-variance decomposition; section 2, linear and log-linear regression. Part two, estimating the test error: section 3, cross-validation on the motorcycle data. Part three, classification: section 4, logistic regression; section 5, discriminant analysis. Part four, counts: section 6, Gaussian against Poisson regression. Then the support notes: the formula sheet, the pitfalls, and the Python implementation.

The place of Class 2 in the course. From Class 1: mean squared error equals variance plus squared bias for one parameter becomes the decomposition pointwise in x; least squares, the hat matrix, the R squared, AIC, BIC and overfitting are the tools section 3 reuses; the Bernoulli and Poisson likelihoods become the logistic and Poisson regressions; and the bootstrap and its failure for the maximum precede the bootstrap band. In Classes 3 and 4, Bayesian learning, the posterior predictive replaces the plug-in, with Bayesian logit, Poisson and model comparison, ridge and lasso as posterior modes, and Bayes rules and loss functions generalising the cost-based cut-off. In Classes 5 and 6, causal inference, the inference column of section 1 becomes formal: which controls are legitimate, as in the Mroz return to education that vanishes, and confounding, as in the student sign flip. In Class 7, generalized linear models return, with probit, ordered and multinomial probit beside the logit, Tobit, and heteroskedastic errors like the motorcycle noise. In Class 8, model choice by information criteria and out-of-sample error continues, now with time order and no random folds. In Class 9, shrinkage in large Bayesian vector autoregressions is the bias-variance trade-off at scale.

Recap of part zero. The best predictor under squared loss is the conditional mean; every learned estimate pays squared bias, variance and noise; the training error cannot choose flexibility, so cross-validation estimates the test error; classification uses the Bayes rule with a cost-based cut-off; and counts use a log link with a variance tie that must be checked.


Part one, section 1. The best predictor is the conditional mean, and every estimate of it pays squared bias, variance and noise.

This section covers Marques' Fundamentos deck, slides 2 to 30, and the script bias variance; ISLP section 2.1, printed page 15, equation 2.1; section 2.1.1, printed pages 17 to 20, equations 2.2 and 2.3; sections 2.2.1 and 2.2.2, printed pages 28 to 34, equations 2.5 to 2.7; and AME section 1.4, printed pages 9 to 11, with Teorema 1 and Observação 1.3; section 1.5.1, printed page 14, equation 1.1; and section 1.5.2, printed pages 22 to 23.

The claim. Under squared loss the best possible predictor of Y from X is the regression function psi of x, the expected value of Y given X equal to x: a two-line calculus result. Any learned psi hat then misses Y by three separate amounts: noise, sigma squared, that no method can remove; squared bias, which falls as the learner gets more flexible; and variance, which rises with it. The expected test error is U-shaped in flexibility; the training error is not. So the training error can never choose the flexibility. Section 2 reads the coefficients of the linear learners, and section 3 estimates the test error by cross-validation.

The notation. X is the vector of p predictors and Y the response, with an unknown joint distribution, and a marginal distribution for X. Psi of x is the regression function; Marques writes psi, ISLP writes f, and AME writes r. Phi is any candidate predictor, and t is its value at one point. D is the iid training data of n pairs, and psi hat, the learned function, is the learning method applied to D. Epsilon is the additive error, with mean zero and variance sigma squared, independent of X and of D. X and Y written without an index are always a new observation, independent of D. The EQM of psi hat at x, erro quadrático médio, is the mean squared error of the estimator at x, and Viés is bias. Graus de liberdade, degrees of freedom, measure a smoother's flexibility; d is the polynomial degree, and a degree-d polynomial has d plus one degrees of freedom.

One point one. Intuition: supervised learning, and the two things one wants from it.

The set-up, slides 2 to 5. One observes predictors X and a response Y; in ISLP's Advertising data, TV, radio and newspaper budgets and the sales of 200 markets. It is regression if Y is quantitative and classification if it is a label, sections 4 and 5. With no Y at all, as in clustering or principal components, the problem is unsupervised and out of this course's scope.

Two purposes, usually in tension, slides 10 to 12, ISLP section 2.1.1, printed pages 17 to 20. Compare them in four dimensions. How to treat psi hat: for prediction, as a black box; for inference, as an object to read. What success means: for prediction, a small expected squared error on new data; for inference, knowing which predictors matter and how. The typical question: for prediction, "what sales will this market have?"; for inference, "is TV associated with sales? what does 25 per cent more newspaper do?". What each favours: prediction favours flexible methods, such as trees, boosting and support vector machines; inference favours rigid, interpretable ones, such as least squares, subset selection and the lasso.

Flexibility buys accuracy and costs interpretability, slide 12, after ISLP Figure 2.7. This section is about the prediction side; section 2 is the inference side of the same linear learners.

One point two. The regression function minimises the expected prediction error.

The problem, slide 6. Find the function phi that minimises the expected prediction error, also called the expected test error or just the test error. Equation 1: the expected value of Y minus phi of X, squared, which is the integral of y minus phi of x, squared, against the joint distribution.

Restating the strategy: condition on X, then solve a one-variable problem at each x. Three steps.

Step one, condition on X, slide 7. By iterated expectations, equation 2: the expected squared error equals the expected value of the conditional expected squared error given X, which is the integral over x of the expected value of Y minus phi of x, squared, given X equal to x, against the distribution of X. Every integrand in equation 2 is non-negative and involves phi only through the single number phi of x, so equation 2 is minimised by minimising the integrand separately at each x.

Step two, one variable at a time, slide 8. Fix x and write t for phi of x. Expand the square inside the conditional expectation. Equation 3: g of t, the expected value of Y minus t squared given X equal to x, equals the conditional expected value of Y squared, minus 2 t times the conditional expected value of Y, plus t squared.

Step three, first- and second-order conditions. Differentiate equation 3 in t. Equation 4: g prime of t equals minus 2 times the conditional mean of Y plus 2 t. Equation 5: g prime of t is zero if and only if t equals the conditional mean of Y given X equal to x, and g double prime of t is 2, which is positive.

So the minimiser is unique at every x, and putting the pointwise solutions back into equation 2 gives equation 6, boxed: the function that minimises the expected squared prediction error is psi of x, the expected value of Y given X equal to x.

The same result as a decomposition, AME Teorema 1, printed page 10. For any fixed function g, add and subtract psi of X. Equation 7: the expected value of Y minus g of X, squared, equals the expected value of psi of X minus g of X, squared, plus the expected value of Y minus psi of X, squared, plus two times the expected value of the product of psi of X minus g of X and Y minus psi of X.

The cross term vanishes by conditioning on X, because psi of X minus g of X is then a constant. Equation 8: the expected product equals the expected value of psi of X minus g of X, times the conditional mean of Y minus psi of X, which is times zero, which is zero. And the middle term is the expected value of the conditional variance of Y given X. Substitute equation 8 into equation 7. Equation 9: the prediction risk of g equals the regression risk of g, the expected value of psi of X minus g of X squared, plus the expected conditional variance of Y given X.

Reading equation 9. The second term does not depend on g. So predicting Y well and estimating the regression function well are the same problem under squared loss: the reason the whole course of regression methods is also a course in prediction. With absolute loss the answer would be the conditional median instead, slide 6, remark 2, and AME Observação 1.2.

The catch, slide 9. Psi depends on the unknown joint distribution. Learning means building psi hat from the training data; each method, least squares, a spline, a tree, is one such rule.

One point three. The additive model: reducible and irreducible error.

The model, slides 13 and 14, ISLP equation 2.1, printed page 15. Equation 10: Y equals psi of X plus epsilon, and for the training data, Y i equals psi of X i plus epsilon i, for i from 1 to n. The new X and the training X's are iid; the new and training errors are iid with mean zero and variance sigma squared; and the X's and the errors are all mutually independent. Then the conditional mean of Y given X equal to x is psi of x plus the mean of epsilon, which is psi of x, so psi in equation 10 is the regression function of section 1.2. The prediction for the new point is Y hat equal to psi hat of X.

Conditioning on the new X, slide 15, exactly as in equation 2. Equation 11: the expected value of Y minus psi hat of X, squared, is the integral over x of the conditional expected value of Y minus psi hat of x, squared, given X equal to x. The expectation now runs over both the new epsilon and the training sample inside psi hat. AME Observação 1.3, printed page 11, calls this the expected risk, as opposed to the conditional risk of one fixed psi hat.

The proof of the split, slide 16. Four steps.

First, substitute equation 10 for Y at X equal to x. Equation 12: the conditional expected squared error equals the expected value of psi of x plus epsilon minus psi hat of x, squared.

Second, write the bracket as minus the quantity psi hat of x minus psi of x, plus epsilon, and expand. Equation 13: the expected value of psi hat of x minus psi of x, squared; minus 2 times the expected value of psi hat of x minus psi of x, times epsilon; plus the expected value of epsilon squared.

Third, psi hat is built from D alone and the new epsilon is independent of D, so the expected product factorises. Equation 14: the expected product equals the expected value of psi hat of x minus psi of x, times the mean of epsilon, which is times zero, which is zero. The slide writes the cross term with a plus 2; the expansion gives minus 2. It is zero either way.

Fourth, the expected value of epsilon squared is its variance plus zero squared, which is sigma squared. Substitute equation 14 into equation 13. Equation 15, boxed: the conditional expected squared error at x equals the EQM of psi hat at x, the reducible part, plus sigma squared, the irreducible part.

Integrate equation 15 through equation 11. Equation 16: the expected squared prediction error equals the integral of the EQM of psi hat at x against the distribution of X, plus sigma squared.

The choice of method moves the EQM; nothing moves sigma squared, slide 16. ISLP equation 2.3, printed page 18, is equation 15 with psi hat and X held fixed, so its reducible part is the plain square, psi of X minus psi hat of X, squared. Sigma squared is a floor on the test error that is almost always unknown in practice, ISLP page 18: it is the variation in Y due to everything not in X.

One point four. The reducible part splits into squared bias plus variance.

The definitions, slide 17, at a fixed x, with expectations over training samples. Equation 17: the bias of psi hat at x is the expected value of psi hat of x minus psi of x; and the variance of psi hat at x is the expected value of psi hat of x minus its expectation, squared.

The proof, slide 18: the same add-and-subtract as Class 1's mean squared error equals variance plus squared bias, now pointwise in x. Insert the expected value of psi hat of x. Equation 18: the EQM equals the expected value of the square of two pieces, psi hat of x minus its expectation, minus psi of x minus the expectation of psi hat of x. Equation 19, expanding: the expected value of psi hat minus its expectation, squared; minus 2 times the expected value of psi hat minus its expectation, times psi of x minus the expectation of psi hat; plus psi of x minus the expectation of psi hat, squared. The middle factor is a constant times the expectation minus the expectation, which is zero. So equation 20: the EQM of psi hat at x equals its variance plus its squared bias.

Substitute equation 20 into equation 16 to get the full factorisation. Equation 21, boxed: the expected squared prediction error equals the integral of the squared bias of psi hat at x, plus the integral of the variance of psi hat at x, both against the distribution of X, plus sigma squared. That is expected squared bias, expected variance, and irreducible error. ISLP equation 2.7, printed page 32, is the pointwise version of equation 21 at a point x zero, stated there without proof. AME section 1.5.2, page 22, writes it with the conditional variance of Y given X in place of sigma squared, which also covers heteroskedastic noise.

Where the randomness lives, the point the bias variance script asks the reader to check, term by term. Sigma squared is random over the new epsilon; it is driven by the world, and it is untouchable. The squared bias is random over nothing, a number for each x; it measures how far the average learned curve is from psi, and a rigid learner cannot bend to psi. The variance is random over the training sample; it measures how much psi hat moves when the sample is redrawn, and a flexible learner chases its sample.

"Variance" here is the variance of the learned function across training samples, not of Y. A high-variance method: change 10 per cent of the sample and psi hat changes a lot, slide 19. High bias: the method can only learn a restricted class of functions, like a straight line fitted to a strongly non-linear psi.

One point four point one. Worked: a high-bias, low-variance learner, quantified, from the script bias variance.

The script draws 1000 points from y equal to one minus x plus 2 x squared plus epsilon, with x uniform on zero to one and sigma equal to 0.1, and refits a straight line to 90 per cent subsamples, asking: "Can you even see the fitted regression line changing?" The two terms of equation 21 can be computed exactly.

The bias. As n grows, the least squares line converges to the population projection of psi on one and x. For x uniform on zero to one, the projection of x squared is x minus one sixth, with residual x squared minus x plus one sixth, orthogonal to one and to x. Substitute into psi. Equation 22: psi of x, one minus x plus 2 x squared, equals one minus x plus 2 times x minus one sixth, plus 2 times x squared minus x plus one sixth; which is two thirds plus x, plus 2 times x squared minus x plus one sixth. So the bias of the line is minus 2 times x squared minus x plus one sixth. And the integral from zero to one of x squared minus x plus one sixth, squared, is one over 180. Equation 23: the integrated squared bias is 4 times one over 180, which is one over 45, which is 0.0222.

The variance. Least squares with 2 coefficients on 900 points averages sigma squared times 2 over 900 over the design. Equation 24: the average variance is about 0.1 squared times 2 over 900, which is 0.0000222.

The squared bias, 0.0222, is 1000 times the variance, 0.0000222, and more than twice the noise, sigma squared equal to 0.01. That holds with the design fixed, as equation 24 assumes. If every training set draws fresh x values, the variance is about 8.6 times 10 to the minus 5, so the squared bias is only about 260 times larger; the Python note computes both. That is "high bias, low variance": the line is stable to the fourth decimal across subsamples and systematically wrong. The script's tall regression tree is the opposite corner. In the G layer, boosting attacks bias with many short trees and random forests attack variance by averaging many tall ones; they are named in the script and not developed in this course.

The slide's Monte Carlo, slide 25. For each number of degrees of freedom from 2 to n: draw M test points from the distribution of X; for each, draw N training samples of size n, fit the learner, and estimate the two terms of equation 21. Equation 27: the estimated squared bias at a test point is the square of the average of the N fitted values minus psi at that point; and the estimated variance is the variance of the N fitted values. Then average each over the M points. Slide 26 shows the result: the squared bias falls to near zero by about 7 degrees of freedom, the variance climbs steadily, and the prediction error bottoms out near 20, above the dotted line at 16. In the slide's words, "aproximadamente 7 graus de liberdade", approximately 7 degrees of freedom, slide 27.

One point six. Why the training error cannot pick the flexibility.

The training mean squared error, slide 28, ISLP equation 2.5, printed page 28, AME equation 1.1, printed page 14. Equation 29: the training EQM is one over n times the sum over the training points of y i minus psi hat of x i, squared.

Slides 29 and 30: as the degrees of freedom grow, equation 29 falls monotonically and hits zero at 30 degrees of freedom, where the spline interpolates. Choosing the degrees of freedom by equation 29 always picks the most overfitted model. ISLP's test mean squared error, equation 2.6, printed page 29, is the average of y zero minus psi hat of x zero, squared, over previously unseen pairs: the quantity equation 21 describes.

How optimistic is it? Exactly, for least squares. This is the G layer, proved because it explains the AIC and C p penalties of Class 1 section 6. Four steps.

First. With hat matrix H equal to B times B transposed B inverse times B transposed, the residual vector is equation 30: e equals I minus H times y, which is I minus H times psi plus I minus H times epsilon.

Second. I minus H is symmetric and idempotent with trace n minus d plus one, and the zero mean of epsilon kills the cross term. Equation 31: the expected squared length of e is the squared length of I minus H times psi, plus the expected value of epsilon transposed times I minus H times epsilon, which is the squared length of I minus H times psi plus sigma squared times n minus d minus one.

Third. Divide by n. Equation 32: the expected training EQM is the squared length of I minus H times psi over n, plus sigma squared times one minus d plus one over n.

Fourth. For a new response at the same design points, the error is the squared length of I minus H times psi over n, plus sigma squared times d plus one over n, plus sigma squared: squared bias plus variance plus noise, averaged over the design points. Subtract equation 32. Equation 33: the expected in-sample test error minus the expected training EQM equals two sigma squared times d plus one, over n.

The training error understates the error on new responses by two sigma squared times d plus one over n, growing with every parameter. Adding exactly that penalty back is Mallows' C p or AIC; BIC uses log n in place of 2, AME section 1.5.1.1, printed page 21. At degree 5, n equal to 30 and sigma squared equal to 16: 2 times 16 times 6 over 30, which is 6.4. The other route, estimating the test error directly on held-out data, is cross-validation, section 3.

Part one, section 2. Reading a linear regression: levels, logs, elasticities and interactions.

This section covers the multiple linear regression deck, slides 17, 29 and 44; the scripts capm regression and salaries for professors; the page on the return to education with the Mroz data; Wooldridge section 2.4, printed pages 36 to 40, with equations 2.39 to 2.46, Examples 2.10 and 2.11, and Table 2.3; Wooldridge section 6.2, printed pages 171 to 178, with equations 6.6 to 6.13 and 6.17 and Example 6.2; and ISLP section 3.3.1, printed page 91, for the Credit units.

The claim. "Linear" regression is linear in the parameters, not in the variables, so logs, squares and products of the regressors are all fitted by the same least squares. What changes is the reading of a coefficient: a unit change, level-level; an approximate percentage, log-level, 100 beta; an elasticity, log-log, beta; or a slope that depends on another variable, with interactions and quadratics. The 100 beta reading is a first-order approximation; the exact change is 100 times e to the beta delta x, minus one, and the gap is already 5 points at beta equal to 0.306.

The notation. y is the response, x one to x k the regressors, and u the error, with conditional mean zero given x. The betas are population coefficients and the beta hats their least squares estimates. Delta is a discrete change, and the percentage change of y is 100 times delta y over y. Ceteris paribus means all other regressors and u held fixed. Log is the natural log throughout, Wooldridge's convention. s y and s x j are sample standard deviations, and a star marks a standardised variable, minus its mean, over its standard deviation. A dummy D is one for the named category and zero for the base category.

Two point one. Intuition: "linear" means linear in beta.

The model y equals beta zero plus beta one x plus u is linear because it is linear in beta zero and beta one; nothing stops y or x from being the log of wage, the square root of income, x squared, or x one times x two, Wooldridge section 2-4c, page 40. The mechanics of least squares, beta hat equal to X transposed X inverse times X transposed y, from Class 1 section 6, do not care how the columns of X were built.

The mechanics are free; the interpretation is not. Wooldridge, page 40: "it is much more important to become proficient at interpreting coefficients than to become efficient at computing formulas." The same fact is what makes polynomial regression a linear model, section 3.

Two point two. Level-level: a coefficient is a slope in the variables' own units.

For y equal to beta zero plus beta one x one up to beta k x k plus u, holding the other regressors and u fixed, equation 1: delta y equals beta j times delta x j.

Units move the coefficient, never the fit, Wooldridge section 2-4a, pages 36 to 37. If x j is replaced by c times x j, equation 1 stays true only if the slope becomes beta j over c; if y is replaced by c times y, every coefficient is multiplied by c. The R squared is unchanged. Wooldridge's own case: the CEO salary slope on return on equity is 18.501, equation 2.39, salary in thousands of dollars per percentage point; and on return on equity divided by 100 it is 1,850.1, equation 2.41, page 37. Dividing x by 100 multiplied the slope by 100.

Two point three. Interactions: the slope of one variable depends on another.

With an interaction, Wooldridge section 6-2c, pages 177 to 178, equation 6.17. Equation 6: y equals beta zero plus beta one x one plus beta two x two plus beta three x one x two plus u, which implies that delta y over delta x two equals beta two plus beta three x one.

Beta two alone is the effect of x two at x one equal to zero, often meaningless. Wooldridge's example: the effect of a bedroom on a house of zero square feet. Report equation 6 at interesting values of x one, or centre x one at its mean before forming the product, so that beta two becomes the effect at the mean, Wooldridge page 178.

When x one is a dummy, equation 6 gives two slopes. The script salaries for professors fits the full model of salary on the sex dummy, years of service, and their product, with salary in thousands of dollars and the dummy equal to one for men. Equation 7: the expected salary given sex and years equals beta zero, plus beta one times male, plus beta two times years, plus beta three times male times years. Equation 8: the slope of expected salary in years is beta two for women and beta two plus beta three for men; and the gap, the expected salary for men minus that for women, is beta one plus beta three times years.

So beta one is the gender gap at zero years of service, and beta three is how much the gap widens per year. The reduced model, salary on the dummy plus years, forces beta three to zero: parallel lines, one constant gap. The script prints no estimates; it compares the two models by a Bayes factor, Classes 3 and 4.

Two point four. Log-level: 100 beta is a semi-elasticity, and an approximation.

The model, Wooldridge equation 2.42, page 37. Equation 9: log y equals beta zero plus beta one x plus u.

Three steps.

Step one. Take two values of x with the same u, and subtract the two versions of equation 9. Equation 10: log y one minus log y zero equals beta one times x one minus x zero, which is beta one delta x.

Step two, exact. Exponentiate equation 10: y one over y zero equals e to the beta one delta x. So equation 11: the percentage change of y equals 100 times y one over y zero minus one, which is 100 times e to the beta one delta x, minus one.

Step three, approximate. For small z, e to the z is one plus z plus z squared over two and so on, approximately one plus z. Substitute z equal to beta one delta x into equation 11. Equation 12: the percentage change of y is approximately 100 times beta one times delta x. This is Wooldridge's equation 2.43; 100 beta one is the semi-elasticity of y with respect to x. Equation 11 is Wooldridge's 6.8, page 171, and with delta x equal to one it is his 6.9. The error of equation 12 is about 100 z squared over two: negligible for z up to 0.05 in absolute value, five points at z equal to 0.3.

Two point five. Log-log and level-log.

Log-log, Wooldridge equation 2.45, page 39: log y equals beta zero plus beta one log x plus u. Differencing as in equation 10, equation 13: the change in log y equals beta one times the change in log x, so beta one equals d log y over d log x, which is d y over y divided by d x over x: the elasticity of y with respect to x. Using the approximation that a change in log v is about the percentage change of v over 100 on both sides, equation 14: the percentage change of y is approximately beta one times the percentage change of x. Exactly, exponentiating equation 13, y one over y zero equals x one over x zero raised to beta one, so equation 15: the percentage change of y equals 100 times the quantity one plus the percentage change of x over 100, raised to beta one, minus one.

Worked: Wooldridge Example 2.11, page 39. The predicted log salary equals 4.822 plus 0.257 log sales, with n equal to 209 and R squared 0.211. A 1 per cent rise in sales goes with a 0.257 per cent rise in CEO salary. For a 10 per cent rise, equation 14 gives 0.257 times 10, which is 2.57 per cent, and equation 15 gives equation 16: 100 times 1.10 to the 0.257, minus one, which is 100 times 1.024797 minus one, which is 2.48 per cent.

Level-log: y equals beta zero plus beta one log x plus u gives delta y equal to beta one times the change in log x, approximately beta one over 100 times the percentage change of x.

Wooldridge Table 2.3, page 39, the summary to keep, model by model. Level-level: dependent y, independent x, delta y equals beta one delta x. Level-log: dependent y, independent log x, delta y equals beta one over 100 times the percentage change of x. Log-level: dependent log y, independent x, the percentage change of y equals 100 beta one times delta x. Log-log: dependent log y, independent log x, the percentage change of y equals beta one times the percentage change of x.

Part two, section 3. Choosing the polynomial order by cross-validation, and putting a bootstrap band on the winner.

This section covers the motorcycle deck, 11 slides, and its R Markdown page by Lopes, dated October 28th, 2025; Fundamentos slides 27 to 35; the multiple linear regression deck, slides 36 to 38; ISLP section 5.1, printed pages 202 to 209, equations 5.1 to 5.3, section 5.2, printed pages 212 to 214, equation 5.8, and section 7.1, printed pages 290 to 291, equations 7.1 and 7.2; and AME section 1.5.1, printed pages 14 to 16, with equations 1.1 and 1.2 and Observação 1.5, and section 1.5.1.1, printed page 21.

The claim. The test error of section 1 is not observable, but it can be estimated by refitting on part of the data and predicting the rest. A single hold-out split is noisy: on the site, 100 random 80 to 20 splits pick order 12 only 63 times. 10-fold cross-validation picks it 99 times out of 100. Leave-one-out cross-validation, LOOCV for short, costs a single fit for least squares, thanks to the formula: CV sub n equals one over n times the sum of the squared quantity y i minus y i hat, over one minus h i i. The adjusted R squared, BIC, LOOCV and 10-fold all land on order 12 for the motorcycle data, and a pairs bootstrap draws the uncertainty band around that fit.

The notation. The data are pairs x i and y i, for i from 1 to n. For the motorcycle data, x is the time after impact in milliseconds and y the head acceleration in g, both standardised, with n equal to 132. d is the polynomial order, the tuning parameter; the model has d plus one coefficients. X is the n by d plus one design with columns one, x, up to x to the d; beta hat is X transposed X inverse times X transposed y; y i hat is the fitted value; and e i is the residual. H, X times X transposed X inverse times X transposed, is the hat matrix, and h i i, x i transposed times X transposed X inverse times x i, is the leverage of point i. beta hat with i removed, and y hat with i removed, are the fit and prediction with point i left out. k is the number of folds, and n sub j the size of fold j. B is the number of bootstrap resamples, and f hat star b is the fit on resample b.

Three point one. Intuition: two jobs for an error estimate.

Fundamentos slide 27 names them. Model selection: pick the flexibility, here d, that best balances bias and variance. Model assessment: say how well the chosen model will predict. The training mean squared error does neither: it falls with every added term, section 1.6. Two fixes exist: penalise the training error, with the adjusted R squared, C p, AIC or BIC, from Class 1 section 6; or estimate the test error directly by predicting observations the fit has not seen, which is this section.

Three point two. Polynomial regression and the motorcycle data.

Polynomial regression is a linear model, ISLP equation 7.1, page 290. Equation 1: y i equals beta zero plus beta one x i plus beta two x i squared, up to beta d x i to the d, plus epsilon i. It is least squares on the columns x, x squared, up to x to the d. ISLP warns that d above 3 or 4 gives "very strange shapes", "especially near the boundary of the X variable", page 290. Keep that in mind when order 12 wins below.

The data, slides 2 and 3. The mcycle data from the MASS package: head acceleration in a simulated motorcycle crash, used to test helmets, Silverman 1985. The code keeps the first 132 rows, maps x to x over its maximum, then to minus one plus 2 x, then standardises; y likewise. The first two maps are wasted work: standardising a x plus b, with a positive, gives back the standardised x, because the mean shifts by b and scales by a while the standard deviation scales by a. Equation 2: a x i plus b minus the quantity a x bar plus b, over a s x, equals x i minus x bar over s x.

The plot, slide 3, is flat and tight before impact, then a deep dip, a rebound and a noisy tail: the noise variance is far from constant, which matters for section 3.7.

Three point three. In-sample criteria: the adjusted R squared and BIC. Slides 4 and 5.

Slide 4 fits orders 1, 2, 5, 8, 10, 11, 12 and 20, and the panel labels read: 0.0769, 0.1323, 0.5259, 0.6941, 0.769, 0.7686, 0.7798 at order 12, and 0.7688 at order 20. The page's code prints the adjusted R squared in that legend, and slide 5's adjusted R squared curve peaks at the same value, about 0.78, at order 12.

The page computes, with sigma hat squared equal to RSS over n minus d minus one, equation 3: the adjusted R squared at order d is one minus one minus R squared times n minus one over n minus d minus one; and BIC at order d is one over n times the quantity RSS plus log n times d plus one times sigma hat squared. That is BIC in AME's penalised-risk form, the EQM plus log n times p times sigma hat squared over n, with p equal to d plus one, AME section 1.5.1.1, page 21. Both pick order 12, the dotted lines on slide 5. Going from 12 to 20 adds eight coefficients and lowers the adjusted R squared from 0.7798 to 0.7688: the penalty at work.

A number to carry. y is standardised, so its variance is one, and the adjusted R squared equals one minus sigma hat squared over the variance of y. So at order 12, equation 4: sigma hat squared is one minus 0.7798, which is 0.2202, and sigma hat is the square root of 0.2202, which is 0.4693.

Three point four. The validation-set, or hold-out, approach.

The estimator, ISLP section 5.1.1, pages 202 to 204, AME equation 1.2, page 14. Split at random into a training part T and a validation part V; fit on T only. Equation 5: the estimated risk at order d is the average over the validation points of y i minus the training fit at x i, squared.

Since V played no part in the fit, equation 5 is an honest estimate, consistent by the law of large numbers, AME page 14. Two drawbacks, ISLP section 5.1.1 and page 208: it varies a lot with the split, and it fits on fewer points than n, so it tends to overestimate the error of the full-sample fit.

One split, slide 6, 80 per cent to 20 per cent: the root mean squared error curve falls from about 0.92 at orders up to 4 to its minimum at order 12.

One hundred splits, slide 7, with the variability made visible. The times each order wins, out of 100: orders 1 to 5, zero; order 6, once; order 7, zero; order 8, twice; order 9, twice; order 10, 15 times; order 11, 7 times; order 12, 63 times; order 13, 10 times; order 14, zero. The total is 1 plus 2 plus 2 plus 15 plus 7 plus 63 plus 10, which is 100. It checks. Order 12 wins 63 per cent of the time; orders 10 to 13 together, 15 plus 7 plus 63 plus 10, win 95 per cent. The same experiment drives the best-subset size in the houseprice data, in the multiple linear regression deck, slides 36 to 38: 1,000 random splits with 50, 60, 70 and 80 per cent for training, and the winners pile up at sizes 5 to 7.

A single split answers "which d?" with a random variable. Repeating the split and reporting the distribution of winners is honest; reporting one split is not.

Three point five. Leave-one-out cross-validation, and the one-fit shortcut.

The definition, Fundamentos slide 31, ISLP equation 5.1, page 204, AME page 15. For each i, fit on the other n minus one points and predict y i. Equation 6: CV sub n equals one over n times the sum of y i minus the left-out prediction of y i, squared.

There is no randomness, and each fit uses n minus one points, so there is little bias, ISLP page 205. AME Observação 1.5, pages 15 to 16: LOOCV on n plus one points is exactly unbiased for the expected risk of the method fitted on n.

The shortcut, ISLP equation 5.2, page 205, stated there without proof. For least squares, equation 7, boxed: CV sub n equals one over n times the sum of the square of y i minus y i hat, over one minus h i i. One fit instead of n.

The derivation. Restating the goal: express the left-out residual in terms of the full-sample residual. Write A for X transposed X and u for x i, so h i i is u transposed A inverse u. Four steps.

Step one, drop row i. Removing one row from X and y, equation 8: X with row i removed, transposed, times itself, equals A minus u u transposed; and X with row i removed, transposed, times y with entry i removed, equals X transposed y minus u times y i.

Step two, Sherman-Morrison, valid when one minus u transposed A inverse u is not zero. Equation 9: the inverse of A minus u u transposed equals A inverse plus A inverse u u transposed A inverse over one minus h i i.

Step three, substitute equations 8 and 9 into the left-out estimate. Equation 10: beta hat with i removed equals the quantity A inverse plus A inverse u u transposed A inverse over one minus h i i, times the quantity X transposed y minus u y i. Multiply out, using A inverse X transposed y equal to beta hat, u transposed beta hat equal to y i hat, and u transposed A inverse u equal to h i i. Equation 11: beta hat with i removed equals beta hat, minus A inverse u y i, plus A inverse u y i hat over one minus h i i, minus A inverse u h i i y i over one minus h i i. Put the last three terms over one minus h i i. Equation 12: beta hat plus A inverse u times the bracket minus y i times one minus h i i, plus y i hat, minus h i i y i, all over one minus h i i; the bracket simplifies to minus y i minus y i hat; so beta hat with i removed equals beta hat minus A inverse x i times e i, over one minus h i i.

Step four, the left-out residual. Premultiply equation 12 by x i transposed. Equation 13: y i minus the left-out prediction equals y i minus y i hat plus h i i e i over one minus h i i, which is e i times one plus h i i over one minus h i i, which is e i over one minus h i i. Substitute equation 13 into equation 6 and you have equation 7. That completes the proof.

Reading equation 13. The left-out residual is the ordinary residual inflated by one over one minus h i i, "exactly the right amount", ISLP page 205. Since the leverages sum to the trace of H, which is d plus one, the average leverage is d plus one over n: at order 12 with n equal to 132, it is 13 over 132, which is 0.0985. But leverages are largest at the ends of the x range, which is where a degree-12 polynomial is least trustworthy. Equation 7 holds for least squares and some linear smoothers, not in general: logistic regression or linear discriminant analysis must be refitted n times, ISLP page 205.

On the site, slide 8, the LOOCV curve's minimum is at order 12, about 0.48 on the slide's "Root MSE" axis.

The variance of CV sub n, Fundamentos slide 35. With EQM i the squared left-out error of point i, equation 14: the variance of CV sub n equals one over n squared times the sum of the variances of the EQM i, plus two over n squared times the sum over pairs i less than j of their covariances. The n fits share n minus 2 of their n minus 1 points, so the covariances are large and positive: LOOCV is nearly unbiased but high-variance, ISLP section 5.1.4, pages 208 to 209.

Three point six. k-fold cross-validation.

The definition, Fundamentos slide 33, ISLP equation 5.3, page 206. Split at random into k folds of about equal size; fit without fold j, compute the mean squared error on fold j; average. Equation 15: CV sub k equals one over k times the sum of the fold errors, where the error of fold j is one over n sub j times the sum over the fold of y i minus the fit without fold j at x i, squared. With k equal to n it is LOOCV, slide 35. AME, page 15, and the site's code use the observation-weighted version. Equation 16: one over n times the double sum of the squared left-out errors, which equals the sum over folds of n sub j over n times the fold error. It equals equation 15 only when all folds have exactly n over k points.

The site's folds, worked. The code sets L equal to the integer part of 132 over 10, which is 13. Folds 1 to 9 are 13 consecutive shuffled positions each, 9 times 13 is 117, and fold 10 takes the rest, 132 minus 117, which is 15. So the weights are 13 over 132 or 15 over 132, not one tenth, and equations 15 and 16 differ slightly.

Why k equal to 5 or 10, ISLP section 5.1.4 and Fundamentos slide 35. Compare three methods in four dimensions. The 80 to 20 hold-out fits on about 105 points, 0.8 times 132 being 105.6; the bias of its error estimate is the highest; its variance is high, from one split; and it costs one fit. 10-fold fits on about 119 points, 0.9 times 132 being 118.8; its bias is small; its variance moderate; and it costs 10 fits. LOOCV fits on 131 points; its bias is the smallest; its variance the highest, because of correlated fits; and it costs n fits, or one with equation 7.

On the site, one 10-fold run, slide 9, has its minimum at order 12; repeating the random fold assignment 100 times, slide 10, order 12 wins 99 times and order 13 once. Compare 63 for the hold-out: every observation is predicted once in every run, so the only randomness left is the fold assignment.

The axis label is not the quantity, but the chosen order is safe. In the page's code, the leave-one-out loop accumulates the sum of squared errors and the 10-fold loop divides it by n, giving a mean squared error, and both are plotted under "Root MSE". On the slides the curves sit near 0.48 at order 12. By equation 4, a mean squared error there cannot be below about sigma hat squared, 0.22, while the square root of 0.22 is 0.47, so the slides show root mean squared error. The sum, the mean and the root of squared errors are increasing transforms of one another: the chosen d is the same; only the vertical scale differs.

Three point seven. Bootstrap bands for the chosen fit.

The pairs bootstrap, slide 11, the page's section 8, ISLP section 5.2, pages 212 to 214. With the order fixed at 12, for each of B equal to 1000 resamples: draw n indices with replacement, refit equation 1 on those pairs, and keep the fitted curve. Slide 11 draws all 1,000 curves in grey under the red order-12 fit. A pointwise 95 per cent percentile band keeps, at each x, the 2.5 and 97.5 per cent quantiles of the bootstrap curves. The bootstrap standard error at x is ISLP's equation 5.8, page 214. Equation 17: the square root of one over B minus one times the sum over resamples of the squared distance between each resampled curve at x and their average at x.

The formula alternative, ISLP equation 7.2 and footnote 1, page 291. With l zero the vector one, x zero, up to x zero to the d, and C hat equal to sigma hat squared times X transposed X inverse, equation 18: the estimated variance of the fit at x zero is l zero transposed C hat l zero, and the band is the fit plus or minus two times the square root of that.

Equation 18 assumes one error variance for all x. Resampling pairs does not: each resample carries the quiet pre-impact points and the noisy tail in their own proportions. With the motorcycle data's heteroskedasticity, the two bands disagree, the bootstrap one being wider where the noise is large.

What the band does not cover: the order was chosen on the same data and is held at 12 in every resample. Model-selection uncertainty is outside it. In the G layer, re-running the cross-validation inside each resample would put it in.

Three point nine. Graduate layer: named, not developed here.

The one-standard-error rule: pick the simplest model whose cross-validation error is within one standard error of the minimum, ISLP chapter 6. On the stand-in, orders 10 and 12 differ by less than 0.01 in cross-validation error, so the rule could well prefer the simpler one.

Selection bias of the minimum: the smallest of 14 cross-validation estimates is optimistic for the chosen model; an untouched test set, AME's training, validation and test split, page 16, or nested cross-validation fixes it.

Cross-validation for classification, ISLP equation 5.4: the error indicator replaces the squared error. Sections 4 and 5.

Recap of part 1. Under squared loss the best predictor is the conditional mean, and any learned estimate pays squared bias, variance and noise; in the tanh world the optimum is degree 5 with test error 19.81 against a floor of 16, and the training error is optimistic by two sigma squared times d plus one over n, 6.4 there. Linear regression is linear in the parameters; read level coefficients as slopes, log-level coefficients exactly as 100 times e to the beta minus one, 35.8 per cent at 0.306, log-log coefficients as elasticities, and interactions and quadratics as slopes that move. Cross-validation estimates the test error: one hold-out split picks the motorcycle order 12 only 63 times in 100, 10-fold 99 times, and LOOCV costs one fit through the leverages.
