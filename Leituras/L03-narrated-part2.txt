Part three of the notes, section 6. Monte Carlo: replace an integral by an average of draws, and know its error.

This section covers the ingredients deck, slides 53 to 56, the Monte Carlo toy example that estimates pi; Greenberg chapter 5, printed pages 63 to 75: section 5.1 with equation 5.1, section 5.2, section 5.3 with Algorithms 5.2 to 5.4 and equation 5.2, section 5.4 with Algorithm 5.5, section 5.5 and section 5.6; Greenberg section 4.5, printed page 52; Morettin and Singer appendix B, section B.2, printed pages 456 to 457, and sections B.6.1 and B.6.2, printed pages 467 to 471, equations B.15 to B.17; and three site pages: the four physicists, for sampling importance resampling, the correlation coefficient, and zero-inflated Poisson fishing.

The claim, before the derivations. Every Bayesian summary is an integral: a posterior mean, a probability, a predictive, a marginal likelihood. Monte Carlo writes it as an expectation, draws M independent values and averages. The error falls like one over the square root of M and is measured by the numerical standard error. Hit-or-miss gives an estimate of pi with standard error 0.052 at M equal to 1000, so the deck's 3.196 is one error away from pi. When direct draws are impossible there are four tools: the inverse distribution function for univariate targets; composition for mixtures, since a Student t is a normal with a gamma precision; accept-reject with acceptance probability one over c, which for a Beta of 3 and 3 from uniforms means c equal to 1.875 and 53 per cent acceptance; and importance sampling and sampling importance resampling, which reweight draws from another density. Importance sampling fails silently when the proposal's tails are too thin: Greenberg's own Beta of 2 and 3 example has infinite variance.

The notation. f is the target density, often a posterior known up to a constant. g or h is the proposal, or importance, density we can sample. The number of draws is M in the deck, capital G in Greenberg and n in Morettin and Singer. g bar M is the Monte Carlo average of g of X i. The numerical standard error is the square root of the estimated variance of g of X over M. c is the accept-reject constant, with f at most c times g; it is at least one for normalised f and g. And w i is the importance weight, f of x i over g of x i, non-negative.

Six point one. Integrals that cannot be done by hand. The deck's slides 42 to 43 and 48 to 52. This is the U layer.

The physicist C posterior of section 3 needs the integral of theta times p of x given theta times p of theta with a Student t prior: no closed form. The deck, slide 43: "Bayesian computation was done on limited, unrealistic models until the Monte Carlo revolution, and the computing revolution, of the late 1980's and early 1990's". The idea is the law of large numbers: if we can draw from a distribution, sample averages approximate its expectations.

Six point two. Monte Carlo integration and its error. Slides 53 to 56; Greenberg printed page 63 and section 5.6; Morettin and Singer section B.2. This is the C layer.

For X distributed f and a function g whose absolute value has a finite expectation, equation 1: the expected value of g of X, the integral of g of x times f of x, is approximately g bar M, one over M times the sum from i equal to 1 to M of g of X i, where the X i are independent draws from f.

g bar M is unbiased, converges by the law of large numbers, and has variance equal to the variance of g of X over M. Greenberg's numerical standard error, printed page 73, is equation 2: the square root of the estimated variance of g of X, divided by M.

Probabilities are the case where g is the indicator of an event A: the probability of A is approximately the fraction of draws in A, Greenberg printed page 63.

Worked: hit-or-miss pi, slides 53 to 56. Draw pairs x i and y i uniform on the unit square. The probability that x squared plus y squared is below one is pi over four, the area of a quarter disc, so the estimate of pi is 4 times the fraction of points inside. The indicator is Bernoulli with p equal to pi over 4, which is 0.7854. By equation 2, equation 3: the standard error of the estimate of pi is 4 times the square root of p times one minus p over M, which is 4 times the square root of 0.7854 times 0.2146 over M, which is 1.6422 over the square root of M.

At M equal to 1000: 1.6422 over 31.623, which is 0.0519. The deck's estimate, 3.196 on slide 54, is 3.196 minus 3.1416, over 0.0519, which is 1.05 standard errors high: an ordinary draw. At M equal to 10,000 the standard error is 0.0164; a seeded Python run gives 3.1452.

The accept-reject figure has two panels; both are seeded. The left panel belongs to subsection 5. The right panel shows six running estimates of pi against thousands of draws, slides 55 and 56, all inside a funnel of pi plus or minus two standard errors from equation 3, which narrows from about plus or minus 0.1 at 1000 draws to 0.033 at 10,000.

The right panel is slide 56 with its envelope drawn. Every path wanders, none escapes the plus or minus two over the square root of M funnel for long, and the funnel narrows slowly: four times the draws for half the error.

Six point three. The inverse distribution function method, also called the probability integral transform. Greenberg section 5.1; Morettin and Singer section B.4.

If U is uniform on zero to one and F is a continuous distribution function, then Z equal to the inverse of F at U has distribution function F. Equation 4: the probability that Z is at most z equals the probability that F inverse of U is at most z, which equals the probability that U is at most F of z, which is F of z.

An example, Greenberg printed pages 64 to 65. The density three eighths y squared on zero to two has distribution function y cubed over 8, so Y equals 2 times U to the one third.

Truncation, Greenberg equation 5.1. To draw X from F restricted to the interval from c one to c two, set X equal to F inverse of: F of c one plus U times the difference F of c two minus F of c one. This is how the truncated normals of the Tobit and probit samplers are drawn, Greenberg section 8.2. The method needs the full distribution function and is univariate only.

Six point four. Composition and scale mixtures. Greenberg sections 5.2 and 4.5.

If f of x is the integral of g of x given y times h of y, draw y from h, then x from g given y; the x's are draws from f.

The Student t as a normal scale mixture, Greenberg printed page 52. Let u given lambda be normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared over lambda, and let lambda be Gamma with parameters nu over two and nu over two. Equation 5: the joint density is proportional to lambda to the one half, times e to the minus lambda u squared over two sigma squared, times lambda to the nu over two minus one, times e to the minus nu lambda over two. Collected, that is lambda to the power nu plus one over two, minus one, times the exponential of minus lambda times u squared plus nu sigma squared, over two sigma squared.

Equation 5 is a Gamma kernel in lambda, with shape nu plus one over two and rate u squared plus nu sigma squared over two sigma squared. Integrating lambda out leaves the reciprocal of the rate raised to the shape. Equation 6: f of u is proportional to u squared plus nu sigma squared over two sigma squared, raised to the minus nu plus one over two; which is proportional to one plus u squared over nu sigma squared, raised to the minus nu plus one over two. That is the kernel of the t with nu degrees of freedom, location zero and scale squared sigma squared.

So a t draw is a gamma draw followed by a normal draw. A seeded check with nu equal to 5 and 400,000 draws: variance 1.660, against the theoretical five thirds; and the probability that the absolute value of u exceeds 2.571 is 0.0502, against 0.05. This representation is why Student t errors and priors fit inside Gibbs samplers, section 7 and Greenberg section 8.1.2, and why the horseshoe is a normal with a random scale, section 10.

Six point five. Accept-reject. Greenberg section 5.3; Morettin and Singer section B.6.1.

Algorithm 5.2. Find a density g we can sample and a constant c with f of y at most c times g of y for all y. Step one: draw y from g. Step two: draw u uniform on zero to one. Step three: accept y if u is at most f of y over c times g of y; otherwise return to step one.

Why it works, Greenberg printed page 67. By Bayes' rule, the density of an accepted value is equation 7. h of y given acceptance equals the probability of acceptance given y times g of y, over the integral of the same. That is f of y over c g of y, times g of y, over the integral of the same; which is f of y over c, divided by one over c; which is f of y.

The acceptance probability is the denominator, one over c. Choose c as small as possible. If f is known only up to a constant, f equal to k times r with r at most c times g, then accepting with probability r over c g still yields draws from f; then c need not exceed one.

Worked: a Beta of 3 and 3 from uniforms, Algorithm 5.3. The density f of y equals 30 y squared times one minus y squared, and it peaks at y equal to one half. So c is 30 times one sixteenth, which is 1.875, and the acceptance rate is one over 1.875, which is 0.5333. A proposal y equal to 0.15 has f of 0.15 equal to 30 times 0.0225 times 0.7225, which is 0.4877, and is accepted with probability 0.4877 over 1.875, which is 0.2601. A seeded run of 200,000 gives acceptance 0.5339; the accepted draws have mean 0.500 and variance 0.0357, which is one twenty-eighth.

The left panel of the accept-reject figure shows exactly this: the Beta of 3 and 3 density under the flat line c g of y equal to 1.875, with 600 seeded proposals plotted as points at height u times 1.875. Points under the density are accepted, 52 per cent of them, and points above it are rejected.

Worked: a standard normal from a Laplace proposal, Algorithm 5.4. By symmetry, sample the absolute value of y from the half-normal, f of x equal to the square root of two over pi times e to the minus x squared over two, with proposal g of x equal to e to the minus x for positive x, and attach a random sign. The ratio f over g, the square root of two over pi times e to the x minus x squared over two, is maximised at x equal to one. Equation 8: c is the square root of two over pi times e to the one half, which is the square root of two e over pi, which is 1.3155; and the acceptance rate one over c is 0.7602.

Greenberg's test, equation 5.2, accepts if u is at most e to the power x minus x squared over two minus one half. That is exactly u at most f of x over c g of x, with equation 8. His text says "the probability of acceptance is one over c, 0.6577". But 0.6577, the square root of e over two pi, is the constant that dominates the unnormalised kernel, one over the square root of two pi times e to the minus x squared over two, whose integral over positive x is one half. So the acceptance rate is one half over 0.6577, which is 0.7602, either way. A seeded run of 200,000 accepts 0.7605.

Six point six. Importance sampling. Greenberg section 5.4; Morettin and Singer section B.6.2.

Multiply and divide by a density h we can sample. Equation 9: the expected value under f of g of X is the integral of g of x times f of x over h of x, times h of x; and that is approximately one over G times the sum over i of g of X i times w i, where w i is f of X i over h of X i and the X i are drawn from h.

When f is known only up to a constant, use the self-normalised form, Morettin and Singer equation B.17. Equation 10: theta tilde equals the sum over i of w i times g of X i, divided by the sum of the w i. It is consistent if the support of h covers that of f, Geweke 1989, as cited by Morettin and Singer.

The variance condition, Greenberg printed page 70. The estimator's second moment is the expected value under h of g f over h, squared, which is the integral of g squared f squared over h. It is large "when the tail values of h are very small compared to the tail values of f".

Worked: Greenberg's Algorithm 5.5, and its hidden flaw. The target is the expected value of one over one plus x squared, for x exponential with rate one truncated to zero to one, so f of x is e to the minus x over one minus e to the minus one. The importance density h is the Beta of 2 and 3, h of x equal to 12 x times one minus x squared. The exact value is 0.8302, Greenberg printed page 71; quadrature gives 0.830217. Greenberg reports 0.8268 with numerical standard error 0.0030 from G equal to 10,000. A seeded Python run gives 0.8003 with a computed numerical standard error of 0.0089: three "standard errors" away.

The reason is near x equal to zero. There f of zero times g of zero is 1.582, while h of x is approximately 12 x. So equation 11: the integral from zero to epsilon of g squared f squared over h is approximately the integral from zero to epsilon of 1.582 squared over 12 x, which is infinite.

The estimator has infinite variance. It is still consistent, but its sample standard error means nothing and convergence is erratic. Numerically, each factor of 100 closer to zero adds 1.582 squared times the log of 100 over 12, which is 0.960, to the truncated second moment. A proposal with a positive density at zero, such as the uniform on zero to one or the truncated exponential itself, removes the problem.

Six point seven. Sampling importance resampling, SIR. Morettin and Singer section B.6.2; the physicists, correlation and fishing pages.

Three steps. First, draw x one to x n from g. Second, compute normalised weights: w i equals f of x i over g of x i, divided by the sum over j of f of x j over g of x j. Third, resample from the x i with probabilities w i. The resampled values are approximately draws from f. Morettin and Singer's argument, printed page 470: the resampled distribution function, the sum of the w i over the x i at most a, converges to the integral of f up to a, by the strong law.

With a uniform g on a box, w i is proportional to likelihood times prior: the lecturer's pages use exactly this.

Worked: physicist C, from the site page on the four physicists. Ten to the sixth proposals from the uniform on 600 to 1000; weights equal to the normal density of 850 given theta with standard deviation 40, times the t density with 5 degrees of freedom at theta, location 900 and scale squared 240; and ten to the sixth resampled draws. Seeded Python gives a mean of 891.13 against the quadrature value 891.12. The effective sample size, the square of the sum of the weights over the sum of the squared weights, is 144,275: only one draw in seven carries real weight, because most uniform proposals land where the posterior is negligible.

Draws theta one to theta G from a joint posterior give, column by column, draws from each marginal without computing any integral, Greenberg printed page 74. Six point nine. Multivariate draws. Greenberg section 5.5. This is the G layer, named only.

X equal to mu plus C transposed Z, with Z a p-variate standard normal and sigma equal to C transposed C, the Cholesky factorisation, gives X p-variate normal with mean mu and covariance sigma. Section 7. Gibbs sampling: a Markov chain whose invariant distribution is the posterior.

This section covers Greenberg chapter 6, printed pages 76 to 87, equations 6.1 to 6.9 and theorems 6.1 to 6.7; Greenberg section 7.1, printed pages 91 to 95, with Algorithms 7.1 to 7.3, equations 7.1 to 7.4 and the bivariate-normal example; Greenberg section 7.3, printed pages 102 to 103, on batch means and the relative numerical efficiency; Tsay sections 12.1 to 12.3, printed pages 614 to 621, equations 12.1 to 12.3 and results 12.1 to 12.8; Morettin and Singer section C.5, printed pages 487 to 488, equations C.16 and C.17; the coal-mining data of Jarrett, 1979, kept in the project's agent notes; and the Gibbs code of the hierarchical Gaussian linear model page, used in section 9.

The claim, before the derivations. When a posterior cannot be sampled directly but each full conditional can, draw the blocks one at a time, each from its conditional given the latest values of the others. The draws form a Markov chain whose invariant distribution is the joint posterior; after a burn-in, their histograms are the marginals. On the coal-mining series, with Class 1's prior on both rates, a three-block Gibbs sampler puts the switch at 1891, with posterior probability 0.240 against the exact 0.240, and with posterior means of lambda one equal to 3.106 and of lambda two equal to 0.930. The price is dependence. On a bivariate normal with correlation rho, the chain's lag-h autocorrelation is rho to the two h, so rho equal to 0.999 needs about 1000 draws for the information of one independent draw.

The notation. Capital P, with entries p i j, is the transition matrix: p i j is the probability that X at t plus one equals j given that X at t equals i; its rows sum to one. Pi is the invariant distribution, satisfying pi times P equals pi; it is a probability vector or a density. p of x and y is the transition kernel for a continuous state, a density in y. Theta, with components theta one to theta d, are the parameter blocks, and theta minus j denotes all blocks except theta j. Capital B and capital G are the burn-in length and the number of retained draws. k is the switch point, the last year of the first regime, from 1 to n. Lambda one and lambda two are the Poisson rates before and after the switch. S k, the sum of the y i for i up to k, is the cumulative count. And rho h is the lag-h autocorrelation of a chain, between minus one and one.

Seven point one. Independent draws are a luxury. This is the U layer.

Chapter 5's methods, section 6, need a density we can sample whose shape is close to the target, and that is hard to find in many dimensions. Markov chain Monte Carlo reverses the question. Instead of a distribution that is easy to sample, build a process that is easy to run and that settles into the target. The draws are no longer independent, but averages still converge, Theorem 6.3, and the recipe works for "a great variety of distributions without having to undertake an intensive analysis of the special features of the distribution", Greenberg printed page 76.

Seven point two. Markov chains on a finite state space. Greenberg section 6.1. This is the C layer.

A process X t on the states 1 to s is a homogeneous Markov chain if, equation 1, p i j, the probability that X at t plus one equals j given that X at t equals i, does not depend on t, nor on X at t minus one, t minus two, and earlier.

The n-step transition probabilities are the entries of P to the n, Greenberg equation 6.3. A distribution pi is invariant if, equation 2, pi j equals the sum over i of pi i times p i j, for all j; that is, pi times P equals pi.

Worked, Greenberg equation 6.2, printed page 79. The transition matrix has first row 0.750 and 0.250, and second row 0.125 and 0.875. The first column of equation 2 gives pi one equal to 0.750 pi one plus 0.125 pi two. Substitute pi two equal to one minus pi one. Equation 3: pi one equals 0.750 pi one plus 0.125 times one minus pi one; so 0.375 pi one equals 0.125; so pi is one third and two thirds.

Powers of P, Greenberg printed page 80. P to the 10th has rows 0.339 and 0.661, and 0.330 and 0.670. P to the 20th has both rows equal to 0.333 and 0.667. Every row converges to pi, so after 20 steps the starting state is forgotten to three decimals. The speed is set by the second eigenvalue of P, one minus 0.250 minus 0.125, which is 0.625, Greenberg exercise 6.5: the distance to pi shrinks like 0.625 to the n.

When convergence fails, Greenberg printed pages 78 to 81. A reducible chain, with a block-diagonal P, never leaves the block it starts in, and has many invariant distributions. A periodic chain alternates, and P to the n does not converge. Theorem 6.2: if P is irreducible and aperiodic on a finite space, there is a unique invariant pi, and the absolute difference between the n-step probability p i j and pi j is at most r to the n over nu, with r below one.

Seven point three. General state spaces and the ergodic average. Greenberg sections 6.2 and 6.3.

For continuous states the transition kernel p of x and y replaces p i j, and a density pi is invariant if, Greenberg equation 6.9, equation 4: pi of y equals the integral of pi of x times p of x and y, over x.

An example, Greenberg printed pages 85 to 86. The first-order autoregression y t equals phi y t minus one plus u t, with the absolute value of phi below one and u t normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared, has invariant density normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared over one minus phi squared: the equilibrium distribution of Class 1 section 7. If y t minus one is normal with mean zero and variance v, with v equal to sigma squared over one minus phi squared, then the variance of y t is phi squared v plus sigma squared, which is v.

Theorem 6.3, the ergodic average. For an irreducible chain whose states are positive recurrent, there is a unique invariant pi and, for any starting distribution, equation 5: one over n times the sum from m equal to 1 to n of f of X m converges to the expected value under pi of f of X, whenever the expected absolute value of f of X under pi is finite.

Equation 5 is the law of large numbers that Markov chain Monte Carlo needs: chain averages estimate posterior expectations. Markov chain Monte Carlo turns Markov-chain theory around, Greenberg printed page 84: instead of finding pi for a given P, it starts from the target pi and constructs a kernel with that invariant distribution. Theorems 6.5 and 6.6 then give convergence for pi-irreducible, aperiodic kernels.

Seven point four. The Gibbs algorithm. Greenberg section 7.1.1; Tsay section 12.2.

Algorithm 7.1, with two blocks. Choose a starting value of x two. At iteration g, equation 6: draw x one at iteration g from f of x one given x two at iteration g minus one; then draw x two at iteration g from f of x two given x one at iteration g.

Discard the first B draws, the burn-in, and keep G draws. With d blocks, Algorithm 7.2 and Tsay's three parameters in equation 12.1, each block is drawn from its full conditional given the most recent values of all the others.

Why the target is invariant, Greenberg printed pages 91 to 92. The Gibbs kernel from x, with components x one and x two, to y, with components y one and y two, is p of x and y equal to f of y one given x two, times f of y two given y one. Check equation 4, in three steps. Equation 7. First, the integral of p of x and y times f of x over x is the double integral of f of y one given x two, times f of y two given y one, times f of x one and x two, over x one and x two. Second, integrating x one out of the joint leaves f of y two given y one, times the integral of f of y one given x two times f of x two over x two. Third, by the law of total probability, that integral is f of y one, so the result is f of y two given y one times f of y one, which is f of y.

How to find a full conditional, Greenberg printed page 55. Write the joint posterior, likelihood times prior; keep only the factors that contain the block; and recognise the kernel. Everything else goes into the constant. Conditionally conjugate priors, Greenberg section 4.9, make every kernel standard.

Using the output, Tsay equation 12.3. Point estimates and variances are sample moments of the retained draws; differences, ratios and other functions are computed draw by draw, Tsay printed page 616.

Seven point five. Worked: the normal mean and precision. Greenberg Algorithm 7.3, equations 7.1 and 7.2.

The y i are independent normal with mean mu and variance one over h. The priors are mu normal with mean mu zero and variance one over h zero, and h Gamma with parameters alpha zero over two and delta zero over two, independent; by Greenberg section 4.9, this is not jointly conjugate. Seven point seven. Data augmentation. Tsay section 12.1; Morettin and Singer section C.5. This is the C layer.

Seven point eight. Mixing, burn-in and the numerical standard error. Greenberg printed page 94 and section 7.3.

When Gibbs mixes badly, Greenberg printed page 94. Sample X one and X two from a bivariate normal with means zero, unit variances and correlation rho, by Gibbs, with X one given x two normal with mean rho x two and variance one minus rho squared, and X two given x one normal with mean rho x one and variance one minus rho squared. Substitute one conditional into the other. Equation 17: x one at iteration g equals rho times x two at iteration g minus one plus e one; which is rho times the bracket rho x one at g minus one plus e two, plus e one; which is rho squared times x one at g minus one, plus rho e two plus e one.

So the x one chain is a first-order autoregression with coefficient rho squared, and equation 18: its lag-h autocorrelation, rho h, is rho to the two h.

For rho equal to 0.5: the lag-one autocorrelation is 0.25, and its absolute value stays below 0.05 from lag 3, since 0.5 to the sixth is 0.016; that is Greenberg's report. For rho equal to 0.999: the lag-20 autocorrelation is 0.998 to the 20th, which is 0.961, "over 0.900 even at lag 20". With G equal to 5000, the sample mean of x one was 1.084 or minus 0.623 depending on the start, Greenberg printed page 94, against the true zero.

The cost of dependence. The variance of a chain average is the variance of X over G, times one plus two times the sum over h from 1 of rho h. The bracket is the inefficiency factor, also called the autocorrelation time; Greenberg printed page 103 calls its reciprocal the relative numerical efficiency. For a first-order autoregression with coefficient phi it is one plus phi over one minus phi; with phi equal to rho squared, equation 19: the inefficiency factor is one plus rho squared over one minus rho squared. That is 1.67 at rho equal to 0.5; 9.5 at rho equal to 0.9; and 999.5 at rho equal to 0.999.

A chain of G draws carries the information of G over the inefficiency factor independent ones.

The mixing figure has two panels. The left panel shows the autocorrelation of the Gibbs chain for x one against lags 0 to 30, for rho equal to 0.5, which falls to zero by lag 3; 0.9, which decays over about 15 lags; and 0.999, which stays above 0.94 at lag 30. Remedies. Block the correlated parameters together; Tsay, printed page 617, says "when parameters are highly correlated, it pays to draw them jointly". Reparameterise. And run several chains from dispersed starts.

The numerical standard error by batch means, Greenberg section 7.3. Find a lag j zero at which the absolute estimated autocorrelation is below 0.05, cut the G draws into N equal to G over j zero batches, and use the variance of the batch means. Equation 20: the numerical standard error is the square root of one over N, times one over N minus one, times the sum over the batches b of the squared deviation of the batch mean Z bar b from the overall mean Z bar.

The naive square root of the estimated variance over G understates the error by the factor of the square root of the inefficiency factor.

Section 8. Metropolis-Hastings: propose anywhere, accept with the right probability.

This section covers Greenberg section 7.2, printed pages 96 to 101, with equations 7.5 and 7.6, Algorithms 7.4 to 7.6, Theorem 7.2, the Beta of 3 and 4 example and Figure 7.1; Greenberg section 8.2.3, printed pages 126 to 129, with equation 8.10 and Table 8.3; Tsay section 12.4, printed pages 622 to 624, and section 12.7, printed pages 636 to 638, equations 12.20 to 12.23; the site pages on Bayesian logistic regression and on the Petrobras Gaussian and Student t stochastic volatility models; the text file of examples one, two and three in R and Stan; and the Bayesian beta regression page, named only.

The claim, before the derivations. When a full conditional is not a standard distribution, propose a move from any density q of x and y, and accept it with probability alpha of x and y equal to the minimum of one and f of y times q of y and x, over f of x times q of x and y; otherwise stay. This makes the chain reversible with respect to f, so f is invariant, and the unknown normalising constant cancels in the ratio. Gibbs is the special case where the proposal is the full conditional and alpha equals one. The proposal's scale trades acceptance against step size: for a standard normal target and random-walk steps normal with standard deviation s, the acceptance rate is exactly two over pi times the arc tangent of two over s, which is 0.445 at s equal to 2.38. The Bayesian logit is the standard use: a normal prior is not conjugate, a tailored random walk samples it, and the posterior means sit next to the maximum likelihood estimate.

The notation. f is the target density, the posterior, known up to a constant. q of x and y is the proposal density of y given the current x. Alpha of x and y is the acceptance probability, between zero and one. s is the random-walk proposal standard deviation. Beta is the vector of logit coefficients, with K components. P i, the probability that y i equals one, is e to the x i transposed beta, over one plus e to the x i transposed beta. And h t is the volatility in the stochastic volatility model, whose log follows an autoregression.

Eight point one. Accept or stay. This is the U layer.

Gibbs needs every full conditional in a form we can sample. Many models break this at one block: the logit coefficients, a GARCH parameter, a log-volatility. Metropolis-Hastings needs only the ability to evaluate the target up to a constant. It proposes a candidate from a convenient density and then corrects for the mismatch between proposal and target by accepting some moves and rejecting others. Unlike accept-reject, section 6 subsection 5, a rejection does not mean "try again": the chain repeats its current value, so values recur and draws are correlated, Greenberg printed page 97.

Eight point two. Reversibility and the acceptance probability. Greenberg printed pages 96 to 98. This is the C layer.

A kernel p of x and y is reversible with respect to f if, equation 1: f of x times p of x and y equals f of y times p of y and x, for all x and y.

Integrating equation 1 over x gives: the integral of f of x times p of x and y equals f of y times the integral of p of y and x over x, which is f of y. Reversibility implies that f is invariant, section 7 equation 4.

A proposal q is usually not reversible. For some pair, f of x times q of x and y exceeds f of y times q of y and x, so moves from x to y happen too often. Multiply by a factor alpha to restore balance, Greenberg equation 7.5. Equation 2: f of x times alpha of x and y times q of x and y equals f of y times alpha of y and x times q of y and x.

Moves from y to x are too rare, so make them as frequent as possible: set alpha of y and x equal to one. Substitute into equation 2 and solve for alpha of x and y. Equation 3: alpha of x and y equals f of y times q of y and x, over f of x times q of x and y.

Combining both cases gives Greenberg equation 7.6. Equation 4: alpha of x and y equals the minimum of that ratio and one, if f of x times q of x and y is positive, and zero otherwise.

The normalising constant cancels. Only the ratio f of y over f of x enters equation 4, so a posterior known as likelihood times prior is enough, Greenberg printed page 98 and Tsay printed page 622.

Algorithm 7.4. Given x: first, draw Y from q of x and dot; second, draw U uniform on zero to one; if U is at most alpha of x and Y, return Y; otherwise return x. Theorem 7.2, due to Tierney: a pi-irreducible Metropolis kernel is Harris recurrent, so the ergodic theorem, section 7 equation 5, applies.

Eight point three. Two standard kernels. Greenberg printed page 99; Tsay sections 12.4.1 and 12.4.2.

The random walk. y equals x plus u, with u symmetric about zero, so q of x and y equals q of y and x, and equation 4 becomes equation 5: alpha of x and y equals the minimum of f of y over f of x, and one.

This is the original Metropolis algorithm, Tsay section 12.4.1, with a symmetric "jumping distribution": uphill moves are always accepted, and downhill moves with probability equal to the density ratio. Hastings's generalisation, Tsay section 12.4.2, allows an asymmetric q through the correction in equation 4.

The independence kernel. q of x and y equals q of y, so equation 6: alpha of x and y equals the minimum of the ratio f of y over q of y, divided by f of x over q of x, and one.

That is a random walk on the importance ratio f over q; compare section 6 equation 9. Greenberg recommends a tailored kernel: a fat-tailed density, a multivariate t with small degrees of freedom, centred at the posterior mode, with scale equal to the negative inverse Hessian there, printed page 99.

Worked: a Beta of 3 and 4 from uniform proposals, Greenberg Algorithm 7.5. With q equal to one on zero to one and f of x proportional to x squared times one minus x cubed, equation 6 gives equation 7: alpha of the previous value x and the proposal U two equals the minimum of U two squared times one minus U two cubed, over x squared times one minus x cubed, and one.

Greenberg: 5000 draws after 500 of burn-in, acceptance 0.57, sample mean 0.4296 against three sevenths, which is 0.4286. The stationary acceptance rate of an independence sampler with q equal to one is the double integral of the minimum of f of x and f of y over x and y, obtained by averaging equation 6 over x from f and y uniform; for the Beta of 3 and 4 it is 0.5726. A seeded Python run gives acceptance 0.581 and mean 0.4224.

Eight point four. The proposal scale: an exact acceptance curve.

Take the standard normal target and random-walk proposals y equal to x plus u, with u normal with mean zero and standard deviation s. The stationary acceptance rate is the expected value of the minimum of one and r, where r is f of y over f of x and x is drawn from f. Split it. Equation 8: the expected value of the minimum of one and r equals the probability that r is at least one, plus the expected value of r times the indicator that r is below one.

The second term is the double integral of f of y times q of y minus x, times the indicator that f of y is below f of x. Relabelling x and y and using the symmetry of q turns it into the probability that r exceeds one. So the acceptance rate is twice the probability that f of y exceeds f of x, which is twice the probability that the absolute value of x plus u is below the absolute value of x.

Write u equal to s times z, with z standard normal independent of x. The absolute value of x plus s z is below the absolute value of x exactly when z times two x plus s z is negative: the double wedge between the lines z equal to zero and z equal to minus two x over s. That wedge has angle equal to the arc tangent of two over s on each side, and the pair x and z is rotation-invariant, so its probability is two times the arc tangent of two over s, over two pi. Hence equation 9: the acceptance rate at scale s is two over pi times the arc tangent of two over s.

Six values of equation 9. At s equal to 0.1, acceptance 0.968. At 0.5, 0.844. At 1, 0.705. At 2.38, 0.445. At 8, 0.156. At 20, 0.063.

The right panel of the mixing figure in section 7 plots equation 9 against the proposal standard deviation on a log scale, the curve falling from near one to near zero, with seeded runs on it and s equal to 2.38 marked at 0.445. Small s accepts almost everything but crawls, with high autocorrelation, as with rho tending to one in Gibbs. Large s proposes bold moves that are almost always rejected, and the chain sits still. That is Greenberg's warning, printed page 98, in numbers: neither a high nor a low acceptance rate is a goal in itself. The value s equal to 2.38, in one dimension, is the scale that minimises the autocorrelation time of this chain; that result is due to Gelman, Roberts and Gilks, and is G layer. The tailored kernels in subsection 6 use 2.38 squared over K times the inverse Hessian.

Eight point five. Gibbs is Metropolis-Hastings with acceptance one. Greenberg printed pages 100 to 101.

In a block update, propose Y one from the full conditional f of x one given x two. Then equation 4 reads, equation 10: alpha of x one and Y one given x two equals f of Y one and x two times f of x one given x two, over f of x one and x two times f of Y one given x two. Writing each conditional as a joint over f of x two, that is f of Y one and x two times f of x one and x two over f of x two, divided by f of x one and x two times f of Y one and x two over f of x two, which is one.

Every proposal is accepted. This licenses Metropolis-Hastings within Gibbs, Greenberg Algorithm 7.6: Gibbs steps for the blocks with standard conditionals, Metropolis-Hastings steps for the rest, in any order within a sweep. Tsay's Griddy Gibbs, section 12.4.3, is a third option for a univariate nonstandard conditional: evaluate it on a grid, build an approximate inverse distribution function, and draw by section 6 equation 4.

Eight point seven. Stochastic volatility by Markov chain Monte Carlo. Tsay section 12.7; the Petrobras page.

Tsay's univariate stochastic volatility model, equations 12.20 and 12.21. Equation 12: r t equals x t transposed beta plus a t; a t equals the square root of h t times epsilon t; and the log of h t equals alpha zero plus alpha one times the log of h t minus one, plus v t; with epsilon t standard normal and v t normal with mean zero and variance sigma v squared, and the absolute value of alpha one below one.

The likelihood, f of R given beta and alpha, equal to the integral of f of R given beta and H times f of H given alpha over H, is an n-dimensional integral. Part four of the notes, section 9. Hierarchical models: estimate each unit by borrowing strength from the others.

This section opens part four of the notes, priors as models. It covers Greenberg section 4.5, printed pages 50 to 52, equations 4.8 and 4.9; section 4.6, printed pages 52 to 53, equations 4.10 to 4.12; and section 4.9, printed pages 54 to 56. It also covers the hierarchical Beta-Binomial site page, on 13 Manhattan hospitals and heart-attack deaths in 2015, with Stan output, after Albert's Bayesian Hierarchical Modeling, section 10.3; and the hierarchical Gaussian linear model site page, on 68 pregnant women's weight gain, with a Gibbs sampler, after Gamerman and Lopes, 2006, example 5.6.

The claim, before the derivations. When units, such as hospitals, women or funds, are similar but not identical, treat their parameters as exchangeable draws from a common distribution whose parameters are themselves estimated. Each unit's posterior mean is then a weighted average of its own estimate and the group mean, with weight eta over eta plus n i on the group: small units are pulled hardest. For 13 Manhattan hospitals the death rates range from 2.9 per cent to 13.0 per cent separately; partially pooled, from 5.7 per cent to 8.9 per cent, around a posterior mean of mu of 7.2 per cent. An exact grid computation reproduces the lecturer's Stan output: a posterior median of mu of 0.0714 against 0.0715. For 68 women's weight-gain curves, the Gibbs sampler of the random-coefficient model separates within-woman noise, sigma about 1.10 kilograms, from between-woman spread, which a pooled regression lumps into one residual standard deviation of 3.61 kilograms.

The notation. y i and n i are the deaths and heart-attack cases at hospital i, for i from 1 to 13. p i is the death probability of hospital i. Mu is the mean of the population of hospital rates. Eta is the precision of that population, a plus b of the Beta, which acts as a prior sample size. w i is the weight on mu in the posterior mean of p i, eta over eta plus n i. y i j and x i j are the weight gain in kilograms and the week of visit j of woman i, for i up to 68 and j up to n i. Alpha i and beta i are woman i's intercept and slope. And alpha, beta, tau alpha and tau beta are the population means and precisions of the coefficients.

Nine point one. Three ways to estimate 13 rates. The hierarchical Beta-Binomial page, section 1. This is the U layer.

The page's data: 13 Manhattan hospitals, 2366 heart-attack cases, 163 deaths. Mount Sinai Roosevelt loses 6 of 46 patients, 13.0 per cent, "more than four times the rate of Harlem Hospital Center at 2.857 per cent", which is 1 of 35. Three analyses.

Complete pooling: one p for all, 163 over 2366, which is 6.89 per cent. It ignores real differences between hospitals.

No pooling: 13 separate binomials. A hospital with 35 cases gets an estimate as noisy as its sample, and nothing learned about the other 12 hospitals is used.

Partial pooling, the hierarchical model: the p i are draws from a common Beta whose mean and spread are estimated. Each estimate leans on the group in proportion to how little data the hospital has.

Nine point two. Exchangeability. Greenberg section 4.5. This is the C layer.

Random variables z one to z n are exchangeable if their joint distribution is unchanged by any permutation of the indices, Greenberg printed page 50. Independent and identically distributed variables are exchangeable, but exchangeable variables need not be independent. Applied to parameters: if nothing but the data distinguishes hospital 3 from hospital 7, the prior should treat p one to p 13 symmetrically, and the natural way is independent draws given hyperparameters, with uncertainty about those hyperparameters making the p i dependent a priori. Greenberg's heteroskedastic regression, equations 4.8 and 4.9, is the same idea for variances: each observation's precision lambda i is a draw from a Gamma with parameters nu over two and nu over two, and marginally the errors are Student t, section 6 equation 6.

Nine point three. The hierarchical model. Greenberg section 4.6.

Greenberg's three levels, equations 4.10 to 4.12. Equation 1: y is distributed f of y given theta; theta is distributed pi of theta given theta zero; and theta zero is distributed pi of theta zero given theta zero zero, with theta zero zero fixed.

Two remarks, Greenberg printed page 53. First, theta zero is not identified by the data: f of y given theta and theta zero equals f of y given theta, so the data inform theta zero only through theta. Second, theta zero can be integrated out: pi of theta given theta zero zero is the integral of pi of theta given theta zero times pi of theta zero given theta zero zero. The hierarchy is a way to build a prior with dependence across units and to compute with it, not a different model.

Nine point four. Worked: the Beta-Binomial for the hospitals. The hierarchical Beta-Binomial page.

The model, from the page's Stan file. Equation 2: y i given p i is binomial with n i trials and probability p i; p i given mu and eta is Beta with parameters mu eta and one minus mu times eta; mu is uniform on zero to one; and the prior density of eta is n one over n one plus eta squared, with n one equal to 10.

This Beta has mean mu and variance mu times one minus mu over eta plus one, so eta acts as a prior sample size: a large eta means the hospitals are nearly identical. The prior on eta is proper, since the integral from zero to infinity of n one over n one plus eta squared is one, with median n one, equal to 10, and a heavy right tail.

Integrate the p i out. For one hospital, Beta-binomial conjugacy, section 2 equation 4, gives the marginal likelihood. Equation 3: p of y i given mu and eta equals n i choose y i, times the Beta function of mu eta plus y i and one minus mu times eta plus n i minus y i, over the Beta function of mu eta and one minus mu times eta.

So the posterior of the two hyperparameters is equation 4: p of mu and eta given y is proportional to n one over n one plus eta squared, times the product over the 13 hospitals of the same ratio of Beta functions.

Equation 4 is two-dimensional. It can be evaluated exactly on a grid, here 1000 by 1400 points in mu and the log of eta, with no Markov chain Monte Carlo at all.

Each hospital, given the hyperparameters. Conjugacy again. Equation 5: p i given mu, eta and y is Beta with parameters mu eta plus y i, and one minus mu times eta plus n i minus y i. Its mean is mu eta plus y i over eta plus n i, which equals w i times mu plus one minus w i times y i over n i, with w i equal to eta over eta plus n i.

The second form splits mu eta plus y i exactly as section 2 equation 6. Averaging equation 5 over equation 4 gives the posterior mean of p i given y.

Section 10. Sparsity priors: shrink the noise, leave the signals alone.

This section covers the sparse-priors deck, nine image pages: Carvalho, Polson and Scott, 2010, in Biometrika volume 97, section 1.1, Table 1 and Figure 2 on printed page 468, and Table 4 on printed page 477, the Vanguard funds; and Griffin and Brown, 2010, in Bayesian Analysis volume 5, equations 1 to 3, Figure 1, section 5.3 with Table 3, and Figure 7. It also covers the sparse-regression simulation, its PDF and its R script, which uses the bayesreg package for ridge, lasso and horseshoe; Izbicki and Mendonça section 3.6, printed pages 42 to 43; and ISLP section 6.2, printed pages 240 to 251, equations 6.5 and 6.7 and Figure 6.11. For the horseshoe and the normal-gamma, the slides are the only source; there is no book on disk.

The claim, before the derivations. With many coefficients and few of them non-zero, the prior should pull small estimates hard toward zero and leave large ones almost untouched. A normal prior cannot: its posterior mean is y times a constant. That is ridge, which is the posterior mode under a Gaussian prior, as lasso is under a Laplace prior. Scale mixtures of normals give each coefficient its own variance. The horseshoe, with half-Cauchy local scales, puts a Beta prior with parameters one half and one half on the shrinkage factor kappa, equal to one over one plus lambda squared, with mass at both ends: kappa near one kills noise, and kappa near zero keeps signals. With y given theta normal with mean theta and variance one, and tau equal to one, an observation y equal to 6 is estimated at 5.66 under the horseshoe, 4.59 under a Laplace prior of the same variance, and 3 under the normal prior. In the lecturer's simulation, with n equal to 200 and 3 signals among 50 coefficients, the horseshoe's null estimates are about 8 times closer to zero than ridge's, with the signals equally well estimated.

The notation. Beta j is a regression coefficient and theta i a normal mean, both real. Psi j, or lambda j squared, is the coefficient-specific, or local, variance. Tau is the global scale. Kappa i, equal to one over one plus lambda i squared, is the shrinkage factor, with sigma and tau equal to one; it lies between zero and one. C plus of zero and a is the half-Cauchy with scale a. Ga of c and d is the gamma with shape c and rate d, in Griffin and Brown's notation. And lambda, for ridge and lasso, is the penalty weight, non-negative.

Ten point one. The problem with one variance for everyone. This is the U layer.

A normal prior, beta j normal with mean zero and variance v, shrinks every least squares coefficient by the same factor. If v is small enough to silence 97 null coefficients, it also drags the three real ones toward zero; if it is large enough to spare the signals, it lets the noise through. Griffin and Brown, deck page 5: a zero-mean normal prior "performs poorly if there are large differences in the size of regression coefficients". The fix is to let each coefficient carry its own variance, drawn from a distribution with a lot of mass near zero and heavy tails.

Ten point two. Ridge and lasso as posterior modes. Izbicki and Mendonça section 3.6; ISLP printed pages 250 to 251. This is the C layer.

Let y i given x i and beta be normal with mean beta transposed x i and variance sigma squared, with sigma squared known. The log posterior is the log-likelihood plus the log prior. Equation 1: the log posterior of beta equals minus one over two sigma squared times the sum of y i minus beta transposed x i squared, plus the log prior, plus a constant.

The Gaussian prior: beta j independent normal with mean zero and variance tau squared, so the log prior is minus the sum of beta j squared over two tau squared, plus a constant. Multiply equation 1 by minus two sigma squared: maximising the posterior is minimising equation 2, the sum of y i minus beta transposed x i squared, plus sigma squared over tau squared times the sum of beta j squared.

That is the ridge criterion, ISLP equation 6.5, printed page 240, with lambda equal to sigma squared over tau squared. The posterior is normal, so its mode equals its mean.

The Laplace prior: pi of beta j equals one over two b times e to the minus the absolute value of beta j over b, so the log prior is minus the sum of the absolute values of beta j over b, plus a constant. The same step gives equation 3: the sum of y i minus beta transposed x i squared, plus two sigma squared over b times the sum of the absolute values of beta j.

That is the lasso criterion, ISLP equation 6.7, printed page 244, with lambda equal to two sigma squared over b. Izbicki and Mendonça, printed page 42: "under the Bayesian approach, the tuning parameter lambda is defined by the prior distribution"; a tighter prior means a larger lambda, and choosing lambda by cross-validation "is equivalent to choosing the prior that gives the best predictive power". Two cautions. The lasso's zeros are a property of the mode; the posterior mean under a Laplace prior is never exactly zero. And ISLP's Figure 6.11 makes the shapes plain: the Laplace density has a sharp peak at zero, the normal a rounded one.

Ten point three. Scale mixtures of normals. Griffin and Brown section 3; deck pages 5 to 7.

Equation 4: pi of beta j is the integral of the normal density of beta j with mean zero and variance Psi j, against the mixing distribution G of Psi j. Equivalently, beta j given Psi j is normal with mean zero and variance Psi j, and Psi j is drawn from G.

Every such prior has heavier-than-normal tails, unless G is a point mass. Five special cases, each a mixing distribution and the prior it gives on beta j.

A point mass at v gives the normal prior: ridge.

A mixture that puts Psi j at sigma beta squared with probability w, through an indicator z j, and at zero otherwise, gives the spike-and-slab: w times a normal with variance sigma beta squared, plus one minus w times a point mass at zero.

An exponential mixing distribution gives the double exponential: the Bayesian lasso of Park and Casella, 2008.

A gamma with shape lambda and rate one over two gamma squared gives the normal-gamma prior.

And Psi j equal to lambda j squared times tau squared, with lambda j half-Cauchy with scale one, gives the horseshoe.

Equation 4 is the composition idea of section 6, subsection 4: the Student t was the case where Psi is inverse-gamma. It also makes Gibbs sampling easy: given the Psi j, the coefficients are normal.

The normal-gamma moments. With Psi distributed gamma with shape lambda and rate one over two gamma squared, the expected value of Psi is two lambda gamma squared, and its variance is lambda times two gamma squared, squared. By equation 4, equation 5: the variance of beta is the expected value of the conditional variance, which is the expected value of Psi, two lambda gamma squared; and the expected value of beta to the fourth is the expected value of three Psi squared, which is three times the bracket lambda times two gamma squared squared, plus lambda squared times two gamma squared squared.

So the kurtosis, the expected value of beta to the fourth over the variance squared, is three times lambda plus lambda squared, over lambda squared, which is three plus three over lambda: an excess kurtosis of three over lambda, Griffin and Brown's statement. As lambda falls, the prior "places a lot of mass close to zero but at the same time has heavy tails", their Figure 1, which shows variance 2 with lambda equal to 0.1, 0.333 and 1. A simulation check with lambda equal to 0.333 and unit variance gives a sample excess kurtosis of 8.9, against three over 0.333, which is 9.0. Lambda equal to one is the Bayesian lasso.

Ten point four. The horseshoe. Carvalho, Polson and Scott, 2010; deck pages 1 to 2.

For y given theta p-variate normal with mean theta and covariance sigma squared times the identity, equation 6: theta i given lambda i is normal with mean zero and variance lambda i squared; lambda i given tau is half-Cauchy with scale tau; tau given sigma is half-Cauchy with scale sigma; and the prior of sigma squared is proportional to one over sigma squared.

In the paper's words: "Each theta i is mixed over its own lambda i, and each lambda i has a half-Cauchy prior with common scale tau."

The posterior mean as shrinkage. Fix sigma and tau equal to one. Given lambda i, the normal-normal update of section 3 equation 8, with prior variance lambda i squared and data variance one, gives the posterior mean of theta i given y and lambda i equal to one minus kappa i, times y i, with kappa i equal to one over one plus lambda i squared. Average over lambda i given y. Equation 7: the posterior mean of theta i is the integral from zero to one of one minus kappa i, times y i, times the posterior density of kappa i; which is one minus the posterior mean of kappa i, times y i.

The posterior mean of kappa i "can be interpreted as the amount of shrinkage toward zero, a posteriori".

Why "horseshoe". With lambda half-Cauchy with scale one, the density of lambda is two over pi times one plus lambda squared. Change variables to kappa equal to one over one plus lambda squared. Then lambda is the square root of one minus kappa over kappa, and the absolute derivative of lambda with respect to kappa is one half times kappa to the minus three halves times one minus kappa to the minus one half. Substitute, using one plus lambda squared equal to one over kappa. Equation 8: the density of kappa is two over pi, times kappa, times one half kappa to the minus three halves times one minus kappa to the minus one half; which is one over pi times kappa to the minus one half times one minus kappa to the minus one half.

That is the Beta density with parameters one half and one half, U-shaped like a horseshoe. "The left side of the horseshoe, kappa i near zero, yields virtually no shrinkage, and describes signals. The right side, kappa i near one, yields near-total shrinkage and describes noise."

The posterior of kappa. Since y i given kappa i is normal with mean zero and variance one plus lambda i squared, which is one over kappa i, the likelihood is kappa i to the one half times e to the minus kappa i y i squared over two. Multiply by equation 8. Equation 9: the posterior of kappa i is proportional to one minus kappa i to the minus one half, times e to the minus kappa i y i squared over two.

For small absolute y i, equation 9 keeps the pole at kappa equal to one, so the posterior mean of kappa is near one and theta is shrunk hard. For large absolute y i, the exponential kills kappa away from zero and the posterior mean of kappa tends to zero: the signal is left alone.

Other priors through kappa, from Carvalho, Polson and Scott's Table 1 and Figure 2. The double exponential: the density of kappa is proportional to kappa to the minus two times e to the minus one over two kappa, which vanishes at kappa equal to zero, so even large signals keep some shrinkage. The Cauchy: kappa to the minus one half, times one minus kappa to the minus three halves, times e to the minus kappa over two times one minus kappa. Strawderman-Berger: kappa to the minus one half. Normal-Jeffreys: kappa to the minus one times one minus kappa to the minus one, improper at both ends. Only the horseshoe is unbounded at both ends and integrable.

Ten point five. Worked: shrinkage profiles.

y given theta is normal with mean theta and variance one, under three priors with unit variance where defined: the standard normal; the Laplace with b equal to one over the square root of two, whose variance, two b squared, is one; and the horseshoe with tau equal to one and with tau equal to 0.1. Posterior means by quadrature of equations 7 to 9, for three cases.

At y equal to 2: normal, y over 2, which is 1.000; Laplace, 0.904; horseshoe with tau equal to one, 1.063.

At y equal to 6: normal, 3.000; Laplace, 4.586; horseshoe, 5.656.

For large y: normal, y over 2; Laplace, approximately y minus the square root of two; horseshoe, tending to y.

For large y the Laplace prior subtracts a constant, one over b, which is the square root of two, the slope of its log density; so big signals stay biased by 1.41. The horseshoe's bias vanishes.

The right panel of the shrinkage figure plots the posterior mean of theta against y from 0 to 8 under the four priors. The normal-prior line is y over 2. The Laplace curve approaches a line parallel to the 45-degree line, about 1.4 below it. The horseshoe curve with tau equal to one is close to the 45-degree line from y equal to 4. And the horseshoe curve with tau equal to 0.1 stays near zero up to y equal to 2.5, then rises steeply to meet the 45-degree line near y equal to 6.

The comparative static in tau. Shrinking the global scale to 0.1 makes the horseshoe treat every absolute y below 2.5 as noise, with the posterior mean below 10 per cent of y at y equal to one, while still letting y equal to 6 through almost unshrunk. That is the "global-local" design: tau sets how sparse the vector is believed to be, and the lambda i let individual coefficients escape.

Ten point six. Worked: the sparse-regression simulation, from the R script.

The design. n equal to 200; k max equal to 100 standard-normal covariates; sigma equal to 0.1; beta equal to 1, 2, 3, then zeros; and y equal to X beta plus epsilon. Fit with the first k columns, for k equal to 10, 20, 40, 50 and 100, under ridge, lasso and horseshoe priors, with the R package bayesreg, 1000 burn-in and 1000 draws, and box-plot the posterior draws of beta one, beta two and beta three and of the nulls. The PDF's boxplots show the three signals estimated to within about plus or minus 0.01 by all three priors, and the null coefficients spread over roughly plus or minus 0.04 for ridge and lasso, noticeably tighter for the horseshoe, and more so as k grows.

The NumPy port, in the Python note, section 10. bayesreg is R-only, so the three samplers are written out, with no intercept, since the simulated covariates have mean zero. Each is a Gibbs sampler on the scale-mixture representation of equation 4, with beta j given everything else normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared times D j. Equation 10: beta given everything else is k-variate normal with mean A inverse X transposed y and covariance sigma squared times A inverse, where A is X transposed X plus D inverse; and sigma squared given everything else is inverse gamma with parameters n plus k over two, and the residual sum of squares plus beta transposed D inverse beta, over two.

The priors differ only in D and its update. Three cases.

Ridge: D is tau squared times the identity, with tau half-Cauchy with scale one, drawn through the auxiliary-variable form, tau squared given xi inverse gamma with parameters one half and one over xi, and xi inverse gamma with parameters one half and one, after Makalic and Schmidt, 2016.

The Bayesian lasso of Park and Casella: D is the diagonal matrix of the tau j squared; one over tau j squared, given everything else, is inverse Gaussian with mean the square root of lambda squared sigma squared over beta j squared, and shape lambda squared; and lambda squared, given everything else, is Gamma with parameters one plus k and 0.1 plus the sum of the tau j squared over two.

The horseshoe: D is tau squared times the diagonal matrix of the lambda j squared. Lambda j squared, given everything else, is inverse gamma with parameters one and one over nu j plus beta j squared over two tau squared sigma squared. Tau squared is inverse gamma with parameters k plus one over two, and one over xi plus the sum of beta j squared over two sigma squared lambda j squared. Recap of part 2. When a posterior integral has no closed form, simulate it: Monte Carlo averages independent draws with an error that falls like one over the square root of M, and the inverse distribution function, composition, accept-reject, importance sampling and resampling produce those draws. When independent draws are out of reach, build a Markov chain whose invariant distribution is the posterior: Gibbs draws each block from its full conditional, as in the coal switch point at 1891; Metropolis-Hastings proposes and accepts with a probability that makes the chain reversible, with the scale trade-off two over pi times the arc tangent of two over s; and dependence costs draws, measured by the inefficiency factor. Finally, priors are models: a hierarchy learns how much to pool, with weight eta over eta plus n i, and sparsity priors give each coefficient its own variance, so the horseshoe kills noise and keeps signals.
