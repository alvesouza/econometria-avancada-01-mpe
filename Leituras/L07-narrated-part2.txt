The claim, before the derivations. With N units observed T times there are three models. Pooled: one line for all. Individual effects: one intercept per unit. Random coefficients: one line per unit. Giving the unit parameters a common prior whose mean and variance are themselves estimated, the hierarchical prior, makes each unit's posterior mean a weighted average of its own estimate and the population mean, with weight T V alpha over T V alpha plus sigma squared on its own data. Units with little or noisy data borrow strength from the others, and the data decide how much, through the between-unit variance: V alpha, or tau in the eight schools. Every conditional is normal, inverse gamma or Wishart: a pure Gibbs sampler.

The notation, slide 3. i runs from one to N over units, and t from one to T over occasions; y i t is the response and x i t, k by one, the regressors. y i is the vector y i one up to y i T; X i is T by k with rows x i t transposed; X i equals the column of ones, one sub T, next to X tilde i, which splits the constant from the other regressors X tilde i; y and X stack the units. Beta, equal to alpha and beta tilde, holds the common coefficients; alpha i are the unit intercepts; and beta i, equal to alpha i and beta tilde i, are the unit coefficient vectors. The first-level prior parameters are mu alpha and V alpha for the intercepts, or beta zero and V zero for the coefficient vectors. The second-level priors, the hyperpriors, are: mu alpha normal with mean mu zero and variance V mu; V alpha inverse-gamma with parameters eta zero over two and eta zero sigma zero squared over two; beta zero normal with mean beta zero zero and variance V beta; and V zero inverse Wishart with nu zero degrees of freedom and scale V zero zero inverse. For the eight schools, y i is the estimated effect, sigma i its standard error, theta i the true effect, and mu and tau the population mean and standard deviation, slide 23.

Four point one. Pool, separate, or compromise. The U layer.

Pooling all 427 weight-gain measurements of 68 pregnant women into one regression ignores that some women gain systematically more. Fitting 68 separate lines uses 5 to 7 points per line and produces wild intercepts, from minus 14.4 to 5.2. The hierarchical model says the 68 lines are draws from a population of lines: each woman's line is estimated from her data and from what the other 67 say about the population. Greenberg section 4.6, printed page 52, gives the formal shape: data f of y given theta, prior pi of theta given theta zero, hyperprior pi of theta zero given theta zero zero. He notes that theta zero "is not identified" by itself, since the likelihood does not depend on it, but is introduced "to facilitate computations or modeling".

The partial-pooling weight. Divide the mean in equation 5 by the precision, with r bar i equal to one sub T transposed r i over T, the unit's average. Equation 6: mu tilde i alpha equals T over sigma squared times r bar i, plus one over V alpha times mu alpha, all over T over sigma squared plus one over V alpha; which is w times r bar i plus one minus w times mu alpha, where w equals T V alpha over T V alpha plus sigma squared.

Equation 6 is the whole idea. w tends to one, no pooling, the fixed-effects estimate r bar i, when T goes to infinity or V alpha goes to infinity. w tends to zero, complete pooling, everyone gets mu alpha, when V alpha goes to zero or sigma squared is huge. Because V alpha is estimated, the data choose the degree of pooling.

Beta tilde given the intercepts, slide 12. y i minus one sub T alpha i equals X tilde i beta tilde plus epsilon i, for all i: a pooled regression. V tilde beta inverse equals V beta inverse plus sigma to the minus two times the sum over i of X tilde i transposed X tilde i; and V tilde beta inverse beta one equals V beta inverse beta zero plus sigma to the minus two times the sum over i of X tilde i transposed times y i minus one sub T alpha i.

Mu alpha given the intercepts. The alpha i are N independent draws from a normal with mean mu alpha and variance V alpha: a normal-mean problem. The terms are minus one half times the bracket of the sum over i of alpha i minus mu alpha, squared, over V alpha, plus mu alpha minus mu zero, squared, over V mu. The slip on slide 13: it prints sigma to the minus two times N and sigma to the minus two times the sum of the alpha i. Step two, mu given tau. Equation 11 is a normal-mean problem with weights w i equal to one over sigma i squared plus tau squared. With a normal prior with mean zero and variance v zero, equation 12: mu given y and tau is normal with mean mu hat and variance V mu, where V mu inverse equals v zero inverse plus the sum of the w i, and mu hat equals V mu times the sum of w i y i.

Step three, theta i given mu and tau. A normal prior with mean mu and variance tau squared, and one normal observation y i with variance sigma i squared. Part three, section 5. The Tobit: a regression on a latent y star, seen only above zero. Fill in the hidden part and it is ordinary least squares again.

This section covers LDV slides 4 to 10: the Tobit examples, from Greene printed page 761; the censoring rule; the likelihood; data augmentation; the linear Tobit full conditionals; the truncation-probability example; and the posteriors with and without the censoring. It also covers Greenberg section 8.2.1, printed pages 117 to 122, with the likelihood, equation 8.6, Algorithm 8.2 and the Mroz example in Table 8.1; and Wooldridge section 17.2, printed pages 536 to 543, equations 17.18 to 17.30, Example 17.2 and Tables 17.3 and 17.4; and section 17.4, printed pages 547 to 553, equations 17.36 to 17.41. The numbers are re-run in the Python checks, section 5.

The claim, before the derivations. A corner-solution or censored outcome is y equal to the maximum of zero and y star, with y star equal to x transposed beta plus u, and u normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared. The likelihood mixes densities, for y positive, and probabilities, capital Phi of minus x transposed beta over sigma, for y equal to zero, and it is awkward for Bayes. Data augmentation, Chib 1992, draws the hidden y i star of the zeros from a normal truncated to the interval from minus infinity to zero, after which beta and sigma squared have the plain regression conditionals. The coefficient beta is the effect on the latent mean. The effect on what is observed is the partial derivative of the expected value of y given x with respect to x j, which is beta j times capital Phi of x transposed beta over sigma. And ordinary least squares on the censored data estimates roughly that, not beta: with normal regressors, exactly beta times the probability that y is positive.

The notation. y i star equals x i transposed beta plus u i, with u i normal with mean zero and variance sigma squared: the latent outcome. The observed y i equals y i star times the indicator that y i star is positive, which is the maximum of zero and y i star; slide 5, Greenberg printed page 118, Wooldridge equations 17.18 and 17.19. C is the set of i with y i equal to zero, and k, its size, is the number of zeros, slide 6. z i equals x i transposed beta over sigma. Lowercase phi and capital Phi are the standard normal density and distribution function, and lambda of c, equal to phi of c over capital Phi of c, is the inverse Mills ratio, Wooldridge printed page 538. TN on the interval from a to b, with mean mu and variance sigma squared, is the normal truncated to that interval; slide 8 writes it as a normal on minus infinity to zero.

Five point one. Corner solutions, censoring, truncation. The U layer.

Slide 4 lists Tobin's 1958 durable-goods purchases, hours worked by married women, extramarital affairs, arrests, and vacation spending: many exact zeros, and a continuous spread above zero. Two stories produce the same data, Greenberg printed page 118. Top or bottom coding: the value exists but is reported as the limit; that is true censoring. And corner solutions: the zero is a genuine choice. The Tobit algebra is the same; the interpretation differs. Wooldridge, printed page 539, notes that for corner solutions y star often has no meaning and sigma is not "ancillary". Truncation is different again: observations with y star at or below zero are not in the sample at all, x included, Greenberg printed page 118 and Wooldridge section 17.4b. The slides call the censoring rule "truncation", on slide 5, and the probability that y is zero the "probability of truncation", on slides 9 and 10. Read censoring throughout.

Five point two. The likelihood. Slide 6; Wooldridge equations 17.20 to 17.22.

For y i positive, y i equals y i star, and the density is the normal one. Equation 1: f of y i given x i, beta and sigma equals one over sigma times phi of y i minus x i transposed beta, over sigma, for y i positive.

For y i equal to zero, a probability mass. Equation 2: the probability that y i is zero given x i equals the probability that u i is at most minus x i transposed beta, which is capital Phi of minus x i transposed beta over sigma, which is one minus capital Phi of z i.

Multiply over independent observations. The claim, before the derivations. Each model is a normal regression on a latent y star observed only through a cut: its sign, in the binary probit; the interval between cut-points it falls in, in the ordered probit; or which latent utility is largest, in the multinomial probit. Only the cut is seen, so the scale is not identified: sigma is set to one, or the first diagonal entry of the covariance is set to one. The Albert-Chib trick makes all three Gibbs samplers: draw y star from a truncated normal consistent with the observed category, then draw beta by a normal regression on y star. Cut-points get uniform conditionals. The multinomial version is a seemingly unrelated regression in utility differences, with a truncated multivariate normal. Coefficients are not effects: the effect on the probability that y is one is phi of x transposed beta times beta j, which is at most 0.4 times beta j.

The notation. y i star equals x i transposed beta plus u i, with u i standard normal: the latent index. The binary y i is the indicator that y i star is positive, Greenberg 8.8 and 8.9, Wooldridge 17.6. Ordered: y i takes values from one to J, and y i equals j if and only if y i star lies above gamma j minus one and at or below gamma j, with cut-points running from gamma zero equal to minus infinity, through gamma one, up to gamma J equal to plus infinity, in increasing order, slide 11. Multinomial: alternatives zero, one, up to J; U i j is the utility; y i j star equals U i j minus U i zero, which equals x i j transposed beta j plus epsilon i j; y i star is the vector of the y i j star for j from one to J; and epsilon i is J-dimensional normal with mean zero and covariance Omega, slides 13 and 14. Capital Lambda of z, equal to e to the z over one plus e to the z, is the logistic distribution function.

Six point four. The multinomial probit as a seemingly unrelated regression. Slides 13 to 15.

The choice rule, slide 13. Individual i picks the alternative with the largest utility. Differencing from alternative zero, y i j star equals U i j minus U i zero, which equals x i j transposed beta j plus epsilon i j. The econometrician sees, equation 9: y i equals zero if and only if the largest y i j star is negative; and y i equals j if and only if y i j star is the largest of the y i star and is at least zero.

Stacking, slide 14. y i star equals X i beta plus epsilon i, where X i is block diagonal with blocks x i one transposed up to x i J transposed, beta stacks beta one up to beta J, and epsilon i is J-dimensional normal with mean zero and covariance Omega. Over individuals, y star equals X beta plus epsilon, with the variance of epsilon equal to the identity of size N Kronecker Omega: a seemingly unrelated regression, GLM3 slides 31 and 32, but latent.

The conditionals, slide 15. Beta is normal, in the seemingly-unrelated-regression generalized least squares form: V one inverse equals V zero inverse plus the sum over i of X i transposed Omega inverse X i, and V one inverse beta one equals V zero inverse beta zero plus the sum over i of X i transposed Omega inverse y i star, GLM3 slide 32. Omega is inverse Wishart, with its scale updated by the sum over i of y i star minus X i beta, times its transpose. And, equation 10: y i star is distributed as the normal with mean X i beta and covariance Omega, times the indicator that the largest of its entries is negative, when y i is zero; and times the indicator that its largest entry is entry j and is at least zero, when y i is j.

That is a truncated multivariate normal, drawn one coordinate at a time from its univariate conditional, which is normal and truncated: below zero if y i is zero; above the maximum of zero and the other coordinates, for the chosen j; and below the chosen coordinate otherwise. It is the same device Greenberg uses for the multivariate probit, his equation 9.9.

Identification.
