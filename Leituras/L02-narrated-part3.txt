Advanced Econometrics, Class 2. Introduction to statistical learning. Narrated study notes, part 3 of 3: the support material.

How to listen. This file holds the support parts of the Class 2 narration. Seven parts, in order. First, the pitfalls: fifteen confusions, each said as "the error is, the correction is", with the minimal counterexample. Second, the spoken formula sheet, built for active recall: you hear a prompt, pause the player, say the result aloud, then hear the answer. Third, the implementation: the reference Python routines described as sequences of steps, never read as code. Fourth, the reading route and exercises, as addresses only. Fifth, a thirty-item true or false self-test, with a pause cue before each answer. Sixth, a glossary. Seventh, the whole class in five lines.

A reminder on scope. The final exam has no code. Each item of the specification is "derive it, compute it by hand, read it". Ridge, lasso, elastic net and every Bayesian part are Classes 3 and 4; probit and multinomial models are Class 7.


Support part one. The pitfalls.

This part covers the pitfalls note, part one. Every counterexample uses a number already derived in a section note; the few new ones are checked in the Python script.

Pitfall one. Training error against test error.
The error: believing the model with the smaller training error predicts better.
The counterexample: the tanh world of section 1. Degree 15 has expected training error 7.47 and expected test error 26.20. Degree 5 has training error 13.79 and test error 19.81. The training error ranks degree 15 above degree 5; the test error ranks them the other way. Degree 15 even trains below the noise floor of 16: it has reproduced noise.
The correction: the training error falls with every added term, because nested least squares cannot do worse in sample; the test error is U-shaped. The gap is two sigma squared times d plus one over n on average.

Pitfall two. The bias-variance of a learner against the bias of an estimator.
The error: saying "least squares is unbiased, so its bias term in the decomposition is zero."
The counterexample: fit a straight line to psi of x equal to one minus x plus 2 x squared. Least squares is unbiased, for the coefficients of the best linear approximation, two thirds plus x. The bias in the decomposition is measured against psi itself: minus 2 times x squared minus x plus one sixth, with integrated square 0.0222, a thousand times the variance. And the "variance" in the decomposition is the spread of psi hat at x across training samples, not the variance of Y, which is sigma squared.
The correction: "unbiased" always names a target. Gauss-Markov's target is the beta of the fitted model; the decomposition's target is the conditional mean of Y.

Pitfall three. Leave-one-out against k-fold: which has less bias, which less variance.
The error: saying "leave-one-out uses n minus one points and involves no random split, so it is the best estimate on every count."
The counterexample: on bias, yes, leave-one-out's fits are the closest to the full fit. On variance: its n fits share n minus 2 of their n minus 1 points, so the squared left-out errors are strongly positively correlated. In the extreme of perfect correlation, the variance of CV sub n collapses to the variance of a single squared error: averaging n terms buys nothing. Deterministic given the data does not mean precise: redraw the data and CV sub n moves.
The correction: bias falls from hold-out to 10-fold to leave-one-out; variance does not. Cost too: one fit for least squares by the shortcut, n fits for a logit.

Pitfall four. Cross-validation picks a model, not a truth.
The error: saying "10-fold cross-validation chose order 12 in 99 of 100 runs, so the motorcycle curve is a 12th-degree polynomial."
The counterexample: the stand-in data's mean is a sum of two Gaussian bumps, no polynomial at all, and cross-validation picks order 12 there too. Cross-validation ranks the candidates by estimated test error; if the truth is not among them, it still returns a winner. ISLP, page 290, warns that d above 4 gives "very strange shapes", especially at the boundary, where the order-12 band flares.
The correction: the 99 out of 100 measures the stability of the choice, not its truth.

Pitfall five. Selecting the order, then reporting its in-sample fit.
The error: saying "order 12 has an adjusted R squared of 0.7798; that is how well it will predict."
The counterexample: regress pure noise on a 12-term polynomial with n equal to 132. The expected R squared is k over n minus one, 12 over 131, which is 0.0916, although the true R squared is zero; this was checked by simulation with 4,000 draws. Pick the best of many candidates and the winner's in-sample fit is biased upward again: the adjusted R squared corrects for the parameters of one model, not for the search. The same holds for the smallest of 14 cross-validation estimates, and for the bootstrap band, which holds the order at 12 in every resample.
The correction: the data that chose the model cannot also grade it. Keep an untouched test set, or nest the cross-validation.

Pitfall six. Log points against percent.
The error: saying "log price has coefficient 0.306 on rooms: one more room, plus 30.6 per cent."
The counterexample: exactly, 100 times e to the 0.306 minus one is plus 35.8 per cent; one fewer room, 100 times e to the minus 0.306 minus one, is minus 26.4 per cent. The 30.6 is a first-order approximation, five points off here. And a percentage point is not a percent: unemployment from 8 to 9 is one point, 12.5 per cent, and log 9 minus log 8 is 0.118 log points.
The correction: use the exact reading, 100 times e to the beta delta x minus one, when beta delta x is not small.

Pitfall seven. Odds against probability.
The error: saying "an odds ratio of 1.733 means the probability rises by 73 per cent."
The counterexample: at the default cut-off, the fitted probability is 0.5 and the odds are one. 100 dollars more balance multiplies the odds by e to the 0.55, 1.733: odds 1.733, probability 1.733 over 2.733, which is 0.634, a 27 per cent rise, not 73. The two coincide only when the probability is small: on the student table the probability ratio is 4.31 over 2.92, which is 1.478, against an odds ratio of 1.499.
The correction: an odds ratio multiplies odds, not probabilities.

Pitfall eight. A logit coefficient against a marginal effect.
The error: saying "the balance coefficient is 0.0055: each dollar adds 0.0055 to the default probability."
The counterexample: the derivative of the probability is beta times pi times one minus pi: 0.001375 per dollar at the cut-off, and 0.0000315 at a balance of 1,000. That is 44 times apart, and both far below 0.0055.
The correction: the coefficient is constant on the log-odds scale only.

Pitfall nine. The 0.5 cut-off is not cost-neutral.
The error: saying "classify at 0.5; any other cut-off is arbitrary."
The counterexample: 0.5 is a cost choice. A cut-off of c one two over c one two plus c two one equal to one half means a missed defaulter costs the same as a false alarm. If a missed defaulter costs four times as much, the Bayes cut-off is one over one plus 4, which is 0.2, and ISLP's LDA tables give costs of 1031 at 0.5 against 787 at 0.2.
The correction: choose the cut-off from the costs.

Pitfall ten. Accuracy under class imbalance.
The error: saying "the rule with the lower error rate is the better classifier."
The counterexample: on ISLP's default data, "nobody defaults" has error 3.33 per cent, 333 out of 10,000, and catches no defaulter. LDA at 0.2 has error 3.73 per cent, higher, and catches 195 of 333. At a 4 to 1 cost the trivial rule costs 4 times 333, which is 1332, and the LDA rule 787.
The correction: with 3 per cent positives, the error rate is dominated by the negatives. Read the confusion matrix, or the ROC curve, not one number.

Pitfall eleven. The false discovery rate against the false positive rate.
The error: saying "the false positive rate is the share of flagged e-mails that are not spam."
The counterexample: the spam glm. The false positive rate, false positives over false positives plus true negatives, is 80 over 1405, which is 5.69 per cent: of the ham, how much was flagged. The false discovery rate, false positives over false positives plus true positives, is 80 over 883, which is 9.06 per cent: of the flagged, how much was ham. Same numerator, different denominators; the false discovery rate is higher here because fewer e-mails were flagged, 883, than were ham, 1405. The deck's column, false positives over false positives plus true positives plus false negatives, 8.20 per cent, is neither.
The correction: name the denominator.

Pitfall twelve. LDA against logistic regression.
The error: saying "LDA's posterior is a logistic function of x, so LDA and logistic regression are the same method."
The counterexample: same form, different estimator. LDA plugs Gaussian maximum likelihood estimates of the means and covariance into Bayes' theorem; the logit maximises the conditional likelihood of y. On perfectly separated data the logit maximum likelihood estimate does not exist, and IRLS runs off to infinity, while LDA returns a finite rule. With x equal to the student dummy, LDA's Gaussian assumption is plainly false, while the logit is exact, since it is saturated.
The correction: the functional form agrees; the assumptions and the estimates do not.

Pitfall thirteen. The maximum likelihood rule against the Bayes rule.
The error: saying "allocate to the class under which the observation is most likely."
The counterexample: the Bernoulli example with prior pi one equal to 0.25. The maximum likelihood rule, zero to class one and one to class two, errs with probability 0.25 times one half plus 0.75 times one quarter, which is 0.3125. Since pi one is below one third, the Bayes rule sends everything to class two and errs only 0.25 of the time.
The correction: the likelihood ratio must clear the prior odds, not one.

Pitfall fourteen. Gaussian against Poisson regression for counts.
The error: saying "on the blog data both models give the means 109 and 83.33, so it does not matter which one is fitted."
The counterexample: the means coincide because the model is saturated in two groups; the variance each model assumes does not: constant 140.4 against equal to the mean. Hence t equal to minus 5.67 against z equal to minus 6.77 for the same effect. With a continuous covariate and small counts the Gaussian line also goes negative, as in 9.6 per cent of ISLP's Bikeshare fits, page 168; the log link cannot.
The correction: the variance assumption drives the standard errors; choose the model that matches the data's variance.

Pitfall fifteen. Overdispersion, a two-sided confusion.
The error: either "a variance above the mean makes the Poisson coefficients wrong", or "Poisson standard errors are fine whatever the variance."
The counterexample: the blog data have variance to mean ratios of 1.36 and 1.57, and a dispersion estimate of 1.444. Quasi-Poisson leaves beta hat, minus 0.26850, unchanged, because the mean model is still right and the score equations are the same, but multiplies the standard error by sigma hat, 1.202: z falls from minus 6.77 to minus 5.63.
The correction: point estimates survive overdispersion; the precision was overstated.

Section 1, prediction error and bias-variance.

Prompt: the expected prediction error, equation 1. Pause. Answer: the expected value of Y minus phi of X, squared.

Prompt: the pointwise problem and its solution, equations 2 to 5. Pause. Answer: condition on X; minimise g of t, the conditional expected value of Y minus t squared; g prime of t is zero if and only if t is the conditional mean of Y given X equal to x; g double prime is 2, positive.

Prompt: the best predictor, equation 6. Pause. Answer: the function that minimises the expected squared prediction error is the regression function psi of x, the expected value of Y given X equal to x.

Prompt: the decomposition of the prediction risk, equations 7 to 9. Pause. Answer: the prediction risk of g equals the regression risk of g plus the expected conditional variance of Y given X; predicting Y and estimating psi are one problem.

Prompt: the additive model, equation 10. Pause. Answer: Y equals psi of X plus epsilon, with mean zero and variance sigma squared, all mutually independent.

Prompt: reducible and irreducible error, equation 15. Pause. Answer: the conditional expected squared error at x equals the EQM of psi hat at x plus sigma squared.

Prompt: the definitions of bias and variance, equation 17. Pause. Answer: the bias is the expected value of psi hat of x minus psi of x; the variance is the expected value of psi hat of x minus its expectation, squared, both over training samples.

Prompt: the split of the EQM, equation 20. Pause. Answer: the EQM equals the variance plus the squared bias.

Prompt: the full decomposition, equation 21. Pause. Answer: the expected squared prediction error equals the integrated squared bias plus the integrated variance plus sigma squared.

Prompt: the line on the quadratic, equations 23 and 24. Pause. Answer: the integrated squared bias is one over 45; the average variance is sigma squared times 2 over n prime, with the design fixed.

Prompt: the tanh world, equation 25. Pause. Answer: psi of x equals 45 times the hyperbolic tangent of x over 1.9 minus 7, plus 57; X uniform on 8 to 18; sigma equal to 4; n equal to 30.

Prompt: the linear smoother's moments, equation 28. Pause. Answer: the mean of psi hat at x zero is b of x zero transposed times B transposed B inverse times B transposed times psi; the variance is sigma squared times b of x zero transposed times B transposed B inverse times b of x zero.

Prompt: the training error, equation 29. Pause. Answer: one over n times the sum of y i minus psi hat of x i, squared.

Prompt: the expected training error and the optimism, equations 32 and 33, graduate layer. Pause. Answer: the expected training error is the squared length of I minus H times psi over n, plus sigma squared times one minus d plus one over n; the optimism is two sigma squared times d plus one over n.

Section 2, coefficient readings.

Prompt: level-level, equation 1. Pause. Answer: delta y equals beta j delta x j; rescaling x by c divides the slope by c.

Prompt: the adjusted R squared, equation 4. Pause. Answer: one minus one minus R squared, times n minus one over n minus k minus one.

Prompt: the interaction slope, equation 6. Pause. Answer: delta y over delta x two equals beta two plus beta three x one; beta two alone is the slope at x one equal to zero.

Prompt: a dummy times a continuous variable, equation 8. Pause. Answer: two slopes, beta two and beta two plus beta three; the gap is beta one plus beta three times years.

Prompt: the exact log-level change, equation 11. Pause. Answer: the percentage change of y equals 100 times e to the beta one delta x, minus one.

Prompt: the approximate log-level change, equation 12. Pause. Answer: 100 times beta one times delta x, with error about 100 z squared over two.

Prompt: log-log, equations 13 to 15. Pause. Answer: beta one is the elasticity; the percentage change of y is approximately beta one times the percentage change of x; exactly, 100 times the quantity one plus the percentage change of x over 100, to the beta one, minus one.

Prompt: level-log, from Wooldridge Table 2.3. Pause. Answer: delta y equals beta one over 100 times the percentage change of x.

Prompt: a standardised coefficient, equation 18. Pause. Answer: the derivative of log y in x j is beta j star times s y over s x j.

Prompt: the quadratic slope and turning point, equations 21 and 22. Pause. Answer: beta one hat plus 2 beta two hat x; the turning point is the absolute value of beta one hat over 2 beta two hat.

Section 3, validation, cross-validation and the bootstrap.

Prompt: why polynomial regression is linear, equation 1, and the standardisation fact, equation 2. Pause. Answer: it is linear in beta; standardising a x plus b, with a positive, returns the standardised x.

Prompt: the adjusted R squared and BIC by order, equation 3. Pause. Answer: one minus one minus R squared times n minus one over n minus d minus one; and BIC equals one over n times RSS plus log n times d plus one times sigma hat squared.

Prompt: the validation-set estimate, equation 5. Pause. Answer: the average over the validation points of y i minus the training fit at x i, squared.

Prompt: leave-one-out, equation 6. Pause. Answer: CV sub n equals one over n times the sum of y i minus the left-out prediction, squared.

Prompt: the shortcut, equation 7. Pause. Answer: for least squares, CV sub n equals one over n times the sum of the square of y i minus y i hat over one minus h i i.

Prompt: dropping row i, and Sherman-Morrison, equations 8 and 9. Pause. Answer: X transposed X becomes A minus u u transposed; its inverse is A inverse plus A inverse u u transposed A inverse over one minus h i i.

Prompt: the left-out estimate and residual, equations 12 and 13. Pause. Answer: beta hat with i removed equals beta hat minus A inverse x i e i over one minus h i i; the left-out residual is e i over one minus h i i.

Prompt: the variance of leave-one-out, equation 14. Pause. Answer: n variances plus n times n minus one large positive covariances, all over n squared.

Prompt: k-fold, equations 15 and 16. Pause. Answer: CV sub k is the average of the fold errors; the weighted version is the sum of n sub j over n times the fold error, equal only when all folds have n over k points.

Prompt: the bootstrap standard error, equation 17. Pause. Answer: the square root of one over B minus one times the sum of the squared deviations of the resampled curves at x from their average.

Prompt: the formula band, equation 18. Pause. Answer: the fit at x zero plus or minus two times the square root of l zero transposed C hat l zero, with C hat equal to sigma hat squared times X transposed X inverse.

Section 4, logistic regression.

Prompt: the model, equation 1. Pause. Answer: pi i equals e to the x i transposed beta over one plus e to the x i transposed beta.

Prompt: the complement, the odds and the logit, equations 2 to 4. Pause. Answer: one minus pi i is one over one plus e to the eta; the odds are e to the eta; the logit is x i transposed beta.

Prompt: the generalized linear model reading, equation 5. Pause. Answer: the mean is pi i, the variance pi i times one minus pi i, and the link is the logit.

Prompt: the odds ratio, equation 6. Pause. Answer: e to the beta j per unit of x j, the same at every x.

Prompt: the derivative of g and the partial effect, equations 7 and 8. Pause. Answer: g prime equals g times one minus g; the partial effect is beta j times pi i times one minus pi i, at most beta j over four.

Prompt: the log-likelihood, equations 9 to 11. Pause. Answer: the sum of y i x i transposed beta minus the log of one plus e to the x i transposed beta.

Prompt: the score, equation 12. Pause. Answer: X transposed times y minus pi; the intercept row makes the mean fitted probability equal the share of ones.

Prompt: the Hessian, equation 13. Pause. Answer: minus X transposed W X, with W the diagonal of pi i times one minus pi i; strictly concave.

Prompt: the Newton-Raphson step, equations 14 and 15. Pause. Answer: beta new equals beta old plus X transposed W X inverse times X transposed times y minus pi.

Prompt: IRLS, equations 16 and 17. Pause. Answer: beta new equals X transposed W X inverse times X transposed W z, with z i equal to eta i plus y i minus pi i over pi i times one minus pi i.

Prompt: the covariate cut-off, equation 19. Pause. Answer: x star equals the logit of c minus beta zero hat, over beta one hat.

Prompt: the likelihood-ratio test and McFadden's pseudo R squared. Pause. Answer: the difference of deviances, referred to a chi-squared; and one minus the unrestricted log-likelihood over the null log-likelihood.

Prompt: plug-in against posterior predictive, equations 20 and 21. Pause. Answer: the plug-in uses beta hat; the predictive integrates the probability over the posterior of beta, Classes 3 and 4.

Prompt: the deviance for zero-one data, equations 22 and 23. Pause. Answer: the sum of squared deviance residuals, which equals minus twice the log-likelihood at beta hat.

Prompt: admissibility. Pause. Answer: all Bayes rules, the maximum likelihood rule included, are admissible.

Prompt: risk and Bayes risk, equation 13. Pause. Answer: the risk for class k is the sum of c j k p j k; the Bayes risk is the prior-weighted sum of the risks.

Prompt: the cost threshold, equation 14. Pause. Answer: allocate to one if and only if h exceeds the log of pi two c one two over pi one c two one.

Prompt: the posterior cut-off, equation 15. Pause. Answer: the posterior of class one exceeds c one two over c one two plus c two one.

Prompt: the equal-variance AUC, graduate layer. Pause. Answer: Phi of mu two minus mu one over the square root of two sigma squared.

Support part three. The implementation, described.

This part covers the Python note, which presents the runnable script Notas, L02, L02 python checks. It uses numpy, scipy and statsmodels only, no scikit-learn. The lecturer's R is the reference, never the code. The script runs in about ten seconds, prints one line per section, and ends with "ALL L02 CHECKS PASSED"; the first disagreement aborts with an assertion error naming the quantity and the printed value. The code is not read here; each routine is described by its inputs, its steps, and its trap.

What is reused rather than rewritten. The figure script supplies the tanh function, the exact polynomial bias-variance routine on an orthogonal basis, the motorcycle stand-in, the design and least squares helpers, the two leave-one-out routines, the site-layout k-fold, the Gaussian discriminant roots, and the 28 visitor counts; importing it draws no figure. The companion check script recomputes the two interactive pages against their own JavaScript; it is cited, not imported, because it checks the pages while this script checks the notes. What is new, and why: the IRLS routine, since no existing code fits a generalized linear model by hand; k-fold for an arbitrary fold list with both the plain and the weighted average; the confusion-matrix rates; and the assertions against the section notes' tables. Support part five. Self-test: thirty true or false items.

This part covers the original self-test of the pitfalls note. Scoring follows the course rule: the maximum of zero and the number right minus half the number wrong, per block. Difficulty is marked easy, medium or hard. Numbers marked illustrative are invented for the item; all others come from the notes. After each statement, pause the player, decide true or false and say why, then resume for the answer.

Block A, prediction error and bias-variance.

Item 1, easy. Under squared loss, among all functions of X, the conditional mean of Y given X has the smallest expected prediction error, whatever the joint distribution of X and Y, with finite second moments. Pause.
Answer: true. Conditioning on X turns the problem into minimising g of t, the conditional expected value of Y minus t squared, separately at each x; g prime of t, minus 2 times the conditional mean plus 2 t, is zero at the conditional mean, and g double prime is 2, positive. No distributional assumption enters beyond the existence of the moments. Under absolute loss the answer would be the conditional median instead.

Item 2, medium. A straight line fitted by least squares to a curved regression function contributes no squared-bias term to the decomposition, because least squares is unbiased. Pause.
Answer: false. Least squares is unbiased for the coefficients of the best linear approximation, not for psi of x. On psi of x equal to one minus x plus 2 x squared, with x uniform on zero to one, the line's bias is minus 2 times x squared minus x plus one sixth, and its integrated square is one over 45, 0.0222, more than twice the noise variance, 0.01.

Item 3, medium. If method A has a lower expected training error than method B, A also has a lower expected test error. Pause.
Answer: false. In the tanh world the degree-15 polynomial trains at 7.47 against 13.79 for degree 5, yet its test error is 26.20 against 19.81. The training error rewards variance; the test error charges for it.

Item 4, medium. In the decomposition, the variance term measures how much Y varies around psi of X. Pause.
Answer: false. That is the irreducible term, sigma squared, random over the new epsilon. The variance term is the variance of psi hat at x, random over the training sample: how much the learned curve moves when the data are redrawn. A perfectly noise-free world, with sigma equal to zero, can still have a positive variance term if the design is random.

Item 5, hard. For least squares with d plus one coefficients on a fixed design and homoskedastic noise, the expected training error understates the expected error on new responses at the same design points by exactly two sigma squared times d plus one over n, whatever the true regression function. Pause.
Answer: true. The residual vector is I minus H times psi plus epsilon; the psi part, the squared length of I minus H times psi over n, appears in both expectations and cancels, leaving sigma squared times d plus one over n plus sigma squared minus sigma squared times one minus d plus one over n, which is two sigma squared times d plus one over n. At degree 5, n equal to 30 and sigma equal to 4: 6.4. This is the penalty C p and AIC add back.

Item 6, medium. Doubling the training sample lowers the irreducible error. Pause.
Answer: false. Sigma squared is the variance of the new noise, which no sample size touches; the floor of the tanh curves stays at 16 for every n. A larger n lowers the variance term, roughly in proportion to d plus one over n for polynomial least squares, which is what moves the next item's optimum.

Item 7, medium. In the tanh world with sigma equal to 4, raising the training size from 30 to 200 cannot lower the test-optimal polynomial degree. Pause.
Answer: true. A larger n shrinks every degree's variance while leaving the squared bias nearly unchanged, so flexibility gets cheaper. The exact computation gives an optimum of degree 5 at n equal to 30 and degree 7 at n equal to 60 and at n equal to 200. The Flexibility Dial shows the optimum sliding right as n grows.

Block B, validation and cross-validation.

Item 8, medium. Although leave-one-out involves no random split, it can have a larger variance as an estimator of the test error than 10-fold cross-validation. Pause.
Answer: true. Given the data, leave-one-out is deterministic, but as a function of a random sample it has a sampling variance, and its n nearly identical fits make the squared left-out errors strongly positively correlated, so the covariance term dominates. 10-fold's fits overlap less.

Item 9, easy. For a least squares fit, leave-one-out can be computed from a single fit on all n points. Pause.
Answer: true. CV sub n equals one over n times the sum of e i over one minus h i i, squared, derived by Sherman-Morrison: the left-out residual is the ordinary residual inflated by one over one minus h i i.

Item 10, medium. The same one-fit shortcut gives the exact leave-one-out of a logistic regression fitted by IRLS. Pause.
Answer: false. The derivation uses that beta hat is linear in y through X transposed X inverse times X transposed y; a logit's beta hat is the fixed point of a re-weighted iteration whose weights change when a point leaves. Leave-one-out for the logit, or for LDA, needs n refits, ISLP page 205.

Item 11, medium. An 80 to 20 hold-out estimate tends to overstate the test error of the model refitted on all n observations. Pause.
Answer: true. It is computed from a fit on about 80 per cent of the data, 105 of 132 points for the motorcycle, and a fit on fewer points is noisier, so its error is higher than that of the full-sample fit, ISLP page 208.

Item 12, medium. A fintech's analyst picks the polynomial degree that maximises the adjusted R squared among degrees 1 to 20 and reports that adjusted R squared as the model's out-of-sample fit; since the adjusted R squared penalises the number of parameters, the report is unbiased. Pause.
Answer: false. The penalty corrects for the parameters of one fixed model, not for choosing the best of twenty on the same data. Under pure noise a 12-term polynomial with 132 points already has an expected R squared of 12 over 131, 0.0916; taking the maximum over candidates pushes the reported figure up further. An untouched test set or nested cross-validation is needed.

Item 13, easy. Since 10-fold cross-validation picked order 12 in 99 of 100 repetitions, the true regression function of the motorcycle data is a polynomial of degree 12. Pause.
Answer: false. Cross-validation ranks the candidates by estimated test error; it says nothing about whether any candidate is the truth. On the stand-in data, whose mean is a sum of Gaussian bumps, it also picks 12. The 99 out of 100 measures the stability of the choice, not its truth.

Item 14, hard. The pairs-bootstrap band drawn around the order-12 fit includes the uncertainty of having chosen the order from the same data. Pause.
Answer: false. Every resample refits order 12; the selection step is outside the loop. Putting it inside, by re-running the cross-validation in each resample, would widen the band.

Item 15, medium. On data whose noise variance changes with x, the pairs-bootstrap band and the formula band, the fit plus or minus two times the square root of l zero transposed C hat l zero, will generally differ. Pause.
Answer: true. The formula uses one sigma hat squared for every x; resampling pairs carries the quiet and the noisy regions in their own proportions. On the stand-in the bootstrap band is wider in the noisy region; on homoskedastic data the two agree to within 10 per cent.

Block C, logistic regression and classification metrics.

Item 16, easy. In a logit, e to the beta j is the factor that multiplies the odds when x j rises by one unit, the same at every value of the covariates. Pause.
Answer: true. The odds are e to the eta; raising x j by one adds beta j to eta, so the odds go from e to the eta to e to the eta plus beta j, a ratio of e to the beta j whatever eta was.

Item 17, medium. In a logit, beta j is the change in the probability that y equals one per unit of x j. Pause.
Answer: false. The probability effect is beta j times pi times one minus pi, largest, beta j over four, at pi equal to one half. For default, 0.001375 per dollar at the cut-off and 0.0000315 at a balance of 1,000, neither equal to 0.0055.

Item 18, medium. A positive coefficient on student alone, 0.405, and a negative one given balance, minus 0.715, can both be correct. Pause.
Answer: true. They answer different questions: students default more overall because they carry larger balances; at the same balance they default less. Balance confounds the marginal comparison; the sign flip is evidence of confounding, not of an error.

Item 19, hard. At the maximum likelihood estimate of a logit that includes an intercept, the average fitted probability equals the sample share of ones. Pause.
Answer: true. The score is X transposed times y minus pi; its intercept row is the sum of y i minus pi i hat equal to zero, so the average fitted probability equals the sample mean of y, 0.0333 in default. It holds for any canonical-link generalized linear model with an intercept; the Poisson version says the fitted total equals the observed total.

Item 20, medium. Lowering the probability cut-off of a credit model from 0.5 to 0.2 lowers its overall error rate. Pause.
Answer: false. ISLP's LDA on default: error 2.75 per cent at 0.5 and 3.73 per cent at 0.2; 212 more false alarms buy 114 fewer missed defaulters. The lower cut-off is right when a missed defaulter costs about four times a false alarm, cost 1031 against 787, not because errors fall.

Item 21, medium. A digital bank in São Paulo has 3.33 per cent defaulters in its portfolio. A rule that predicts "no default" for every client has a lower error rate, 3.33 per cent, than an LDA rule with cut-off 0.2, 3.73 per cent, so the trivial rule is the better credit tool. Pause.
Answer: false. The trivial rule catches none of the defaulters; at any cost ratio above the one that makes the two equal, it loses; at 4 to 1, 4 times 333, which is 1332, against 787. With 3 per cent positives the error rate is dominated by the negatives and cannot rank credit rules.

Item 22, medium. For the spam glm, the false discovery rate exceeds the false positive rate because fewer e-mails were flagged as spam than were actually ham. Pause.
Answer: true. Both have numerator 80 false positives; the false discovery rate divides by the flagged, 883, the false positive rate by the ham, 1405. The false discovery rate is higher exactly when 883 is below 1405: 9.06 against 5.69 per cent.

Item 23, hard. For ungrouped zero-one data, a residual deviance of 1596.5 on 9998 degrees of freedom shows a good fit, because it is far below its chi-squared expectation. Pause.
Answer: false. With one observation per covariate pattern the deviance does not have a chi-squared reference distribution with n minus p degrees of freedom; it equals minus twice the log-likelihood and depends on the fitted probabilities alone. Only differences of deviances between nested models are safely referred to a chi-squared, for example 1596.5 minus 1571.7, which is 24.8, on one degree of freedom for student.

Block D, discriminant analysis.

Item 24, easy. With equal priors and equal misallocation costs, the Bayes discriminant rule coincides with the maximum likelihood rule. Pause.
Answer: true. The cost threshold, the log of pi two c one two over pi one c two one, is the log of one, which is zero: the maximum likelihood rule, h of x positive.

Item 25, medium. Two univariate Gaussian classes with unequal variances can produce a Bayes rule that allocates both tails of the real line to the same class. Pause.
Answer: true. h is then quadratic; in the deck's example, means 1 and 5 and standard deviations 6 and 2.5, it is a U with roots 1.680 and 10.001, so class one, the wider one, takes everything below 1.680 and everything above 10.001. A single midpoint arises only when the standard deviations are equal.

Item 26, medium. In that example, an observation at x equal to 12 is allocated to class two because it is closer to 5 than to 1. Pause.
Answer: false. h of 12 is 0.066111 times 144, minus 0.772222 times 12, plus 1.110642, which is 1.364, positive: class one. Far from both means the density with the fatter tails is the larger one; distance to the mean decides only when the variances are equal.

Item 27, hard. A public hospital in Recife screens for a disease, class one, with the deck's two Gaussians and equal priors, an illustrative setting. Raising the cost of a missed case far enough sends every patient to class one. Pause.
Answer: true. The threshold, the log of c one two over c two one, falls as c two one rises; once the ratio c two one over c one two exceeds e to the 1.144, which is 3.14, it sits below the minimum of h, minus 1.144, so h exceeds it everywhere and the class-two region is empty.

Item 28, medium. Since every Bayes rule is admissible, an admissible rule is a sensible rule. Pause.
Answer: false; the reciprocal fails. "Allocate everything to class two" is admissible in the Bernoulli example, being the Bayes rule for pi one below one third, but it ignores the data. Admissibility only excludes rules beaten everywhere, such as the reversed rule with p one one equal to one half and p two two equal to one quarter.

Block E, counts.

Item 29, medium. Because the Gaussian and Poisson regressions on the weekend dummy give the same fitted means, 109 and 83.33, they attach the same statistical significance to the weekend effect. Pause.
Answer: false. Same means, different variance functions: constant s squared of 140.4 against a variance equal to lambda. The Gaussian t is minus 5.67, the Poisson z is minus 6.77, and the quasi-Poisson z is minus 5.63.

Item 30, medium. Overdispersion, a variance above the mean, makes the Poisson estimate of the weekend coefficient inconsistent. Pause.
Answer: false. The score equations, X transposed times y minus lambda equal to zero, only need the mean to be right; with a dispersion of 1.444 the quasi-Poisson fit returns the same beta hat, minus 0.26850. What overdispersion breaks is the standard error, too small by the factor sigma hat, 1.202.

Support part six. Glossary.

Supervised learning: learning a map from predictors X to a response Y from labelled pairs; regression for a quantitative Y, classification for a label.
Prediction against inference: using the learned function as a black box for new cases, against reading it to learn which predictors matter and how.
Regression function: psi of x, the conditional mean of Y given X equal to x, the best predictor under squared loss.
Expected prediction error, or test error: the expected squared distance between a new Y and its prediction.
Training error: the average squared residual on the data used to fit the model; it falls with every added term.
Irreducible error: sigma squared, the variance of the noise, a floor no method can cross.
Reducible error: the mean squared error of the learned function at x, which the choice of method moves.
Bias of a learner: the gap between the average learned curve and the regression function at x.
Variance of a learner: the spread of the learned curve at x across training samples.
Bias-variance trade-off: more flexibility lowers bias and raises variance, so the test error is U-shaped.
Optimism: the expected gap between test and training error, two sigma squared times d plus one over n for least squares.
Degrees of freedom of a smoother: its effective number of parameters; d plus one for a degree-d polynomial.
Semi-elasticity: 100 beta in a log-level model, the approximate percentage change in y per unit of x.
Elasticity: beta in a log-log model, the percentage change in y per one per cent change in x.
Interaction: a product term that makes the slope of one variable depend on another.
Validation set, or hold-out: a part of the data set aside to estimate the test error of a model fitted on the rest.
Leave-one-out cross-validation: fit n times, each time leaving one point out and predicting it.
Leverage: h i i, the diagonal of the hat matrix, how much a point pulls its own fit.
Sherman-Morrison: the formula for the inverse of a matrix after a rank-one update, which yields the leave-one-out shortcut.
k-fold cross-validation: split into k folds, fit without each fold in turn, and average the fold errors.
Pairs bootstrap: resample observation pairs with replacement and refit, to get a band around a fitted curve.
Percentile band: the pointwise 2.5 and 97.5 per cent quantiles of the bootstrap curves.
Heteroskedasticity: noise variance that changes with x.
Linear probability model: least squares on a zero-one response, which can give probabilities outside zero to one.
Logistic function: e to the eta over one plus e to the eta, mapping any real number into zero to one.
Odds: pi over one minus pi.
Logit: the log of the odds, linear in x in logistic regression.
Odds ratio: e to the beta j, the factor that multiplies the odds per unit of x j.
Partial effect: beta j times pi times one minus pi, the change in probability per unit of x j.
Link function: the transformation of the mean that is linear in the predictors.
Canonical link: the link that makes the score equal X transposed times the data minus the fitted mean.
Iteratively reweighted least squares: Newton-Raphson for a generalized linear model, written as repeated weighted least squares on an adjusted response.
Separation: a linear combination of x that splits the zeros from the ones perfectly, so no finite logit estimate exists.
Deviance: twice the gap in log-likelihood to a saturated model; minus twice the log-likelihood for zero-one data.
McFadden pseudo R squared: one minus the model log-likelihood over the null log-likelihood.
Confounding: a third variable that drives both a predictor and the outcome and can flip a marginal association.
Confusion matrix: the two by two table of true and predicted classes.
Sensitivity: the true positive rate, true positives over actual positives.
Specificity: the true negative rate, true negatives over actual negatives.
Precision: true positives over predicted positives.
False discovery rate: false positives over predicted positives.
Discriminant analysis: classification by modelling the distribution of x within each class and inverting with Bayes' theorem.
Bayes rule: allocate to the class with the largest prior times density; it minimises the probability of error.
Maximum likelihood discriminant rule: the Bayes rule with equal priors.
Discriminant function: h of x, the log density under class one minus that under class two.
Linear discriminant analysis: Gaussian classes with a common covariance, giving a linear boundary.
Mahalanobis distance: the distance from x to a class mean measured through the inverse covariance.
Quadratic discriminant analysis: Gaussian classes with their own covariances, giving a quadratic boundary.
Misallocation probability: p i j, the probability that the region of class i catches an individual from class j.
Admissible rule: a rule that no other rule beats in every class.
Bayes risk: the prior-weighted expected cost of a rule.
ROC curve: the false positive rate against the true positive rate as the threshold sweeps.
AUC: the area under the ROC curve, the probability that a random positive scores above a random negative.
Poisson regression: a count model with a log link, log lambda equal to x transposed beta.
Rate ratio: e to the beta j, the factor that multiplies the expected count per unit of x j.
Overdispersion: a count variance larger than the mean.
Quasi-Poisson: the Poisson fit with standard errors multiplied by the estimated dispersion sigma hat.
Generalized linear model: a model in which a link of the conditional mean is linear in the predictors, with a variance tied to the mean.


Support part seven. The whole class in five lines.

One. Under squared loss the best predictor is the conditional mean, and every learned estimate pays squared bias, variance and noise; in the tanh world the optimum is degree 5 with test error 19.81 against a floor of 16.

Two. The training error only falls and is optimistic by two sigma squared times d plus one over n, so estimate the test error by cross-validation: one hold-out split picks the motorcycle order 12 only 63 times in 100, 10-fold 99 times, and leave-one-out costs one fit through the leverages.

Three. Read coefficients on their own scale: 100 beta is only approximate, 35.8 per cent and not 30.6 at 0.306; a logit coefficient is an odds ratio, not a probability effect; and a Poisson coefficient is a rate ratio, 0.7645 for the blog weekend.

Four. Classification is prediction with zero-one loss: logistic regression fits the posterior directly by IRLS, discriminant analysis builds it from class densities, and unequal Gaussian variances give a quadratic rule with two cut-offs, 1.680 and 10.001.

Five. The cut-off is a statement about costs, not a constant one half; judge a classifier by its confusion matrix and ROC curve, never by one error rate; and always check a Poisson model's variance against its mean.

This is the end of the Class 2 narration.
