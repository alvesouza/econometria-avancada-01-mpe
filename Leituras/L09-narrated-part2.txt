The claim, before the derivations. The Minnesota prior puts a normal prior with mean beta i zero and covariance V i zero on each equation, centred on a random walk: own first lag one, everything else zero. The prior standard deviation is lambda over k on own lag k, and theta lambda sigma i over k sigma j on variable j's lag k. With Sigma diagonal and known, the posterior is equation-by-equation Gaussian: V i bar equals the inverse of V i zero inverse plus X transposed X over sigma i squared, and beta i bar equals V i bar times V i zero inverse beta i zero plus X transposed y superscript i over sigma i squared. That is a precision-weighted average of the prior centre and OLS. Lambda is the dial: lambda going to zero returns the random walk, and lambda going to infinity returns OLS. Large VARs work because lambda is tightened as the number of variables grows, as Bańbura and coauthors show.

The notation. Beta i holds the coefficients of equation i, the constant and then the lags, row i of mu and B; and beta stacks beta one to beta q, slide 22. Beta i zero and V i zero are the prior mean and the diagonal prior covariance of beta i. Lambda, positive, is the overall tightness. Theta, between zero and one, is the relative tightness of other variables' lags. k is the lag index, from one to p. Sigma i squared is the error variance of equation i, fixed and known, with Sigma diagonal, slide 24. X is the common regressor matrix and y superscript i equation i's response vector. Beta i bar and V i bar are the posterior mean and covariance. And gamma j, zero or one, is the inclusion indicator of coefficient j in SSVS.

Four point one. The intuition: why shrink.

Part 2, slide 3: "VAR flexibility, over-parameterization, poor forecasts. First solution: Minnesota prior of Doan et al. (1984)." The count is in section 1, point eight: Kadiyala and Karlsson's 7-variable VAR 6 has 43 coefficients per equation and 133 observations. OLS is unbiased and useless. A prior that says "most of these are small, and distant lags are smaller" trades a little bias for a lot of variance: the mean squared error trade of Class 1 and the bias and variance logic of Class 2, with the prior as the regulariser.

Four point two. Course layer: the Minnesota prior. Slides 23 and 24.

Litterman's prior, 1980 and 1986, slide 23: beta i is normal with mean beta i zero and covariance V i zero, "with V i zero chosen to center the individual equations around the random walk with drift". Equation 1: y t i equals mu i plus y t minus one, i, plus u t i.

The prior mean. The coefficient on the own first lag is one; all other lag coefficients are zero; the constant gets a flat, very large variance, prior.

The prior variances, slide 24, with V i zero diagonal. Equation 2: the variance of B k of i i, an own lag, is lambda squared over k squared. The variance of B k of i j, for j not equal to i, is theta squared lambda squared over k squared, times sigma i squared over sigma j squared.

Read each factor. Lambda: how far any coefficient may stray from the random walk. One over k squared: "shrinking the number of lags p towards one"; the prior standard deviation falls like one over k. Theta below one: "own lags should explain more of the variation than the lags of other variables". Sigma i squared over sigma j squared: units; B k of i j converts y j into y i, so its scale is sigma i over sigma j.

"Shrinking the diagonal elements of B one toward one, shrinking the remaining coefficients of B one to B p toward zero", slide 23. In practice sigma i squared is replaced by the residual variance of a univariate autoregression of order p for series i, Litterman's convention, which is what "fixed and known" amounts to.

The two limits in lambda. By equation 2, V i zero is proportional to lambda squared, entry by entry, except for the constant. As lambda goes to zero, V i zero inverse goes to infinity, V i bar goes to V i zero and then to zero, and beta i bar goes to beta i zero: the random walk of equation 1. As lambda goes to infinity, V i zero inverse goes to zero, V i bar goes to sigma i squared times the inverse of X transposed X, and beta i bar goes to beta i hat: OLS, with the frequentist covariance.

The scalar case shows the weight. Take one regressor with the sum of x t squared equal to s x x, and a normal prior with mean b zero and variance v zero. Equation 9: b bar equals v zero inverse b zero plus s x x over sigma squared times b hat, over v zero inverse plus s x x over sigma squared. That is w b zero plus one minus w, times b hat, with w equal to sigma squared over s x x, over sigma squared over s x x plus v zero, which is the variance of b hat over the variance of b hat plus v zero.

The prior wins in proportion to how noisy OLS is relative to the prior variance. Distant lags, where v zero equals lambda squared over k squared is small, and short samples, where s x x is small, shrink most.

Four point four. Course layer: other priors on B and Sigma. Slides 25 and 26.

Kadiyala and Karlsson, 1993 and 1997, extend the Minnesota prior, which fixes Sigma, slide 25. Slide 26 uses Gamma for the coefficient matrix, Z for the regressors, Psi for the error covariance, and m for the number of series. Five priors, each with its posterior.

Minnesota: gamma i is normal, with Psi fixed and diagonal. The posterior of gamma i is normal: equation 7.

Diffuse, or Jeffreys: the prior is proportional to the determinant of Psi to the minus m plus one over two. The posterior of Gamma is matric-variate t, with parameters Z transposed Z, the residual cross-product matrix of the OLS fit, Gamma hat, and T minus k degrees of freedom.

Normal-Wishart: gamma given Psi is normal with covariance Psi Kronecker Omega tilde, and Psi is inverse Wishart. The posterior of Gamma is matric-variate t with T plus alpha degrees of freedom.

Normal-Diffuse: gamma is normal with its own covariance, and the prior on Psi is the diffuse one. There is no closed form; the kernel is on slide 26, and one uses Gibbs sampling.

Extended Natural Conjugate: on slide 26.

The diffuse case, derived, because it is the site's band recipe. Multiply the likelihood, equations 6 and 10 of section 1, by the determinant of Sigma to the minus q plus one over two. Equation 10: the posterior of B and Sigma is proportional to the determinant of Sigma to the minus n plus q plus one over two, times the exponential of minus one half the trace of Sigma inverse S of B hat, times the exponential of minus one half the trace of Sigma inverse times B hat minus B, times X transposed X, times B hat minus B transposed.

For fixed Sigma, the second exponential is the matrix-normal kernel. Equation 11: the vec of B transposed, given Sigma and Y, is normal with mean the vec of B hat transposed and covariance Sigma Kronecker the inverse of X transposed X. Its normalising constant is proportional to the determinant of Sigma to the k over two, with k the number of regressors per equation. Integrating B out of equation 10 leaves the determinant of Sigma to the minus n minus k plus q plus one over two, times the exponential of minus one half the trace of Sigma inverse S of B hat. So, equation 12: Sigma given Y is inverse Wishart with scale S of B hat and n minus k degrees of freedom.

Drawing Sigma from equation 12 and then B from equation 11 is exact posterior simulation. The site's VAR example bands fix Sigma at Sigma hat and draw only from equation 11. Part 2 slides 8 and 9 give the same pair as full conditionals: beta given Sigma and y is normal with mean mu one and covariance V one, with V one inverse equal to V zero inverse plus the sum over t of z tilde t transposed Sigma inverse z tilde t; and Sigma given beta and y is inverse Wishart. Greenberg's SUR Gibbs sampler, Algorithm 9.1, page 137, is the general version with equation-specific regressors.

Why Normal-Wishart cannot be Minnesota. In Psi Kronecker Omega tilde, the prior covariance of equation i's coefficients is psi i i times Omega tilde: the same relative tightness across regressors in every equation. Minnesota's theta below one makes "own" and "other" depend on the equation, which a Kronecker product cannot do, so Normal-Wishart imposes theta equal to one. Kadiyala and Karlsson's verdict, slide 30: "our preferred choice is the Normal-Wishart when the prior beliefs are of the Litterman type", and "for more general prior beliefs the Normal-Diffuse and EN priors are strong alternatives".

The Kadiyala and Karlsson example, slides 27 to 29: Litterman's seven United States series, real GNP growth, inflation, unemployment, M1, investment, the commercial paper rate and the change in business inventories, quarterly from 1948 first quarter to 1981 first quarter, out of sample from 1980 second quarter to 1986 fourth quarter; q equal to 7, p equal to 6, 301 parameters. The RMSE comparison on slide 29 is a figure.

SSVS, slides 11 and 12; George and McCulloch, 1993 and 1997; Jochmann and coauthors, 2011. Instead of an exact zero, a spike-and-slab prior. Equation 14: beta j given gamma j is a mixture, one minus gamma j times a normal with mean zero and variance tau zero j squared, plus gamma j times a normal with mean zero and variance tau one j squared; and gamma j is Bernoulli with probability q j. Tau zero j squared is small, the "coefficient is virtually zero"; tau one j squared is large, "relatively non-informative". The default semi-automatic choice, George and coauthors 2008, slide 12: tau k j equals c k times the estimated variance of beta j from an unrestricted VAR, with c zero much smaller than c one; Jochmann and coauthors set c zero equal to 0.1 and c one equal to 10.

Here gamma j given beta j needs only the two densities. By Bayes, equation 15: the probability that gamma j equals one given beta j equals q j times the normal density of beta j with variance tau one j squared, over the same quantity plus one minus q j times the normal density with variance tau zero j squared.

Worked. Take q j equal to one half, and read the taus as c zero equal to 0.1 and c one equal to 10 times a coefficient's OLS standard error s j. Write z equal to beta j over s j. With the normal density of beta with standard deviation c s equal to one over c s times the standard normal density of z over c, the s cancels in equation 15. The probability becomes 0.1 times phi of z over 10, over 0.1 times phi of z over 10 plus 10 times phi of 10 z, where phi is the standard normal density.

At z equal to 0.2: 0.1 times phi of 0.02 is 0.1 times 0.39886, which is 0.039886; 10 times phi of 2 is 10 times 0.053991, which is 0.53991. So the probability is 0.039886 over 0.579796, which is 0.069. At z equal to 0.5: 10 times phi of 5 is 10 times 1.4867 times 10 to the minus 6, which is 1.4867 times 10 to the minus 5. So the probability is 0.039844 over 0.039844 plus 0.0000149, which is 0.9996.

The two densities cross where z squared over two times c zero to the minus 2 minus c one to the minus 2 equals the log of c one over c zero. So z star equals the square root of two log of c one over c zero, over c zero to the minus 2 minus c one to the minus 2. That is the square root of two log 100 over 100 minus 0.01, which is the square root of 9.2103 over 99.99, which is 0.3035.

A draw of beta j beyond 0.30 OLS standard errors is classified "in" almost surely. The spike is so narrow that SSVS keeps far more than a t test at 1.96 would. The ratio c one over c zero, not a significance level, sets the threshold.

The claim, before the derivations. In the k-factor model, y t equals beta f t plus epsilon t, the m by m covariance is Omega equal to beta beta transposed plus Sigma, with Sigma diagonal. Common factors explain all the dependence, and m times m plus one over two covariances are described by m times k plus one minus k times k minus one over two parameters. Loadings are defined only up to an orthogonal rotation, so a block-lower-triangular beta is imposed: the same k times k minus one over two count as the SVAR's rotation problem. Given beta and Sigma, the factors have a Gaussian posterior, and the Gibbs sampler cycles through three regressions. A FAVAR adds a few factors, extracted from hundreds of series, as regressors in a small VAR: the second answer, after shrinkage, to the curse of dimensionality.

The notation. y t is the m by one observed vector; the slides also write p for m. f t is the k by one vector of common factors, standard normal, with k less than m. Beta is the m by k loading matrix, block lower triangular with positive beta i i. Sigma, diagonal with entries sigma one squared to sigma m squared, holds the idiosyncratic variances. Omega is the variance of y t. h i squared, the sum over l of beta i l squared over Omega i i, is the communality of series i, between zero and one. V f is the posterior covariance of f t given y t, beta and Sigma. And x t is the r by one large panel of predictors in the FAVAR, with r in the hundreds.

Five point one. Course layer: the basic model. Slide 15, and Tsay equation 9.2, page 469.

"For any specified positive integer k at most m, the standard k-factor model relates each y t to an underlying k-vector of random variables f t, the common factors, via", equation 1: y t given f t is normal with mean beta f t and covariance Sigma; f t is standard normal of dimension k; and Sigma is diagonal.

Equivalently, y t equals beta f t plus epsilon t, with epsilon t normal with covariance Sigma and independent of f t. Then, using the covariance of f t equal to the identity and the covariance of f t and epsilon t equal to zero, equation 2: Omega, the variance of y t, equals beta times the variance of f t times beta transposed, plus the variance of epsilon t, which is beta beta transposed plus Sigma.

Element by element, slide 15, equation 3: the variance of y i t is the sum over l from one to k of beta i l squared, plus sigma i squared; and the covariance of y i t and y j t, for i not equal to j, is the sum over l of beta i l beta j l.

Conditionally on f t, the variance of y i t is sigma i squared and the covariance of y i t and y j t is zero: "Common factors explain all the dependence structure among the m variables." Tsay's general form, equation 9.2, allows a mean alpha and correlated factors, with the covariance of r t equal to beta Sigma f beta transposed plus D. When the factors are observed, such as the market return or macro series, it is a multivariate regression estimated by OLS: Tsay section 9.2, page 470, with xi hat equal to the inverse of G transposed G times G transposed R, and the market model, equation 9.5. That is the same algebra as section 1, point four.

Five point two. Course layer: rotation, identification and the bound on k. Slides 16 to 19.

Invariance. For any orthogonal k by k matrix P, set beta star equal to beta P transposed and f t star equal to P f t. Equation 4: beta star f t star equals beta P transposed P f t, which is beta f t; the variance of f t star is P times the identity times P transposed, which is the identity; and beta star beta star transposed equals beta P transposed P beta transposed, which is beta beta transposed.

The data cannot tell beta and f from beta P transposed and P f: exactly the problem of section 3, equations 6 and 7, now for loadings. "Classical approach: beta transposed Sigma inverse beta equal to the identity, or diagonal. Our approach: beta is a block lower triangular", slide 16. That is, beta i j is zero for j greater than i in the first k rows, and beta i i is positive. That removes k times k minus one over two entries, the dimension of the rotation group, and "provides both identification and, often, useful interpretation": series one loads only on factor one, so factor one is named after series one.

The parameter count, slide 17. Beta has m k entries minus k times k minus one over two zeros; Sigma adds m. Equation 5: m times k plus one, minus k times k minus one over two, against m times m plus one over two in an unrestricted Omega.

A factor model must not have more parameters than Omega has entries. Equation 6: m times m plus one over two, minus m times k plus one, plus k times k minus one over two, is at least zero.

Multiply equation 6 by two and collect powers of k: k squared minus two m plus one times k, plus m squared minus m, is at least zero. The roots are k equal to two m plus one, plus or minus the square root of two m plus one squared minus four times m squared minus m, all over two, which simplifies to two m plus one plus or minus the square root of eight m plus one, over two. And k less than m requires the smaller root. Equation 7: k is at most two m plus one minus the square root of eight m plus one, all over two.

Four cases, slide 17. At m equal to 6: 13 minus 7, over 2, is 3.00, so k max is 3. At m equal to 12: 25 minus 9.849, over 2, is 7.58, so k max is 7. At m equal to 20: 41 minus 12.69, over 2, is 14.15, so k max is 14. At m equal to 50: 101 minus 20.02, over 2, is 40.49, so k max is 40.

At m equal to 6 and k equal to 3: 21 minus 6 times 4 plus 3 is zero, an exact fit with no restriction left on Omega. "Even for small m, the bound will often not matter as relevant k values will not be so large."

Rank deficiency, slide 18, a G-layer item. If beta has rank r less than k, there are matrices Q with beta Q equal to zero and Q transposed Q equal to the identity, and beta beta transposed plus Sigma equals beta plus M Q transposed, times its transpose, plus Sigma minus M M transposed, for orthogonal M. Part three, section 6. Letting the VAR move: GARCH and SV in the errors, random-walk coefficients, and the Kalman filter that makes them estimable.

This section covers slides 38 and 39, the VAR with GARCH errors, Pelloni and Polasek 2003; slide 40, the VAR with stochastic volatility, Uhlig 1997; and slides 41 to 44, the time-varying-parameter VAR with stochastic volatility, Primiceri 2005, and Nakajima, Kasuya and Watanabe 2011. It covers Tsay section 11.1, pages 558 to 563, the local trend model, equations 11.1 to 11.14, Example 11.1 and Theorem 11.1; section 11.1.7, page 571, equation 11.25; and section 11.3.1, pages 577 and 578, the CAPM with time-varying coefficients. And Greenberg section 10.3, pages 161 to 164, time-varying parameters, equations 10.10 and 10.11, and Table 10.3.

The claim, before the derivations. There are three ways to relax a constant VAR, in increasing order of ambition: GARCH in Sigma t, from Pelloni and Polasek; SV in Sigma t, from Uhlig; and random-walk coefficients and random-walk log volatilities in a time-varying recursive structure, Sigma t equal to A t inverse D t A t inverse transposed, from Primiceri. Every time-varying coefficient is a state in a linear state-space model, and the Kalman filter is its exact Gaussian posterior updater: a precision-weighted average of the prediction and the new observation, with gain K t. In the local-level model the gain settles at K star equal to p star over p star plus one, with p star equal to q plus the square root of q squared plus 4 q, all over 2. And one minus K star is the moving-average coefficient of the equivalent ARIMA 0, 1, 1: 0.858 for Tsay's Alcoa volatility, from two different routes.

The notation. Sigma t is the conditional covariance of u t, positive definite. Vech stacks the lower triangle, d equal to q times q plus one over two entries. B t and beta t are the time-varying VAR coefficients, as a matrix and as a stacked vector. A t is the unit-lower-triangular contemporaneous matrix, and alpha t its free entries. D t, diagonal with sigma one t squared to sigma q t squared, holds the structural variances. Q, S and W are the innovation covariances of B t, alpha t and log sigma t. Mu t is the state of the local-level model. Sigma e squared and sigma eta squared are the measurement and state innovation variances. Lowercase q, sigma eta squared over sigma e squared, is the signal-to-noise ratio. Mu t given j and Sigma t given j are the expected value and variance of mu t given the information at j, Tsay's notation. v t, V t and K t are the one-step forecast error, its variance, and the Kalman gain. And delta, between zero and one, is the discount factor.

The local trend, or local level, model, Tsay equations 11.1 and 11.2. Equation 7: y t equals mu t plus e t, with e t normal with variance sigma e squared; and mu t plus one equals mu t plus eta t, with eta t normal with variance sigma eta squared.

The derivation of the filter, Tsay pages 561 to 563. Suppose mu t given the information at t minus one is normal with mean mu t given t minus one and variance Sigma t given t minus one. The one-step forecast of y t is mu t given t minus one, so, equation 8: v t, equal to y t minus mu t given t minus one, equals mu t minus mu t given t minus one, plus e t; and V t, its variance given the past, equals Sigma t given t minus one plus sigma e squared.

Six point four point one. The steady state.

If Sigma t given t minus one tends to P, then equation 12 and K equal to P over P plus sigma e squared give the fixed point: P equals P times one minus P over P plus sigma e squared, plus sigma eta squared, which is P sigma e squared over P plus sigma e squared, plus sigma eta squared.

Multiply by P plus sigma e squared: P squared plus P sigma e squared equals P sigma e squared plus sigma eta squared P plus sigma eta squared sigma e squared. So P squared minus sigma eta squared P minus sigma eta squared sigma e squared equals zero.

Divide by sigma e to the fourth, and write p equal to P over sigma e squared and q equal to sigma eta squared over sigma e squared: p squared minus q p minus q equals zero. The positive root, equation 13: p star equals q plus the square root of q squared plus 4 q, all over 2; and K star equals p star over p star plus one.

Comparative statics. As q goes to zero, a nearly constant state, p star is about the square root of q, which goes to zero, and K star goes to zero: the filter averages the whole past. As q goes to infinity, noise-free data, p star is about q, and K star goes to one: the state is the last observation. The derivative of K star with respect to q is positive throughout.

The ARIMA link, Tsay pages 558 and 559. In steady state, equation 10 with mu t plus one given t equal to mu t given t reads, equation 14: mu t plus one given t equals mu t given t minus one plus K star times y t minus mu t given t minus one, which is one minus K star times mu t given t minus one, plus K star y t. That is exponential smoothing with weight K star, which is the forecast function of one minus B, times y t, equal to one minus theta B, times a t, with theta equal to one minus K star. Tsay: the local trend model "is also an ARIMA(0,1,1) model, which is the simple exponential smoothing model of Chapter 2".

Discounting chooses V t so that the prediction step inflates the filtered variance by a fixed factor. The standard West and Harrison form is a G-layer item. Equation 18: P t given t minus one equals P t minus one given t minus one plus V t, and this is set equal to P t minus one given t minus one over delta, so V t equals one over delta minus one, times P t minus one given t minus one.

There is no Q to estimate: one number, delta, replaces a K by K matrix. Information j periods old is down-weighted by about delta to the j, an effective memory of one over one minus delta observations: delta equal to 0.99 gives 100 periods; delta equal to one gives constant coefficients. Ahmadi and Uhlig, 2009, offer a fully Bayesian FAVAR alternative, slide 61.

Practice for this section, by number and printed page only: Greenberg exercise 10.4, page 167.

Recap of section 6. A VAR can move in its covariance, by GARCH, 78 variance parameters for three series, or by Uhlig's SV, where lambda equal to nu plus p over nu plus p plus one makes the precision a martingale; or in everything, as in Primiceri's TVP-VAR with SV, a drifting recursive SVAR with three random walks. Every moving part is a state, and the Kalman filter updates it with gain K t, the regression coefficient of the state on the surprise. The steady-state gain is p star over p star plus one, and one minus it is the ARIMA 0, 1, 1 coefficient: 0.1418 and 0.858 for Alcoa. Random-walk coefficients have variance growing like t times Omega, and discounting replaces Q with a single delta.

Part three, section 7. Time-varying covariances: DCC models the correlations directly, factor SV gets them from the factor's volatility.

This section covers the part 2 deck: slide 38, factor stochastic volatility, Pitt and Shephard 1999; slide 39, correlated factor volatilities, Aguilar and West 2000; slide 40, time-varying loadings, Lopes and Carvalho 2007; and slides 41 to 43, daily exchange rates with k equal to 3. It covers the two site pages: the United States and Brazil page, with univariate SV of order one by the stochvol package, rolling correlations, a static factor analysis, and the factor SV sampler on six stocks; and the WTI and S and P 500 page, with SV with leverage and t errors, a one-factor SV model, univariate GARCH 1, 1 with t errors as the first DCC step, the DCC fits in the Tse and Tsui and the Engle versions, and the diagnostics. And it covers Tsay's chapter 10 introduction and section 10.1, pages 506 and 507, equation 10.2; section 10.3.1, page 516, equation 10.7; section 10.4.3, pages 531 and 532; and section 10.6, page 543.

DCC stands for dynamic conditional correlation, and EWMA for the exponentially weighted moving average.

The claim, before the derivations. A k-dimensional conditional covariance has k times k plus one over two moving entries, so every usable model imposes structure. DCC splits Sigma t into D t R t D t: univariate GARCH for D t, then one scalar recursion with two parameters for all the correlations. Factor SV keeps y t equal to beta f t plus epsilon t and lets the factor and idiosyncratic log-variances follow first-order autoregressions. Then Sigma t equals beta H t beta transposed plus the idiosyncratic covariance, and, with one factor, rho i j t equals the sign of beta i beta j over the square root of one plus a i t over h t, times one plus a j t over h t. Correlations rise exactly when the common factor is volatile: a prediction, not a free parameter. On the site's W and T Offshore and S and P 500 data, the DCC correlation shock has a half-life of 7 days in Engle's version and 2 days in Tse and Tsui's, and the S and P GARCH persistence is above one.

The notation. a t is the k by one return innovation, with expected value zero given the past. Sigma t, the covariance of a t given the past, is the conditional covariance, positive definite. D t is the diagonal matrix of conditional standard deviations, the square roots of sigma one one t through sigma k k t. R t, rho t in Tsay, is the conditional correlation matrix, with a unit diagonal. Epsilon t, D t inverse a t, are the standardised innovations. Theta one and theta two are the DCC scalars, non-negative with sum below one. Lambda, between zero and one, is the EWMA decay. f t and beta are the factors and loadings, as in section 5. h t, sigma squared p plus one t, is the factor variance with one factor, and lambda t, its log, the factor log-variance. Sigma i t squared and eta i t, its log, are the idiosyncratic variances and log-variances. And a i t, sigma i t squared over beta i squared, is the idiosyncratic-to-loading ratio, non-negative.

Seven point one. Course layer: the problem. Tsay page 506.

"Multivariate volatility modeling is concerned with the time evolution of Sigma t. The curse of dimensionality quickly becomes a major obstacle in applications because there are k times k plus one over two quantities in Sigma t for a k-dimensional return series. To illustrate, there are 15 conditional variances and covariances in Sigma t for a five-dimensional return series." The full VEC, or VAR-GARCH, of section 6 gives each of them its own equation; everything below is a way not to.

Seven point two. Course layer: the exponentially weighted benchmark. Tsay section 10.1, equation 10.2, page 506.

Equation 1: Sigma hat t equals one minus lambda over one minus lambda to the t minus one, times the sum over j from one to t minus one of lambda to the j minus one times a t minus j a t minus j transposed, with lambda between zero and one.

The weights sum to one, since the sum over j of lambda to the j minus one is one minus lambda to the t minus one, over one minus lambda. For large t, lambda to the t minus one is about zero. Split off the j equal to one term and re-index the rest, with j equal to i plus one. Equation 2: Sigma hat t equals one minus lambda times a t minus one a t minus one transposed, plus lambda times the bracket, one minus lambda times the sum over i of lambda to the i minus one times a t minus one minus i times its transpose. The bracket is Sigma hat t minus one. So Sigma hat t equals one minus lambda times a t minus one a t minus one transposed, plus lambda Sigma hat t minus one.

One parameter for all k times k plus one over two entries, positive definite by construction. It is an IGARCH: no mean reversion, and the persistence is exactly one.

Seven point three. Course layer: correlations separately, Sigma t equals D t R t D t. Tsay equation 10.7, page 516.

Equation 3: Sigma t equals D t R t D t; entry i j is the square root of sigma i i t, times rho i j t, times the square root of sigma j j t. The k variances can be modelled one series at a time, with GARCH or SV from Class 8. What remains is R t: k times k minus one over two correlations, symmetric, with a unit diagonal, positive definite.

Seven point four. Course layer: dynamic conditional correlation. Tsay section 10.4.3, pages 531 and 532.

Tse and Tsui, 2002. Equation 4: R t equals one minus theta one minus theta two, times R bar, plus theta one R t minus one, plus theta two Psi t minus one. R bar is a fixed correlation matrix, often the sample one, and Psi t minus one is the sample correlation of the standardised shocks over the last m days.

Engle, 2002. Equation 5: Q t equals one minus theta one minus theta two, times Q bar, plus theta one epsilon t minus one epsilon t minus one transposed, plus theta two Q t minus one; and R t equals J t Q t J t, with J t the diagonal matrix of q i i t to the minus one half. This is in Tsay's order, with theta one on the news term. J t rescales Q t into a correlation matrix, and theta one plus theta two between zero and one keeps it positive definite. "An obvious drawback of the prior two models is that theta one and theta two are scalar so that all the conditional correlations have the same dynamics", Tsay page 531.

Mean reversion. Write s equal to theta one plus theta two. Take the expectation given the information at t of equation 5 at t plus two: the expected value of Q t plus two equals one minus s times Q bar, plus theta one times the expected value of epsilon t plus one epsilon t plus one transposed, plus theta two Q t plus one. That expected outer product is R t plus one, not Q t plus one; with the usual approximation R t plus one about equal to Q t plus one, equation 6: the expected value of Q t plus two minus Q bar is about s times Q t plus one minus Q bar, and so the expected value of Q t plus h minus Q bar is about s to the h minus one times Q t plus one minus Q bar.

The same algebra is exact for equation 4 in expectation if the expected value of Psi t is about R t, and exact for a GARCH 1, 1 variance: the expected value of sigma squared t plus h minus sigma bar squared equals alpha plus beta, to the h minus one, times sigma squared t plus one minus sigma bar squared, with sigma bar squared equal to omega over one minus alpha minus beta. The half-life is log 0.5 over log s.

Seven point five. Course layer: factor stochastic volatility. Part 2, slide 38; Pitt and Shephard 1999.

Equation 7: y t given f t is normal with mean beta f t and covariance Sigma t, and f t is normal with mean zero and covariance H t. Equation 8: Sigma t is diagonal with sigma one t squared to sigma p t squared, and H t is diagonal with sigma p plus one t squared to sigma p plus k t squared. Equation 9: eta i t, the log of sigma i t squared, is normal with mean alpha i plus gamma i eta i, t minus one, and variance xi i squared; and lambda j t, the log of sigma p plus j t squared, is normal with mean mu j plus phi j lambda j, t minus one, and variance tau j squared.

It is the static factor model of section 5 with p plus k univariate SV processes of order one, from Class 8, for the variances. Exactly as there, Omega becomes, equation 10: the variance of y t given H t and Sigma t equals beta H t beta transposed plus Sigma t.

One factor, two series. With h t the scalar H t, by equation 10: the i i entry of the variance is beta i squared h t plus sigma i t squared, and the i j entry is beta i beta j h t. Equation 11: rho i j t equals beta i beta j h t over the square root of beta i squared h t plus sigma i t squared, times beta j squared h t plus sigma j t squared.

Divide numerator and denominator by the absolute value of beta i beta j times h t, and write a i t equal to sigma i t squared over beta i squared. Equation 12: rho i j t equals the sign of beta i beta j over the square root of one plus a i t over h t, times one plus a j t over h t.

Both factors under the root fall as h t rises, so the absolute value of rho i j t is increasing in h t, from zero as h t goes to zero, to one as h t goes to infinity. Correlations go up in turbulent markets because the common factor's variance goes up: the factor SV account of "correlations rise in crises", with no correlation parameter at all.

Seven point seven. Course layer: DCC and factor SV, side by side. Seven dimensions.

First, the object modelled. DCC models R t directly, equations 4 and 5. Factor SV models H t and Sigma t, and R t is implied by equation 11.

Second, what moves a correlation. In DCC, products of yesterday's standardised shocks. In factor SV, the factor's volatility, and the idiosyncratic ones.

Third, whether all pairs share dynamics. In DCC, yes, through the scalar thetas. In factor SV, no: each pair's sensitivity is set by a i t and a j t.

Fourth, positive definiteness. In DCC, by theta one plus theta two below one and a positive definite Q bar. In factor SV, by construction, equation 10.

Fifth, estimation. DCC uses two-step quasi maximum likelihood. Factor SV uses MCMC.

Sixth, dimension. In DCC, Q bar has k times k minus one over two entries. In factor SV, the count is linear in k.

Seventh, interpretation. DCC is statistical; factor SV is economic, with market or country factors.

Recap of part 2. With too many parameters, shrink, using the Minnesota prior whose posterior is ridge toward a random walk, or compress, using a few factors that carry the cross-section. When coefficients and volatilities move, treat each as a state and let the Kalman filter update it; its steady-state gain is one minus the ARIMA moving-average coefficient. When only covariances move, DCC models correlations directly with two scalars, while factor SV derives them from the common factor's volatility, which is why correlations rise in crises.
