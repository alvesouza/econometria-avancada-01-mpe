Support part one. The pitfalls.

This part covers the pitfalls note, part one. Most mistakes in causal inference are one of four. Reading the wrong table: conditioning on a mediator or a collider, or failing to condition on a confounder. Confusing a mechanical identity with a causal one: omitted-variable bias, or a regression on a randomised dummy. Mistaking precision for identification: a tight interval around a biased number. And forgetting the assumption that does all the work: exclusion, common trends, continuity at the cutoff. Each confusion below comes with the smallest counterexample.

Pitfall one. "Control for everything" against control for confounders only.
The error: believing more controls are always better.
The counterexample: in the drug table with post-treatment blood pressure as the stratifier, slide page 7, stratifying removes the part of the effect that works through blood pressure. In MHE Table 3.2.1, the occupation dummies move the return to schooling from 0.087 to 0.066 by bad control, not by removing bias.
The correction: condition on forks; never on chains out of the treatment, and never on colliders.

Pitfall two. A collider blocks; conditioning on it opens.
The error: thinking that conditioning on a common effect is harmless.
The counterexample: two independent causes X and Z of W. Their correlation is zero, but the correlation of X and Z given W is minus 0.5 when W equals X plus Z plus e, with the variance of e equal to one. Conditioning on a descendant of W also opens the path: minus one third with unit noise.
The correction: a collider blocks a path only while neither it nor any of its descendants is conditioned on.

Pitfall three. D-separation is not "no arrow between them".
The error: judging independence by whether two nodes are directly connected.
The counterexample: Z and Y in the second graph of slide page 12 share no arrow, yet are dependent through the fork at T. Conversely, Z and Y in the first graph are linked by a path and are still independent, because the path has a collider.
The correction: list every path, mark every junction, and apply the blocking rule.

Pitfall four. Simpson: the arithmetic never decides.
The error: looking for the right table in the numbers.
The counterexample: the same four rates give the right answer in the stratified table for gender, and in the pooled table for post-treatment blood pressure, slide page 7.
The correction: a pooled rate is a mix; which mix to use is a causal question about the arrows.

Pitfall five. The naive difference equals the ATE.
The error: reading the treated minus untreated gap as the effect.
The counterexample: it equals the ATT plus selection bias, MHE page 11. In the drug data the naive gap is minus 4.57 points and the ATT is plus 4.80, so the selection bias is minus 9.37. The ATE, plus 5.37, is a third number.
The correction: write the decomposition, and say which assumption kills the bias term.

Pitfall six. The QTE equals the quantile of individual effects.
The error: reading F one inverse of p minus F zero inverse of p as a statement about individuals.
The counterexample, illustrative: let Y of zero be zero or one with equal probability, and Y of one equal one minus Y of zero. Both margins are identical, so the QTE is zero at every p, yet every individual effect is plus or minus one.
The correction: the QTE compares two margins; the distribution of individual effects needs the joint law, which is never observed.

Pitfall seven. Ignorability can be tested with enough data.
The error: believing a large sample or a balance table can confirm ignorability.
The counterexample: the slide's own words, "The validity of ignorability cannot be assessed from the observed data, regardless of how large the sample size is", page 16.
The correction: balance checks test observed covariates only; ignorability is about the unobserved ones.

Pitfall eight. The omitted-variable formula means the long regression is causal.
The error: treating the long regression as the truth because the short one is "biased" relative to it.
The counterexample: MHE calls the formula "a mechanical link between coefficient vectors that applies to short and long regressions whether or not the longer regression is causal", page 44.
The correction: the formula links two regressions; causality needs an argument about the long one.

Pitfall nine. Measurement error biases towards zero, always.
The error: applying attenuation to every kind of measurement error.
The counterexample: attenuation holds only under classical error in a regressor. Error in y uncorrelated with x leaves least squares consistent, Wooldridge section 9.4a. Error correlated with x, as in Wooldridge Example 9.6, can bias either way. And with other regressors, the other coefficients are biased in directions "not easily derived", Wooldridge page 291.
The correction: say which variable is mismeasured and how the error relates to the regressors before signing the bias.

Pitfall ten. Add the instrument to the regression.
The error: regressing y on x and z and reading the x coefficient.
The counterexample: that coefficient is beta one plus sigma x y over sigma squared of x, the same confounding, slide page 19 and Greenberg page 173.
The correction: the instrument works only through the first stage.

Pitfall eleven. A significant first stage is a strong first stage.
The error: accepting a first stage because it rejects at 5 per cent.
The counterexample: a t of 2 rejects delta one equal to zero at 5 per cent, but fails the Stock and Yogo rule that the absolute t exceed 3.2, Wooldridge page 478. With an exclusion violation phi, IV is off by phi over delta one, and a weak delta one magnifies it, as the figure in section 5 shows.
The correction: require a first-stage F above 10 on the excluded instruments, and remember that weakness amplifies any violation.

Pitfall twelve. IV is "less biased" than OLS.
The error: assuming IV always improves on OLS.
The counterexample: only if exclusion holds exactly. With the correlation of z and x equal to 0.2, IV beats OLS on bias only if the correlation of z and u is less than one fifth of the correlation of x and u, Wooldridge page 470.
The correction: compare the correlation of z and u over the correlation of z and x with the correlation of x and u.

Pitfall thirteen. DiD needs equal levels.
The error: rejecting DiD because the groups start at different levels.
The counterexample: New Jersey and Pennsylvania differ by minus 2.949 FTE before the policy, minus 2.89 in MHE; the state effect absorbs that.
The correction: DiD needs equal trends without treatment, not equal levels. A differential trend Delta moves the estimate one for one.

Pitfall fourteen. The DiD regression's beta two is the effect.
The error: reading the treatment-group dummy as the policy effect.
The counterexample: beta two is the treated minus control gap before treatment, minus 2.949 here.
The correction: the effect is the interaction, beta three, 2.750, Wooldridge Table 13.3.

Pitfall fifteen. RD identifies the average effect.
The error: reading the jump as the effect for everyone.
The counterexample: it identifies the expected value of y one minus y zero at a equal to a zero, the effect at the cutoff, MHE 6.1.7.
The correction: say "at the cutoff"; and remember that, because no age has both treated and untreated units, it rests on extrapolating the trend into the cutoff.

Pitfall sixteen. A jump in a polynomial fit is a discontinuity.
The error: trusting one specification.
The counterexample: a curved untreated mean fitted with lines produces a spurious jump, slide page 30; high-order polynomials produce erratic ones, Gelman and Imbens 2019, slide page 38. And the internal-cause placebo is "significant" for bandwidths between 0.90 and 1.10.
The correction: check windows, orders, placebo cutoffs and placebo outcomes, and do not pick the bandwidth that looks best.

Recap of the pitfalls. Condition on forks only. Colliders open when conditioned on. Arithmetic does not pick the table. The naive gap is ATT plus selection bias. Ignorability, exclusion, common trends and continuity are all untestable, and a weak first stage, a differential trend or a curved trend each move the answer in a computable way.

Support part three. The implementation, described.

This part covers the Python note, which presents the runnable script Notas, L05, L05 python checks. The script uses numpy, scipy, pandas, statsmodels and linearmodels, and no scikit-learn. The lecturer's R is the reference, never the code. Every routine is followed by assertions that tie it to a printed number. The script prints one line per section and ends with "ALL L05 CHECKS PASSED", which was the output of the run on October 25th, 2026; the first disagreement aborts with an error naming the quantity and the printed value. The code is not read here; each routine is described by its inputs, its steps, and the trap that breaks it.

What is reused rather than rewritten. The figure script, imported as a module, supplies the data path, the exact omitted-variable moments and their Monte Carlo, the IV probability limits and the calibration constants, the DiD cell means, and the RD local-polynomial fit; importing it draws nothing. The companion check script runs the two companion pages' JavaScript against numpy at three control vectors; it is cited, not imported, because it checks the pages while this script checks the notes. The existing potential-outcomes notebook covers selection-bias simulations, the STAR experiment and regression on a randomised dummy; it is cited for section 2 and not re-implemented. What is new, and why: no existing project code computed d-separation, inverse weighting, IV or RD, since the notebook stops at randomised experiments, so these routines are written once here and asserted.

The convention. A helper called check passes when a computed value rounds to the printed value at the printed number of decimals, that is, within half a unit in the last place.

Routine one, causal graphs and d-separation. Store each graph as a map from every node to its set of parents. To decide whether X and Y are d-separated given a set, use Lauritzen's criterion: keep only the ancestors of X, Y and the conditioning set; marry every pair of parents that share a child; drop the arrow directions; delete the conditioning nodes; and ask whether X can still reach Y. This is equivalent to the path-blocking definition of slide page 11 and needs no path enumeration. Then confirm the slides' examples: in the first graph, independence with no conditioning, dependence given W or given U, and independence given W and X. In the second graph, check every set on the slide's two lists, and then search all sixteen subsets of T, W, U and X to confirm that the five separating sets are the only ones. On Harrison's graph, enumerate the simple paths between X and Y, which must number five, and classify all junctions, which must be seven chains, three forks and five colliders. For the back-door check, delete the arrow out of X and test which adjustment sets separate X from Y: Z one with Z three, and Z two with Z three, pass; nothing, Z three alone, Z one alone and Z two alone fail. Finally, build the exact population covariance of the slide's structural model, with noise propagating as the equations say and each noise variance equal to the variance of its node's systematic part, and compute population regression coefficients. Adjusting for Z one and Z three, or Z two and Z three, gives exactly 6; Z three alone gives 6.0051; no adjustment gives 5.4381; Z one alone gives 5.5666 and Z two alone 5.4959. The trap: because these are population numbers with no Monte Carlo error, the 6.0051 is a real bias, not noise; and simulating the slide's R code as printed would not follow the equations.

Routine two, potential outcomes, adjustment and inverse weighting. Rebuild the 700 patients from the four cells of slide page 6, with drug, gender and recovery, and check that there are 343 women and 350 takers. Compute the naive gap, minus 0.0457; the adjustment formula, plus 0.0537; both forms of the inverse-weighting identity, with the propensity estimated as the treated share in each gender, which must equal the adjustment formula to machine precision; the propensities, 0.2437 for men and 0.7668 for women; and the ATT by standardising to the treated's gender mix, plus 0.0480. The trap: the exact agreement of inverse weighting and adjustment holds only for a saturated, cell-frequency propensity.

Routine three, omitted-variable bias. Take a trivariate Gaussian with chosen correlations, solve for the conditional-mean coefficients and the conditional variance by the Schur complement, and check them against equations 3 and 4. Support part five. Self-test: twenty-five true or false items.

This part covers the original self-test of the pitfalls note. Scoring follows the course rule: per block, the score is the maximum of zero and the number right minus half the number wrong. Difficulty is marked easy, medium or hard. Numbers marked illustrative are invented for the item; all others come from the notes. After each statement, pause the player, decide true or false and say why, then resume for the answer.

Block A, Simpson and causal graphs.

Item 1, easy. If a treatment raises the recovery rate within every stratum of a variable, it must raise the pooled recovery rate. Pause.
Answer: false. The pooled rate is a weighted average with group-specific weights. The drug wins within gender, 93 against 87 and 73 against 69, and loses pooled, 78 against 83, because 75 per cent of drug takers but 23 per cent of non-takers are women. The composition term, minus 10.5 points, beats the within effect, plus 5.9.

Item 2, medium. In a chain, X into M into Y, conditioning on M makes X and Y independent, so a regression of Y on X and M estimates the total effect of X on Y. Pause.
Answer: false. The first half is Rule 1 and is true; the conclusion is the reciprocal trap. Conditioning on the mediator blocks the causal path, so the X coefficient is the direct effect only, which is zero in a pure chain. That is why the blood-pressure table must be read pooled.

Item 3, medium. Two variables that are marginally independent can become dependent after conditioning on a third variable. Pause.
Answer: true. Rule 3: for a collider, X into W out of Z, with X independent of Z, conditioning on W or a descendant creates dependence. With unit coefficients and noise, the correlation of X and Z given W is minus 0.5.

Item 4, medium. In the second graph of slide page 12, with T into Z, T into Y, Z into W, X into W, X into Y, and W into U, conditioning on T and U makes Z and Y independent. Pause.
Answer: false. T blocks the fork path, but U is a descendant of the collider W and opens the path from Z through W and X to Y. X is not conditioned on, so the path is open. The slide lists U and T among the d-connecting sets.

Item 5, hard. In Harrison's graph, adjusting for Z three alone is a valid back-door adjustment for the effect of X on Y, because Z three lies on every back-door path. Pause.
Answer: false. Z three lies on every back-door path, but on the path X, Z one, Z three, Z two, Y it is a collider: conditioning on it opens that path. Z one with Z three, or Z two with Z three, are valid. In the slide's structural model, Z three alone gives 6.0051 against the true 6.

Block B, potential outcomes and identification.

Item 6, easy. Under random assignment the average treatment effect on the treated equals the average treatment effect. Pause.
Answer: true. Independence of A from the potential outcomes gives the expected value of Y of one minus Y of zero given A equal to one equal to the unconditional expected value, MHE page 12.

Item 7, medium. If hospitalised people report worse health than the non-hospitalised, 2.79 against 2.07 on a scale from 1 to 5 where 5 is poor, the selection-bias term in MHE's decomposition is negative in health terms. Pause.
Answer: true. People who go to hospital have worse untreated health: they would be sicker without hospital too. So their untreated mean is worse than that of the non-hospitalised. MHE calls the selection bias "negative in this example", page 12, large enough to mask a positive effect.

Item 8, medium. Ignorability, the potential outcomes independent of A given L, implies that the observed Y is independent of A given L. Pause.
Answer: false. The observed Y equals Y of A, so it depends on A through the treatment effect; ignorability concerns the potential outcomes. If A has any effect, Y and A are dependent given L, which is how the effect is estimated.

Item 9, medium. Positivity can fail even when ignorability holds. Pause.
Answer: true. They are separate conditions. A sharp RD is the extreme case: D sub a is a function of a, so ignorability given a holds trivially, while the probability of treatment given a is zero or one, which violates positivity everywhere.

Item 10, hard. With a propensity score estimated as the treated share in each covariate cell, the inverse-weighting estimate of the ATE equals the adjustment-formula estimate. Pause.
Answer: true. Weighting by one over e hat re-creates the population cell weights in each arm; on the drug table both give 0.05367. With a parametric propensity model the two differ in finite samples.

Item 11, medium. The QTE at the median, F one inverse of 0.5 minus F zero inverse of 0.5, is the median of the individual effects. Pause.
Answer: false. It is a difference of marginal quantiles; the median of individual effects depends on the unobserved joint distribution. The swap counterexample of pitfall six has a QTE of zero and individual effects of plus or minus one.

Block C, omitted variables, bad control and measurement error.

Item 12, easy. If the omitted variable is uncorrelated with the included regressor, the short and long coefficients on the included regressor coincide. Pause.
Answer: true. Delta is zero in "short equals long plus gamma delta", whatever gamma is.

Item 13, medium. In the site's simulation, with beta equal to 0.75 and gamma equal to 1.2, the long regression has a smaller sampling variance than the short one at every correlation of x and z. Pause.
Answer: false. At rho equal to zero the long standard deviation is 0.1925 against 0.2952 for the short, but at rho equal to 0.95 it is 0.6163 against 0.2018: collinearity inflates the long variance. The long regression still wins on mean squared error, 0.380 against 1.340, because the short bias, 1.2 rho, is larger.

Item 14, medium. Controlling for a test score taken after schooling, as a proxy for ability, gives a schooling coefficient below the causal effect when schooling raises the score. Pause.
Answer: true. By MHE equation 3.2.15 the coefficient is rho minus gamma pi one over pi two, which is below rho when gamma, pi one and pi two are positive; for example 0.09 instead of 0.10, illustrative.

Item 15, easy. Classical measurement error in the dependent variable biases the OLS slope towards zero. Pause.
Answer: false. Error in y uncorrelated with the regressors goes into the error term: OLS stays unbiased, only less precise, Wooldridge section 9.4a. Attenuation is a property of error in a regressor.

Item 16, medium. If the noise variance in a regressor equals the variance of its true value, OLS converges to half the true slope. Pause.
Answer: true. Lambda equals sigma squared of x over sigma squared of x plus sigma squared of u, which is one half.

Item 17, hard. Adding a correctly measured control that is correlated with a mismeasured regressor reduces the attenuation of the latter's coefficient. Pause.
Answer: false. It does the opposite. The control removes signal from the regressor but none of the noise, so lambda sub r, sigma squared of r over sigma squared of r plus sigma squared of u, is at most lambda, Wooldridge 9.36: 0.390 against 0.64 in the note's example.

Block D, instrumental variables.

Item 18, easy. With one instrument and one endogenous regressor, the IV estimate equals the reduced-form coefficient divided by the first-stage coefficient. Pause.
Answer: true. Theta one equals beta one delta one; on Card, 0.04207 over 0.31990 is 0.1315, identical to two-stage least squares.

Item 19, medium. If the covariance of z and x is not zero and the covariance of z and u is zero, the IV estimator is unbiased in finite samples. Pause.
Answer: false. It is consistent, but "essentially never unbiased" when IV is needed, Wooldridge page 465; small samples can carry substantial bias.

Item 20, medium. In Card's data the IV standard error of the return to schooling is about 16 times the OLS one, so the IV estimate is useless. Pause.
Answer: false. The ratio is 0.0549637 over 0.0034983, which is 15.7: the price of consistency, since Wooldridge equation 15.12 divides by the R squared of x on z. The interval, 0.024 to 0.239, still excludes zero; it is wide, not useless.

Item 21, hard. A first-stage t statistic of 2.5 on the excluded instrument is enough, by the Stock and Yogo rule of thumb, to proceed with standard IV inference. Pause.
Answer: false. The rule asks for an absolute t above the square root of 10, about 3.2, that is, an F above 10, Wooldridge page 478. A t of 2.5 rejects at 5 per cent but is weak. Card's 3.64 passes, barely.

Item 22, hard. With an instrument whose direct effect on the outcome is phi equal to 0.005, illustrative, the IV asymptotic bias falls as the first stage gets weaker. Pause.
Answer: false. The bias is phi over delta one, which grows as delta one goes to zero. At the Card calibration, IV is worse than OLS for delta one below about 0.09.

Block E, difference in differences and regression discontinuity.

Item 23, easy. In the regression of y on beta zero plus beta one P plus beta two T plus beta three P T, the coefficient beta two measures the effect of the policy. Pause.
Answer: false. Beta two is the treated minus control difference before the policy, minus 2.949 for New Jersey against Pennsylvania; the DiD effect is beta three, 2.750.

Item 24, medium. If New Jersey's employment would have grown 1 FTE per store faster than Pennsylvania's without the minimum-wage increase, the DiD estimate of 2.750 overstates the policy effect by 1 FTE. Pause.
Answer: true. A differential trend Delta enters additively: the DiD estimates delta plus Delta, so the effect is 2.750 minus 1, which is 1.750.

Item 25, medium. A sharp RD design estimates the average effect of legal drinking on mortality for all young adults aged 19 to 23. Pause.
Answer: false. It estimates the expected value of y one minus y zero at a equal to 21, the effect at the cutoff, MHE 6.1.7. Extending it to other ages needs the constant-effect assumption of MHE 6.1.2, which the design does not test.


Support part six. Glossary.

Simpson's paradox: an association that holds in a whole population and reverses in every subpopulation, because the pooled rate mixes strata with group-specific weights.
Causal graph, or DAG: a directed acyclic graph in which each node is a variable and each arrow a direct cause, with no loops.
Junction: three consecutive nodes on a path; it is a chain, a fork or a collider.
Chain: A into B into C; the ends are dependent, and independent given the middle node.
Fork: B a common cause of A and C; the ends are dependent, and independent given the middle node.
Collider: A and C both cause B; the ends are independent, and become dependent given B or any descendant of B.
D-separation: the condition that every path between two nodes is blocked by the conditioning set, which implies independence given that set.
Back-door path: a path from the treatment to the outcome that starts with an arrow into the treatment.
Potential outcome: Y of a, the outcome a unit would have under treatment a; only one per unit is ever observed.
Average treatment effect, ATE: the expected value of Y of one minus the expected value of Y of zero over the whole population.
Average treatment effect on the treated, ATT: the same contrast within those actually treated.
Conditional average treatment effect, CATE: the average effect within a subpopulation defined by covariates V.
Quantile treatment effect, QTE: the difference of the p-quantiles of the two potential-outcome distributions, not the quantile of individual effects.
Causal relative risk: the ratio of the expected values of Y of one and Y of zero for a binary outcome.
Selection bias: the difference in untreated outcomes between the treated and the untreated, the term that separates the naive gap from the ATT.
SUTVA: the stable unit treatment value assumption; no interference between units and one version of each treatment.
Consistency: the observed outcome equals the potential outcome of the treatment actually received.
Ignorability: the potential outcomes are independent of treatment given the observed covariates; also called unconfoundedness or the conditional independence assumption.
Positivity: every covariate cell has a positive probability of each treatment.
Adjustment formula: the expected value of Y of a as the covariate-weighted average of the conditional means of Y given treatment a.
Propensity score: the probability of treatment given the covariates, a one-dimensional balancing score.
Inverse probability weighting: estimating the ATE by weighting each treated unit by one over the propensity and each untreated unit by one over one minus the propensity.
Omitted-variable bias: the change in a coefficient when a regressor is left out, equal to its effect times its slope on the included regressor.
Bad control: a variable that is itself an outcome of the treatment; conditioning on it reintroduces selection bias.
Proxy control: a stand-in for an unobserved confounder; if the treatment affects it, the treatment coefficient is biased down.
Classical measurement error: noise independent of the true value and of the model error.
Attenuation bias: the shrinking of a slope towards zero by the reliability ratio when a regressor carries classical noise.
Reliability ratio: lambda, the share of a regressor's observed variance that is signal.
Instrument: a variable that moves the treatment, relevance, and affects the outcome only through it, exclusion.
First stage: the regression of the treatment on the instrument and the controls.
Reduced form: the regression of the outcome on the instrument and the controls.
Wald estimator: with a binary instrument, the difference in mean outcomes divided by the difference in mean treatments between the instrument groups.
Two-stage least squares: regress the treatment on instruments and controls, then regress the outcome on the fitted treatment and controls.
Indirect least squares: the reduced-form coefficient divided by the first-stage coefficient.
Weak instrument: an instrument with a first-stage F below about 10 on the excluded instruments, which makes IV imprecise and magnifies any exclusion violation.
Wu-Hausman test: a test of OLS against IV that adds the first-stage residual to the OLS equation and tests its coefficient.
Local average treatment effect, LATE: the average effect on compliers, those whose treatment the instrument changes; G layer.
Difference in differences: the change of the treated minus the change of the control.
Common trends, or parallel trends: the assumption that without treatment both groups' mean outcomes would have moved by the same amount.
Saturated regression: a regression with as many parameters as cells, which reproduces the cell means exactly.
Regression discontinuity: a design in which treatment switches at a cutoff of a running variable, and the jump in the outcome there estimates the effect.
Sharp design: treatment is a deterministic function of the running variable.
Fuzzy design: crossing the cutoff changes only the probability of treatment; estimated as IV; G layer.
Running variable: the variable whose cutoff assigns treatment, here age.
Bandwidth: the half-width of the window around the cutoff used for local estimation.
Local linear regression: separate straight-line fits on each side of the cutoff within the bandwidth.
Placebo outcome: an outcome the treatment should not affect, such as internal-cause deaths.
Placebo cutoff: a fake cutoff where no rule changes, used to check that the estimator finds nothing there.


Support part seven. The whole unit in five lines.

One. A causal effect is a contrast of potential outcomes, and the naive comparison is the effect on the treated plus selection bias: on the drug table, minus 4.57 points naive, plus 5.37 adjusted.

Two. The graph decides what to condition on: block forks, never condition on a mediator or a collider, and let d-separation turn that into a mechanical check.

Three. Regression inherits every mistake: an omitted confounder adds gamma times delta, a bad control adds selection bias, and a noisy regressor shrinks the slope by sigma squared of x over sigma squared of x plus sigma squared of u.

Four. An instrument identifies the effect as the reduced form over the first stage, Card's 0.1315 against least squares' 0.0747, at the price of a standard error 15.7 times larger and fragility when the first stage is weak.

Five. Difference in differences assumes parallel trends, giving plus 2.75 FTE for Card and Krueger, and regression discontinuity assumes a smooth counterfactual, giving plus 4.53 deaths per 100,000 at 21: each design trades one untestable assumption for another.
